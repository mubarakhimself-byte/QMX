## Turn 1 (topic question, model: Smart) - answer

**There is no universally best stop rule:** exits help growth or Sharpe only when they exploit real information about how a strategy’s edge decays or reverses; otherwise, they mostly reshape risk while adding trading costs. For a multi-strategy system, treat each exit rule as part of the strategy—not as a universal safety setting—and size positions from the resulting net return distribution.

### How to choose the exit

**Fixed versus ATR-scaled stops.** A fixed price or percentage stop is simple and auditable, but its risk varies with volatility: it can be needlessly tight in a volatile session and dangerously wide in a quiet one. An ATR-scaled stop makes the distance respond to recent range, which is useful when volatility regimes vary—but it does not make the stop optimal, and a backward-looking ATR can lag a sudden jump. Compare fixed and ATR stops out of sample, by instrument and volatility regime, after costs. Use a causal volatility estimate and cap the distance; do not let a large ATR silently turn a risk stop into a large loss.

**Trailing stops.** They can protect accumulated gains and suit a strategy whose edge is “stay in while the move persists.” They can also give back substantial open profit, exit on ordinary pullbacks, and repeatedly re-enter at a cost. For trend-following strategies, test trailing distance against signal decay or a volatility-scaled reversal—not just the highest backtest Sharpe. For mean-reversion trades, a trailing stop may cut off the intended return to the mean.

**Breakeven moves.** Moving a stop to entry price is not free risk reduction: after fees, spread and slippage, “breakeven” may be a loss. It often creates a cluster of scratch trades when price revisits entry before moving in the intended direction. Use it only if reaching the trigger level genuinely changes the forecast distribution—not because the trade has become emotionally uncomfortable.

**Time exits.** A time stop is appropriate when the expected edge decays with holding time, when the trade must not cross a session boundary, or when capital tied up in stale positions has an opportunity cost. For a mean-reverting trade, a useful starting point is to set a maximum hold relative to the estimated reversion timescale and test whether trades that have not reverted by then still have positive net expectancy. For a trend trade, exiting merely because a fixed number of bars elapsed can discard the largest winners.

**Targets and partial exits.** A target can make sense when the conditional expected return beyond that price is low, or when reversal risk rises. Partial exits reduce exposure and can smooth outcomes, but they also cap winners and may add execution cost. A higher win rate after introducing a target does not by itself mean better performance: compare net expectancy, Sharpe, drawdown and compounded growth. For a trend strategy, avoid a tight target unless the data show that taking profits beats letting winners run.

**Mean-reverting versus trending prices.** In optimal-stopping models, the best exit depends on the assumed price dynamics, transaction costs and reward—not on a rule that is optimal everywhere. For a mean-reverting spread, model an exit around the estimated equilibrium, with a stop beyond the range where the mean-reversion thesis remains credible and a time limit for failed convergence. For a trending strategy, preserve the right tail: use a signal-reversal exit or a suitably loose trailing rule, and test any fixed profit cap carefully. These are model-dependent recommendations, not universal thresholds.

### Stops, sizing and execution risk

A stop changes the whole return distribution. It may cut the left tail, but it can also create more small losses, reduce the right tail, change skew, and increase turnover. That matters for sizing: two strategies with the same average return and volatility can warrant different positions if one has rare gap losses or relies on a small number of large winners.

A practical starting point is to size from the **expected loss under execution**, not the stop distance alone:

$$
\text{position size}
\approx
\frac{\text{risk budget}}
{\text{stop distance} \times \text{point value}
+ \text{estimated slippage and fees per unit}}
$$

This is a planning approximation, not a maximum-loss guarantee. A stop-market order can fill beyond its trigger during a gap or fast market; a stop-limit order may not fill at all. Backtests that assume execution exactly at the stop price will therefore understate tail risk. Stress-test gaps, spread widening, latency and slippage, especially around opens, news, illiquid hours and venue outages. Aggregate these risks across strategies: many “small” agents can become one large correlated position in a shared market shock.

Stops can improve **growth and Sharpe** when they remove states with genuinely negative conditional expectancy, prevent an edge from being overwhelmed by unbounded or regime-break losses, or preserve capital for better opportunities—and when the improvement survives costs and out-of-sample testing. They may mainly protect against **ruin or unacceptable drawdowns** when they cap rare losses but reduce average return, clip trend winners or add churn. That can still be worthwhile under a hard risk constraint, but it should not be presented as alpha.

One 2026 crypto replay illustrates both the potential and the danger of tuning exits: its best reported configuration raised Sharpe from 0.419 to 0.653, but the authors flag the randomized split, finite sample and lack of a clean forward test. [Reported Sharpe](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150?page=2)

### Concrete recommendations for the system

Keep agents responsible for proposing and explaining exits, but require human promotion to pass the same validation gate as the entry logic. For each strategy, compare **no discretionary stop, fixed stop, ATR stop, trailing stop, time exit, target/partial exit, and combinations** on the same trades and execution assumptions. Use chronological walk-forward tests, account for the number of configurations tried, and report net results by instrument, session, volatility regime and long/short side. Record not only Sharpe and growth, but also tail loss, drawdown, average holding time, turnover, missed winners and gap/slippage losses.

Promote an exit change only when its benefit persists out of sample and under harsher execution assumptions. If it improves drawdown but not net growth or Sharpe, classify it as **risk control**, size accordingly, and decide explicitly whether that insurance is worth its cost.

## Turn 2 (follow-up a: top three methods and exit-to-sizing link, model: Smart) - answer

**The three most useful are [Leung and Li’s mean-reversion stopping model](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1411.5062), [Leung and Zhang’s trailing-stop model](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1701.03960), and the 2026 crypto exit-rule replay, [Optimal Stop-Loss and Take-Profit Parameterization for Autonomous Trading Agent Swarm](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150).** The first two explain how an exit should depend on the assumed price process; the third shows how to compare concrete exit configurations, while also illustrating how easy it is to overstate an in-sample result.

### 1. Mean-reverting positions: Leung and Li, 2015

**What it does, step by step.** The model treats a spread as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process: it tends to revert toward a long-run level, with noise around that pull. It first solves for the best voluntary liquidation level given transaction costs. Then it adds a lower stop-loss level, which forces liquidation if hit before the profit-taking level. Finally, it solves for when it is worth entering, accounting for the exit policy and entry costs. In the stop-loss version, the position is held between the lower stop and an upper take-profit boundary. [Model Inputs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1411.5062?page=6)[Stop and Target](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1411.5062?page=14)

**Inputs and outputs.** Estimate the spread’s long-run mean $\theta$, mean-reversion speed $\mu$, and volatility $\sigma$; specify the stop level $L$, transaction costs, and discount or holding-time value. The model returns a take-profit boundary $b_L^*$ and, if entry is also optimized, an entry interval. A notable implication is that raising the stop level lowers the optimal take-profit boundary: the model says a tighter stop makes waiting for a distant target less attractive. [Stop Target Tradeoff](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1411.5062?page=15)

**What it reports—and does not.** This is a derivation and an illustrative pairs-trading example, not a broad empirical comparison of live exit rules. Its example estimates OU parameters from historical ETF spreads, but it does **not** establish that its rule produces a particular out-of-sample return, Sharpe ratio, or drawdown improvement. Do not read the paper’s “optimal” boundary as a measured performance advantage.

**Where it fails.** The recommendation is only as good as the OU approximation and its estimated parameters. A spread can stop reverting, its equilibrium can move, and a stop can fill past its level. The model’s stop boundary constrains the price process, not the realized loss after spread, slippage, a gap or a market halt. Small samples of spread excursions can also make the estimated reversion speed—and thus the target—unstable.

**Follow-on work.** [Optimal Entry and Exit with Signature in Statistical Arbitrage](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2309.16008) is a later optimal-timing approach for mean-reverting spreads using path signatures. [Stop-loss and Leverage in optimal Statistical Arbitrage with an application to Energy market](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1706.07021) takes related mean-reversion and stop-loss ideas into an energy-market setting with leverage. These are useful extensions, but neither should be treated as a universal validation of an OU threshold.

### 2. Trending positions: Leung and Zhang, 2017

**What it does, step by step.** It models a trailing stop as a floor that moves with the running price high. Given that floor rule, the investor chooses when to sell voluntarily; the paper solves the stopping problem and shows when it is optimal to set an additional upper limit-sell target. It also analyzes entry timing. In the paper’s notation, the stop floor is $f(\bar X)$, where $\bar X$ is the running maximum; a percentage trail is one possible choice. [Trailing Floor](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1701.03960?page=4)[Optimal Exit](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1701.03960?page=10)

**Inputs and outputs.** Specify a price-dynamics model—drift and volatility, or more general diffusion functions—plus the reward on exit, discounting, and the trailing-floor function. The method returns an optimal voluntary liquidation level conditional on that stop rule; it can also return an entry region. It **does not** hand you a universally optimal trailing percentage: the drawdown floor is an input to the calculation, and the answer depends on the assumed dynamics.

**What it reports—and does not.** Its results are analytical and illustrated numerically under an exponential OU model; it does **not** report a live or cross-asset backtest with Sharpe, returns, or realized drawdowns. Its key practical warning is that a trailing stop is not inherently beneficial: the paper discusses a geometric-Brownian-motion result in which it is optimal not to use a trailing stop when drift is positive. [Positive Drift](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1701.03960?page=2)

**Where it fails.** The assumed diffusion has relatively tame, continuous paths. Real prices have jumps, gaps, liquidity shortages and changing drift; a trailing trigger does not guarantee a fill at its floor. A model fitted to a trending regime can also recommend holding through a reversal, while a model fitted to noisy mean reversion can trail too tightly and stop out winners. In production, compare the rule against signal-based exits using executable prices and stress cases, not just simulated threshold crossings.

**Follow-on work and contrast.** [Trends and Reversion in Financial Markets on Time Scales from Minutes to Decades](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2501.16772) supplies an empirical challenge to any one-regime drift assumption: it reports short-scale reversal and a shift toward weak-trend persistence at longer intraday horizons, with differences by asset and session at the shortest scales. That work measures return patterns, **not** the performance of trailing-stop orders. The 2017 paper itself situates its analysis after [Trading Securities Using Trailing Stops](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.41.6.1096), a classic 1995 theoretical study; its abstract-level takeaway, as discussed by Leung and Zhang, is another warning that positive drift can make a trailing stop suboptimal.

### 3. Practical calibration: the 2026 crypto swarm replay

**What it does, step by step.** It replays more than 900 historical crypto trades under a grid of exit settings: fixed stop-loss, trailing activation and distance, partial-profit threshold and fraction, and stale-trade close. It ranks candidates primarily by Sharpe, with profit factor, drawdown and unrealized-to-realized return capture as diagnostics. It then refines top candidates with ATR stop and target overlays plus a consecutive-loss circuit breaker. [Replay Design](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150?page=1)[Search Design](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150?page=2)

**Inputs and outputs.** It needs historical trade price paths and a declared grid of settings; it does **not** estimate a structural drift or OU reversion speed. It outputs a ranked configuration. Its best first-pass setting used a 10% stop, 3% trailing activation, 3% trailing distance, 5% partial-take-profit threshold, 75% partial exit and 48-hour stale close. The refined best result added a 1× ATR stop, 2× ATR take-profit, and a circuit-breaker reduction after two losses. These are study-specific candidates, not recommended universal values.

**Performance and the survival trade.** Reported Sharpe rose from **0.419** at baseline to **0.525** for the first-pass winner and **0.653** for the ATR/circuit-breaker refinement; profit factor rose from **1.639** to **1.760** and **2.375**. First-pass maximum drawdown fell from **$829.8** to **$757.2**. The refined-stage simulator did not reconstruct portfolio equity, so its maximum drawdown is unavailable; the paper also does not give total net return figures in the reported comparison. [Reported Metrics](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150?page=2)[Drawdown Limit](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150?page=2)

### Feeding exits into position sizing

**Do not size from nominal stop distance alone.** A stop distance in $R$—the planned loss per unit if filled at the trigger—is a good *first-pass exposure limit*:

$$
q_{\text{initial}} =
\frac{B_{\text{trade}}}
{\text{planned stop loss per unit, including fees and typical slippage}}
$$

But it is not a maximum-loss guarantee. A stop-market order can gap through its trigger; a stop-limit order may not fill. For each candidate rule, replay or simulate the **realized net loss distribution** using executable prices, fees, spread and stressed slippage. Measure tail loss, expected shortfall, and losses conditional on gaps or volatility shocks, not just the average stop-out.

A practical policy is to take the smaller size implied by the nominal stop-risk budget and the tail-loss budget. For example, require that a chosen high loss quantile or expected shortfall—computed from the strategy’s realized net loss distribution—fit within its allocated risk. Where there are too few trades to estimate that tail reliably, use conservative stress scenarios and smaller size rather than treating an estimated quantile as precise. Recompute sizing whenever the exit changes: stops can increase the frequency of small losses while clipping the few large winners, so the resulting return distribution is different from the entry strategy’s.

For a swarm, apply the same logic at portfolio level. Concurrent strategies may all stop in the same gap or volatility shock, so summing their individual stop budgets can understate shared tail exposure. **Use stop-distance sizing to bound ordinary execution risk; use gap-aware realized-loss scenarios to govern survival sizing.**

## Turn 3 (follow-up b: asset classes, performance, trading ideas and combinations, model: Smart) - answer

The largest practical difference is **not simply asset volatility**: it is whether the instrument trades continuously, how its venue triggers and executes stops, and whether trading can be interrupted or repriced before the next liquid session. A stop level is a trigger, not a guaranteed fill.

## 1. How exits should differ by market

| Market | Exit design implications |
|---|---|
| **FX spot** | Spot FX is decentralized and typically trades 24/5, so there is no single consolidated price or universal stop trigger. Overnight rollover, thin liquidity around the daily handoff, weekends, and scheduled macro releases can widen spreads or create jumps. Model stops against the **executable side of the quote**—bid to exit a long, ask to exit a short—and verify the broker’s trigger source, execution policy and weekend handling. Round-number levels can attract clustered orders and short-lived price pressure; test whether your strategy is being stopped in the spread or adverse flow, but do not assume that every stop-out reflects deliberate “stop hunting.” |
| **Stock-index futures and index products** | Futures can trade beyond cash-market hours, but liquidity and spreads vary by session; the cash open can incorporate overnight news in a jump. Futures may also face exchange price limits or trading pauses. A stop on an ETF or cash-market instrument cannot execute while its venue is closed. For futures, model overnight fills and exchange limits, and define whether positions must be closed before cash close or can carry overnight. Keep contract multiplier, expiry and roll basis in the risk calculation. |
| **Metals** | Gold is often more liquid across global sessions than many smaller metal contracts; liquidity can still thin at session transitions and around macro or geopolitical news. Silver and less-liquid contracts can have materially different spread and jump behavior. For futures, separately handle expiry, roll, and delivery-related constraints rather than letting an old-contract stop govern a new-contract position. Calibrate stop buffers and slippage by contract and session, not with one “metals” setting. |
| **Energy** | Crude, products and natural gas have distinct liquidity and jump profiles. Inventory releases, OPEC news, weather and geopolitical events can change prices quickly; contract expiry and delivery mechanics matter. Exchange limits or other trading restrictions can prevent orderly execution. A stop-market order may fill far past its trigger, while a stop-limit order may remain unfilled. Use contract-specific gap and limit stress tests, and explicitly define whether event-window exposure is reduced, hedged, or held. |
| **Crypto spot and perpetuals** | Crypto trades through weekends, but not with uniform liquidity across venues. Perpetuals add funding, margin and liquidation-engine risk. Know whether your stop triggers on last trade, mark price or index price; understand whether liquidation can occur before the stop reaches the venue, whether margin is isolated or cross-collateralized, and what happens during venue outages or de-risking. A stop order is not a substitute for adequate liquidation buffer or collateral controls. Stress weekend gaps and venue-specific spread/slippage, even though the market does not have a conventional weekend close. |
| **Listed futures, across asset classes** | “Futures” is an execution wrapper as well as an asset exposure: contract roll, expiry, tick size, multiplier, trading hours, broker-held versus exchange-native stops and price limits all affect realized risk. Keep exit logic aware of the active contract and roll schedule; don’t evaluate a continuous back-adjusted series as though it were directly tradable at the stop. Treat limit-lock conditions as possible periods with no executable exit. |
| **Single stocks** | Overnight and weekend gaps, earnings, corporate actions, halts and short-sale constraints can dominate a tight intraday stop. A stop cannot execute through a halt and may fill at the next available price after reopening. Adjust for splits and other corporate actions, include borrow availability and recall risk for shorts, and avoid assuming that an earnings-window stop caps the loss. A time-of-day and event calendar often matters as much as ATR. |

For every market, log the **trigger price, order type, venue, quote or mark source, decision time, submission time, fill price and contemporaneous spread**. A backtest that exits at the stop price but ignores these details will usually understate loss tails. In FX, crypto and fragmented equities in particular, use venue- or broker-specific fill records rather than a generic “stop filled” assumption.

## 2. When do exits improve performance, and what signals could become strategies?

The key distinction is between an exit that exploits **conditional return information** and one that merely cuts exposure. The former may improve net return or Sharpe; the latter can still be valuable for survival, but its benefit should be measured as reduced tail loss or drawdown against the return and turnover it costs.

**What can become a strategy or signal**

- **Mean-reversion exit timing:** For a spread modelled as OU, estimate its equilibrium $\theta$, speed $\mu$, and volatility $\sigma$, then set a profit exit toward the estimated mean and a stop where the reversion thesis is no longer credible. The model’s characteristic half-life is $\ln(2)/\mu$ in the same time units used to estimate $\mu$. A useful candidate time-exit rule is: if the trade has not reverted within a horizon informed by that half-life, test whether its remaining conditional expected return, net of costs, has turned non-positive. The half-life is a model input, **not by itself a proven optimal timeout**; re-estimate it causally and monitor uncertainty. The Leung–Li model gives threshold structure, not empirical P&L validation. [OU Dynamics](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1411.5062?page=3)
- **Intraday trend/reversion state:** Safari and Schmidhuber’s study offers a possible state variable: trend strength at several horizons. A candidate system could use that state to choose between a mean-reversion exit and a trend-following exit, or to adjust a trailing distance. The study uses 14 years of futures data from 2010–2023, with end-of-minute data for equity indices, rates, currencies and commodities; its regressions explain only a very small share of return variation. So this is a **conditional exit hypothesis to test**, not a stand-alone signal. The authors find the shortest-horizon effects are not universal across assets and time zones. [Intraday Sample](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2501.16772?page=13)[Small Predictive R2](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2501.16772?page=18)
- **Drawdown proximity as a portfolio state:** The restart method turns drawdown into an exposure-control input: reduce risk as the account approaches a portfolio limit, then restart conservatively rather than permanently disabling strategies. This is a portfolio governor, not a per-trade stop and not evidence that any one trade’s exit has alpha.
- **Exit calibration as a strategy-selection test:** Replay the same entry trades under competing exits, but preserve chronology and realistic fills. Track net expectancy, Sharpe, drawdown, expected shortfall, turnover, profit capture and gap-loss scenarios. The 2026 crypto replay’s large performance spread is a reason to run this test; its randomized final split is a reason not to adopt its winning parameters directly. [Split Limitation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150?page=2)

The classic empirical/theoretical literature is also relevant: [When Do Stop-Loss Rules Stop Losses?](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2013.10.003) (Kaminski and Lo, 2014) analyzes when stops alter return distributions and when they can help or hurt; [Risk Reduction Using Trailing Stop-Loss Rules](https://doi.org/10.1111/irfi.12310) (Dai, Marshall, Nguyen and Visaltanachoti, 2021) studies trailing stops as risk reduction. Neither provides a universal stop setting for the instruments above. Precise, comparable performance numbers for those studies are not available in the evidence I could verify here, so I would not quote a Sharpe or drawdown figure for them.

## 3. Useful combinations with other methods

These combinations are **design proposals**: the cited methods have not necessarily been tested together. The point is to join signal-conditioned exit choices to fill-aware execution and portfolio-level risk controls.

| Combine | Combined method | Why it is useful |
|---|---|---|
| **Triple-barrier/meta-labeling + OU optimal stopping + execution costs**: [Advances in Financial Machine Learning](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Advances+in+Financial+Machine+Learning-p-9781119482086), 2018; [Leung and Li](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1411.5062), 2015; and [Almgren and Chriss, “Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions”](https://doi.org/10.21314/JOR.2001.041), 2001. | Use triple barriers to define candidate profit, loss and time outcomes. Meta-label whether a proposed trade is worth taking; for accepted mean-reversion trades, select target and timeout from the current spread state and estimated reversion speed. Finally, estimate the cost of exiting now versus waiting and execute accordingly. | Separates “is this trade worth taking?” from “when should it exit?” and “how should it be executed?” Barriers define outcomes for learning; they are not guaranteed fills. |
| **Exit calibration + selection-bias correction + fractional sizing**: [2026 crypto exit replay](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150), 2026; [The Deflated Sharpe Ratio](https://doi.org/10.3905/jpm.2014.40.5.094), 2014; [Long-Term Capital Growth: The Good and Bad Properties of the Kelly and Fractional Kelly Capital Growth Criteria](https://doi.org/10.1080/14697680903124632), 2010. | Run a chronological walk-forward exit search; account for how many stop/target combinations were tried when evaluating Sharpe; then size below the estimated growth-optimal fraction when the edge estimate is uncertain. | Helps prevent a noisy “best” exit from receiving full risk budget simply because it topped a large parameter search. Kelly-style sizing does not fix gap risk or a misestimated return distribution. |
| **Trade stops + drawdown modulation/restart + circuit breaker**: [Leung and Li](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1411.5062), 2015; [Hsieh](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2303.02613), 2023; [crypto exit replay](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150), 2026. | Let per-trade exits manage thesis failure and profit-taking; let a portfolio governor reduce aggregate exposure near a drawdown budget; require a human-reviewed restart or conservative restart rule after a circuit-breaker event. | Avoids asking an individual stop to control correlated losses across many agents. Hsieh’s formal bound assumes bounded returns, so add explicit gap and limit-lock stress tests before interpreting it as a real-world maximum-loss guarantee. |
| **Exit timing + live alpha-decay monitoring + risk-off/event state**: [Safari and Schmidhuber](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2501.16772), 2025; [Interpretable Systematic Risk around the Clock](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.13458), 2026; [Optimal Trading with a Trailing Stop](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1701.03960), 2017. | Track whether the strategy’s conditional edge is fading, whether an event or risk state raises jump risk, and whether trend or reversal evidence supports holding. Tighten exposure or flatten when the **risk state** invalidates the trade, rather than mechanically moving every stop to breakeven. | Separates ordinary price noise from a change in the strategy’s premise. The cited papers do not validate this combined controller; estimate decay thresholds and event responses on your own live-like data. |
| **Exit rule + time-of-day cost model + scheduled-news window**: [Almgren and Chriss](https://doi.org/10.21314/JOR.2001.041), 2001; [Interpretable Systematic Risk around the Clock](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.13458), 2026; and the relevant market-specific exit model above. | Estimate spread, depth, impact and slippage by instrument, venue and time of day; add event-window jump and liquidity scenarios. When a target is reached, choose between immediate exit and staged execution using the expected cost of waiting and the risk of reversal or a jump. | Prevents the simulator from treating a stop or target as a frictionless price. It also makes it possible to distinguish an exit that improves gross P&L from one whose advantage survives actual fills. |

For an always-on, multi-strategy system, I’d implement these as separate layers: **trade-level thesis exit**, **execution policy**, and **portfolio-level risk governor**. Size from the realized, net loss distribution—including gap, venue and liquidity scenarios—not just nominal stop distance. Keep the nominal stop-risk budget as a first limit, but make the stressed tail-loss budget the binding survival constraint.

## Turn 1 (topic question, model: Smart) - answer

## Bottom line

For this book, treat stress testing as a **survival test of the whole trading system**, not just a market-return simulation. The dangerous case is many individually small strategies taking similar positions, triggering together, and trying to exit into the same widened, gapping, or disappearing liquidity. A generator that matches average returns, volatility, or pairwise correlations can still miss that failure mode.

Use historical replay and transparent hypothetical shocks as the primary survival tests; add reverse stress testing to find failures you did not think to specify. Use generative models to broaden and condition scenarios—not as the authority on how bad losses can get. Recent work makes an important distinction: for rare joint losses, matching marginal tails is insufficient. Extremal dependence—the directions in which several risk factors become extreme together—governs joint-stress probabilities and conditional scenarios. [*Simulating Stress Laws under Extremal Dependence*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056) formalizes this and proposes estimating the angular tail law from intermediate exceedances, then extrapolating the radial tail. [Tail Preservation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056?page=11)

## How I would build the scenario set

**1. Replay real episodes as synchronized paths.** Replay multivariate market data through the actual strategy and execution stack, preserving timestamps and each strategy’s state. Use multi-day paths as well as shock days: entry signals, inventory, holding periods, stop logic, and exit congestion can make the path matter more than the endpoint. Include episodes relevant to the book’s exposures—equity crashes, sharp rate and FX repricing, commodity shocks, volatility spikes, crypto selloffs, flash moves, exchange halts, and abrupt reopening gaps.

A replay is only as complete as its data and execution assumptions. Mark stale or missing quotes, closed markets, venue halts, and instruments trading on different schedules. Do not treat a stock-market close as evidence that its risk factor was unchanged while FX or crypto kept moving.

**2. Add explicit, coherent hypotheticals.** Shock drivers and linkages, not just individual tickers: for example, equity selloff plus volatility spike, currency moves, wider credit or funding spreads, and reduced depth; or an energy shock with inflation and rate repricing, index moves, and correlated FX exposure. Add path shape—gap, grind, whipsaw, one-way trend—and a volatility-regime transition. Make the scenario’s assumptions visible; a hypothetical is a deliberate challenge, not a forecast with an implied probability.

**3. Find failure modes in reverse.** Define concrete failure outcomes first: breach of drawdown or loss budget, margin shortfall, forced liquidation, or inability to exit within a specified time or cost. Then search for the least-implausible joint market and execution path that produces each outcome. Run this at both portfolio and strategy-cluster level. If a reverse test finds a credible failure combination absent from the historical set, add it to the standing stress library.

**4. Bootstrap dependence in blocks, not one asset or timestamp at a time.** For a nonparametric baseline, resample *synchronized multivariate blocks* so the same dates and intervals move together across assets. Use blocks long enough to retain the relevant intraday pattern and volatility persistence; stratify or reweight by regime rather than assuming calm and crisis observations are interchangeable. An iid bootstrap destroys serial clustering; independent asset bootstraps destroy the joint events the test is meant to find. Block bootstrapping cannot invent a crash or liquidity vacuum absent from the record, which is why it should complement shocks and tail extrapolation, not replace them.

**5. Model liquidity and execution as part of the scenario.** Reprice exits with stressed spreads, lower depth, larger slippage and market impact, latency or partial fills, and gaps through stops. Include venue and trading-hour constraints, fees, funding, and margin changes. For positions too large to liquidate at observed prices, model the exit as a sequence that consumes depth; do not assume every strategy can close at the mark simultaneously. When tick-level depth is unavailable, use explicit conservative liquidity haircuts and test a range rather than presenting a single precise cost estimate.

## How well do generators reproduce tails and dependence?

The answer is **not well enough to trust them without portfolio-specific validation**. Generative families solve different parts of the problem:

- **GANs and VAEs** can learn broad distributional and temporal patterns, but realistic-looking paths do not establish reliable extreme-loss frequency, tail asymmetry, or dependence in the joint tail. Check mode coverage and whether severe losses recur across independent training seeds.
- **Diffusion models** offer flexible conditional generation. A factor-structured model can help in high dimensions, while conditional models can target trend and volatility. But matching those conditions or matching a covariance matrix does not prove correct tail co-movement, liquidity behavior, or path-dependent strategy losses.
- **Copulas** separate margins from dependence and can be useful when paired with heavy-tailed or EVT-based margins. A static Gaussian copula is a poor sole stress engine: it does not represent asymptotic tail dependence. A t-copula adds symmetric tail dependence, but can still impose the wrong joint-extreme geometry.
- **Regime-switching models** can represent transitions between calm, trending, volatile, and crisis states. They help only if regime persistence, transition probabilities, and within-regime dependence are credible; a hidden-state fit is not a substitute for explicit stress overrides.
- **Block bootstrap** retains observed joint episodes and local path structure, but cannot extrapolate beyond the observed extremes and can over-repeat a small number of crisis blocks.
- **EVT or angular-tail generators** are especially promising for extrapolating rare joint events, but depend on stable tail estimation from limited data and a suitable threshold. The recent SSGEN paper makes the relevant target explicit: preserve the multivariate tail law that determines joint-stress probabilities and conditional stressed configurations, rather than merely matching separate marginal tails. [Tail Fidelity](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056?page=2)

In practice, generated scenarios should earn credibility by passing **out-of-sample, regime-aware tests** on the quantities the system cares about: marginal tail exceedances; joint exceedances and co-loss breadth across assets and strategy clusters; volatility clustering and regime duration; drawdown depth and duration; and portfolio loss, margin, liquidation time, and execution cost after replay through the strategies. Compare synthetic and observed statistics with uncertainty intervals. Pay particular attention to “many strategies lose together”: measure the fraction of strategies losing, aggregate loss conditional on that fraction, and which shared exposures or execution constraints caused the losses. Pairwise return correlations alone will not answer that question.

## Turn stress results into limits without capping growth

Separate **survival gates** from **growth controls**.

- **Survival gates:** For every credible historical, hypothetical, and reverse-stress scenario, require stressed loss plus stressed exit cost and liquidity or margin needs to remain inside human-approved loss, drawdown, and capital limits. Include shared exposures and correlated strategy losses; adding more strategies should not create the illusion of diversification if they all fail in the same scenario.
- **Growth controls:** Size live risk using marginal contribution to portfolio stress loss and liquidity demand, not equal allocations per strategy or a blanket cap on strategy count. A new strategy can earn more budget when it reduces portfolio stress loss or performs robustly across scenario families; scale it gradually when it adds concentrated exposure or depends on fragile execution.
- **Avoid false precision:** Use stress results as constraints and comparisons across scenario families, not as a claim that a fitted generator has discovered the true worst case or a precise failure probability. Keep a visible buffer for model and execution error. Do not relax a survival gate just because a generator, trained on a calm sample, reports lower risk.
- **Respect the promotion process:** Agents can propose and test strategies in replay and simulation; only the human promotion decision should allocate live capital. Make promotion contingent on passing the same portfolio-level survival tests, including tests against the existing live book—not just standalone backtests.

The operating principle is: **hard limits on credible paths to ruin; flexible, evidence-based risk budgets for strategies that improve the portfolio under stress.** That protects survival without treating every additional strategy or unit of trading activity as risk by itself.

## Turn 2 (follow-up a: top three methods and scenario-to-limits procedure, model: Smart) - answer

The best three are [Tail-GAN](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2203.01664) for testing the strategies’ actual P&L tails, [SSGEN](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056) for extrapolating rare *joint* losses, and [Correlation scenarios and correlation stress testing](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2107.06839) for challenging the diversification and hedge assumptions underneath the book. They solve different problems; none alone covers intraday execution, every market regime, and cross-asset tail dependence.

## 1. Tail-GAN: generate paths that preserve strategy loss tails

**What it does.** Tail-GAN trains a generator to produce price paths whose P&L distributions match tail-risk properties—especially VaR and expected shortfall—for a chosen set of benchmark strategies. Its key design choice is to train on strategy P&Ls, not just price-return statistics. [The paper](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2203.01664) gives this procedure:

1. Supply historical price paths across assets and code that computes P&L for each benchmark strategy on a path.
2. Generate candidate paths from noise; run the benchmark strategies on both generated and historical paths.
3. Train a discriminator using a tail-sensitive score based on strategy VaR and expected shortfall.
4. Update the generator to reduce the discrepancy in those tail-risk scores.
5. Sample from the trained generator, run the strategies on those paths, and use the resulting P&Ls to estimate each strategy’s loss distribution.

**Inputs and outputs.** Inputs are synchronized multi-asset price histories, a suite of strategy rules and their P&L calculation, and a tail confidence level. The output is a generator of multi-asset price scenarios; the relevant risk output is the distribution of strategy P&Ls under those scenarios, including estimated VaR and expected shortfall. For this trading system, that means including representative scalpers and intraday strategies in the benchmark suite, with costs in the P&L calculation. It does not automatically cover strategies that were not represented in training.

**Evidence and limits.** On a synthetic five-asset dataset, the paper reports a 4.6% out-of-sample relative-error measure for Tail-GAN’s VaR/ES estimates, versus 3.4% for historical simulation and 21.3% for WGAN. Thus Tail-GAN beat the general-purpose WGAN, but did **not** beat historical simulation on that aggregate metric. On the same synthetic experiment, the reported sum of absolute cross-correlation errors was 1.00 for Tail-GAN, compared with 2.95 for its static-strategy variant, 4.24 for its raw-data variant, and 6.11 for WGAN; its summed absolute autocorrelation error through ten lags was 2.73. These are experiment-specific aggregate diagnostics, not universal error rates. In the market-data experiment using 20 stocks and a one-month training period, the eigenportfolio version reported 25.6% out-of-sample VaR/ES relative error, close to historical simulation’s 25.9%, but well above the 2.2% sampling-error benchmark. [Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2203.01664?page=17) [Stock Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2203.01664?page=27)

The paper reports heavy-tail behavior through strategy-level VaR/ES comparisons, rather than a general guarantee that it reproduces every asset’s extreme tail. Volatility clustering and cross-asset dependence are checked, but those checks do not establish correct *joint-tail* dependence in a real, many-asset crisis. The approach also needs enough representative price paths and meaningful strategy examples; changing strategy logic or execution can invalidate the result. The authors explicitly identify limit-order-book data as a needed extension for realistic high-frequency strategy scenarios, so price-only training cannot establish realistic spread, depth, impact, queue, or gap costs. [Future Work](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2203.01664?page=27)

**Later work.** [SSGEN](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056) cites Tail-GAN as a strategy-tail-risk method, but argues that matching selected VaR/ES targets does not by itself ensure that the generator preserves the full multivariate tail law needed for arbitrary joint-loss events. The two methods complement one another: Tail-GAN puts the target strategies into the training objective; SSGEN focuses on the rare joint configurations that can make many strategies lose together. [Positioning](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056?page=5)

## 2. SSGEN: extrapolate rare joint-loss scenarios from intermediate extremes

**What it does.** SSGEN separates extreme observations into **magnitude** and **direction**. The magnitude is modeled with a Pareto tail; the direction captures which risk factors become extreme together. Its generation procedure is:

1. Form a synchronized multivariate risk-factor vector from each historical observation.
2. Compute each vector’s magnitude and direction; select observations above an intermediate magnitude threshold.
3. Fit a model to the directions of those exceedances and estimate the radial tail index.
4. Generate a direction from the fitted angular model and independently sample a Pareto-distributed magnitude.
5. Map generated risk-factor scenarios through strategy P&L functions, then retain or summarize scenarios meeting the chosen stress event—for example, losses across a specified subset of strategies.
6. For reverse stress, find the most plausible high-density configurations inside that loss region.

The theoretical target matters: SSGEN argues that correct marginal tails are not enough. To reproduce rare joint-loss probabilities and conditional stress scenarios, the generator must preserve the multivariate limiting tail measure, including extremal dependence. [The paper](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056) supplies an explicit method for building that tail generator. [Algorithm](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056?page=13) [Tail Fidelity](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056?page=11)

**Inputs and outputs.** Inputs are multivariate observations of risk factors, a threshold that leaves enough exceedances to estimate their directions, and the loss functions or strategy P&L functions to define the stress event. The output is a generated tail-scenario distribution, conditional loss distributions, and—if reverse-stressed—high-plausibility scenario configurations. The angular learner can be a diffusion model, GAN, or other estimator; in the paper’s numerical implementation, it is a diffusion model. [Diffusion Implementation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056?page=25)

**Evidence and limits.** The numerical demonstration is a *synthetic*, deliberately non-elliptical reinsurance example—not a live multi-market trading book. It uses 1,000 training samples, 10 risk factors, 20 loss-producing agents, and 100 intermediate exceedances. At a full-network stress probability of 0.005, median CoVaR relative errors were about 15% for SSGEN, 25% for direct empirical conditioning, and 47% for a fitted t-copula. For the scale-free loss-concentration measure, all three methods had median errors below 1% in the full-network case. The results support SSGEN’s value for the tested joint-stress quantities, but do not establish market-wide performance across real crisis histories. [Experiment](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056?page=24) [Comparison](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056?page=25)

The method’s own analysis exposes the sample-size tradeoff: using a higher threshold reduces extrapolation bias but leaves fewer exceedances with which to learn joint tail directions. It cannot learn a genuinely new regime or dependence pattern absent from the data without additional assumptions or imposed shocks. It also generates tail risk-factor observations, not a validated sequence of intraday paths: its tail-generation procedure does not establish realistic volatility clustering, temporal order, exchange sessions, or 24/7 interactions. A vector of synchronized returns can preserve cross-asset co-movement at its sampling interval, but the user must supply sensible time alignment for crypto, futures, FX, and session-based stocks. Price histories alone also cannot supply stressed depth, queue position, or market impact.

**Later work and contrast.** SSGEN is later than and explicitly contrasts itself with [Tail-GAN](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2203.01664), [ExceedGAN](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056), and other tail-generative methods. Its distinctive contribution is not a novel diffusion architecture; it is a criterion for what the generated law should preserve, plus a Pareto-and-angular construction for extrapolating beyond observed extremes. Its guarantees rely on heavy-tail and regularity assumptions, so they are not a license to extrapolate uncritically across a structural break. [Method Positioning](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056?page=5)

## 3. Packham–Woebbeking: stress correlation regimes and diversification

**What it does.** [Correlation scenarios and correlation stress testing](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2107.06839) models correlation changes through economically interpretable risk factors, then asks how those changes affect portfolio risk:

1. Map assets to a sparse set of factors, such as country and industry; Bayesian variable selection can use prior mappings and select additional factors.
2. Fit a parametric correlation structure linking pairs of assets to their shared and unshared factors.
3. Estimate the history of the correlation-model parameters.
4. Generate scenarios by changing those factor parameters and construct a valid correlation matrix.
5. Revalue a portfolio risk measure under each stressed matrix.
6. For reverse stress, find the highest-risk factor configurations within a plausibility region, measured using a Mahalanobis distance or highest-density region.

**Inputs and outputs.** Inputs are an asset-return panel, asset-to-factor information, and portfolio exposures or weights. Outputs are economically interpretable correlation scenarios and risk estimates such as stressed VaR; this is a stress test of *dependence*, not a complete generated price path or strategy-P&L distribution. A multi-strategy implementation would need to reprice each strategy under the stressed dependence scenarios, rather than treating the paper’s portfolio VaR output as sufficient.

**Evidence and limits.** In its North American and European stock-portfolio example, the authors calibrated a 34-dimensional parameter distribution using 552 observations. A Kolmogorov–Smirnov test did not reject the fitted marginal distributions at the 5% level for 19 of the 34 parameters. In the equally weighted portfolio, 1-day 99% stressed VaR often exceeded unstressed VaR by more than 50% in normal markets; the gap diminished during crises including 2008 and 2020, when the stress was partly realized. This is direct evidence that correlation stress can matter materially for that portfolio, not a calibrated result for high-frequency strategies. [Calibration](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2107.06839?page=18) [VaR Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2107.06839?page=19)

The method is useful for exposing lost diversification or failed hedges, but its correlation model is not the same as a heavy-tailed joint-return generator. Its primary example uses a variance-covariance VaR calculation; correlation shifts alone do not encode asymmetric tail dependence, strategy path dependence, changing volatility, or execution liquidity. The fitted dependence history may also be too short or too calm to represent an unseen regime. Its authors note that the specified correlation matrix may require a positive-semidefinite correction, which can alter the intended scenario. [Matrix Correction](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2107.06839?page=8)

**Later work and challenge.** The approach follows the authors’ earlier [factor-model approach for correlation scenarios](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1807.11381). [Beating the Correlation Breakdown](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2504.15268) develops a broader framework for dependence-matrix inference and flexible scenario restrictions, emphasizing that stress testing should challenge dependence itself—not only shock marginal inputs. Read that as a useful extension of the dependence-stress problem, not proof that one method resolves crisis correlation risk. The distinct limit of the Packham–Woebbeking method remains its focus on correlation scenarios rather than full price paths and strategy execution.

## Turning scenarios into limits without strangling growth

Keep two questions separate: **Can the book survive this scenario?** and **How much live risk should a particular strategy receive?**

1. **Build a common scenario library.** Include synchronized historical replays, explicit hypothetical market shocks, reverse-stress scenarios, and generated joint-tail scenarios. Attach separate execution overlays for spread widening, reduced depth, gaps, partial fills, halts, and stressed margin. Keep session calendars and timestamps explicit; do not silently fill a closed stock market with a crypto price move or assume simultaneous exits at a stale mark.
2. **Replay the whole system.** For each scenario, run all candidate strategies together, using their real entry, inventory, stop, exit, and sizing logic. Record each strategy’s loss and exit cost, losses by common signal/exposure cluster, and the portfolio loss, drawdown, liquidity need, and margin requirement. Shared execution capacity matters: if strategies compete for the same liquidity, calculate the combined exit rather than summing standalone cost estimates.
3. **Set survival gates at portfolio and cluster level.** Choose human-approved tolerances for total scenario loss, drawdown, margin, and liquidation time. Add cluster constraints for risks that can synchronize—for example, strategies trading the same index or sharing a short-volatility or momentum exposure. A per-strategy limit alone is not enough if the cluster or portfolio can breach together.
4. **Allocate strategy limits using incremental stress contribution.** Size a strategy by how much it adds to the book’s stressed loss and liquidity demand, and how it changes losses across the full scenario set. A strategy that diversifies the existing book may justify more budget; one that duplicates a crowded exposure should use more of the shared cluster budget. Avoid fixed equal risk allocations or a blunt strategy-count cap.
5. **Use growth objectives inside the survival constraints.** Among strategies that pass the same survival gates, allocate additional capital to those with robust out-of-sample performance after costs and useful portfolio-level diversification. Promote and scale gradually; rerun the combined-book tests after material changes to strategy logic, capital, venue, or execution assumptions. Agents can propose and simulate strategies, but live promotion remains a human decision.

Liquidity needs its own stress input. [Robust Hedging Valuation Adjustment under Liquidity–Demand Stress](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.26731) is a useful, narrower illustration: it simulates spread and quadratic market-impact costs, then robustly reweights high-loss scenarios. In its one-option, one-year experiment, the selected no-trade band widened from 0.02 in high liquidity to 0.10 in medium liquidity and 0.30 in low liquidity under the paper’s stated objective and assumptions. That is evidence that execution costs can change the risk–trading tradeoff—not a portable setting for scalpers or a multi-asset limit rule. [Liquidity Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.26731?page=9)

**Refresh cadence:** update exposure, P&L, cost, and limit monitoring daily; refresh rolling estimates and data-driven scenario weights monthly; and perform a full scenario and generator validation quarterly. Rerun sooner after a volatility or correlation break, a venue or instrument change, a severe live drawdown, or a material strategy or execution update. Keep a fixed historical crisis library between refreshes so that recent calm data cannot quietly erase the scenarios the book is required to survive.

## Turn 3 (follow-up b: asset-class scenarios, growth vs survival, trading ideas and combinations, model: Smart) - answer

The key is to stress **common mechanisms**, not just asset labels: a macro shock can move several markets, while margin calls, synchronized signals, and thin exits turn those moves into a portfolio-wide loss. The papers offer useful pieces—strategy-level tail calibration, joint-tail extrapolation, drawdown control, and agent-herding tests—but none validates this whole system end to end.

## 1. Asset-specific stresses and contagion

Use each scenario as a **path** with a trigger, propagation, and exit phase. A one-day return shock misses the effects of session boundaries, contract expiry, delayed margin calls, and strategies competing to exit.

| Asset class | Scenarios to include | What to model beyond the price move |
|---|---|---|
| **FX** | Unexpected central-bank decisions or intervention; inflation surprise; peg or band break; weekend gap; carry-trade unwind | Separate liquid G10 from thinner crosses and pegged currencies. Stress funding and cross-currency basis as well as spot; test a move through the peg with poor weekend liquidity, not just a large but continuously tradable devaluation. |
| **Stock indices** | Overnight gap opens; sharp volatility repricing; constituent-market divergence; trading pauses or limit rules; index-futures-led selloffs | Include overnight futures and cash-market reopening, volatility-control or margin-related deleveraging, and basis dislocations. A stop placed at yesterday’s close is not assumed to fill there after a gap. |
| **Metals** | Gold or silver squeeze; short covering; sharp real-rate or dollar moves; physical-market or inventory stress | Stress both outright prices and relevant spreads or basis. Include margin increases, thin depth, and short-covering that makes the exit more expensive as the price rises. |
| **Energy** | Front-month oil collapse near expiry; limit moves; storage or delivery constraint; sharp supply shock; negative prices | Make contract maturity, delivery eligibility, storage constraints, and the front/next-month spread explicit. In its analysis of WTI in 2020 and nickel in 2022, [*Negative Oil & Nickel Squeeze*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.00951) models forced position closure as a price-feedback mechanism: constrained longs selling can amplify a decline, while constrained shorts buying to cover can amplify a squeeze. The paper reports WTI at −$37.63 per barrel on April 20, 2020; it is an explanatory model, not evidence that this mechanism alone caused either episode. |
| **Crypto** | Perpetual-futures liquidation cascade; stablecoin or collateral depeg; funding spike; venue outage; cross-venue basis break | Stress leverage, open interest, taker flow, collateral haircuts, and exchange-specific liquidation rules. Model an outage as both lost execution access and possible concentration of forced flow on venues that remain open. Crypto trades through stock-market closures; do not impute a closed market’s price as if it were continuous. |
| **Futures** | Expiry or roll squeeze; calendar-spread dislocation; margin shock; limit-up/limit-down or halt; delivery constraint | Simulate each contract and roll leg separately, including the spread, expiry calendar, settlement convention, and position migration. A continuous back-adjusted series can conceal a loss on the expiring leg or a costly roll. |
| **Single stocks** | Earnings or guidance gap; halt and reopening; borrow squeeze; sudden downgrade or fraud news; index or sector shock | Include overnight news, borrow availability, halt duration, reopening auction, and jump-through-stop behavior. Sector and index shocks should propagate to single names, but company-specific earnings gaps should not be forced to look like ordinary sector beta. |

For crypto, a useful monitoring candidate is not “rising volatility predicts a crash.” In [*Where does the criticality live?*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.27070), a study of seven BTC perpetual liquidation cascades found that early-warning patterns varied by event. Price showed the tested autocorrelation signature in five of seven events, but not the two sudden-news events; variance trends were generally not discriminating. Taker order-flow variance compression was the one population-level regularity in six events, with a placebo-combined $p \approx 5 \times 10^{-6}$, but it was **not** a reliable alarm for each individual cascade. Treat it as a research signal or scenario-conditioning feature, not a standalone kill switch. [Order Flow](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.27070?page=8)

### Cross-class contagion

Represent the scenario as a **time-indexed dependency graph**, with common shocks and conditional links, not as one static correlation matrix. For example:

- A central-bank or inflation surprise moves rates, FX, gold, and equity-index futures; margin and volatility changes can then force position reductions.
- An energy supply or delivery shock moves front-month energy and calendar spreads, then inflation expectations, rates, FX, and equity sectors.
- A crypto liquidation or collateral event can affect other crypto venues through arbitrage and shared collateral; treat contagion to listed equities as a separate, empirically testable link rather than assuming it.
- A stock-index gap can trigger volatility, margin, and hedging flows that spill into futures, FX funding, and correlated single names.

For each edge, specify direction, plausible lag, stress regime, and evidence source. Replay observed episodes using synchronized timestamps where possible; where history is too short or markets have different sessions, explicitly impose the missing shock and vary the lag. Preserve a scenario where one market is closed or one venue is unavailable. The purpose is not to assert that every link always operates; it is to expose losses when a plausible chain does.

## 2. Performance as well as survival

Limits cost growth when they reduce exposure to strategies that have a real, diversified edge; they pay for themselves when they prevent a loss, forced deleveraging, or execution spiral large enough to impair future compounding. The research suggests making limits **selective**—conditional on horizon, market structure, and portfolio contribution—rather than applying the same haircut everywhere.

In [*Is Trend Still Your Friend?*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.01550), a study of about 100 futures markets from 1995–2025, the reported Sharpe for a fast trend signal fell from $0.84 \pm 0.27$ before 2009 to $0.12 \pm 0.24$ after 2008; a slower signal fell from $0.70 \pm 0.27$ to $0.40 \pm 0.26$. The authors report that post-break trend performance differed by volatility-normalized tick size: small-tick futures deteriorated much more than large-tick ones. That argues for testing limits and capacity by **instrument microstructure and signal horizon**, rather than banning all trend trading or treating a whole asset class as homogeneous. The paper’s explanation is a proposed market-impact mechanism, not proof that every trend signal or venue shares it. [Sharpe Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.01550?page=5) [Tick Size Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.01550?page=11)

A separate example of the survival/growth tradeoff is [CAST](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.14205), which combines an online cross-asset Kalman predictor with a model-predictive controller that penalizes forecast uncertainty. In its NASDAQ ablation, the full system reported annualized Sharpe $0.523$, maximum drawdown $0.114$, and final value $1{,}633$; removing its risk penalty gave Sharpe $-0.884$, drawdown $0.806$, and final value $200$. That is a large difference in the paper’s test, but it does **not** establish that CAST transfers to scalping, FX, or a multi-asset production portfolio. It illustrates why an external controller can manage exposure without simply forbidding trades. [Risk Penalty](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.14205?page=8)

The cost of overly blunt stress limits is that they may shrink profitable activity in normal conditions. The cost of weak limits is potentially nonlinear: forced exits can make the realized loss much larger than the initial shock. In [*Trading Agents or Market Crashers?*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19705), 400 simulated agents selling into a 2010-sized shock deepened the modeled fall from 9.2% to 17.3%; a 10-minute interrupt outside the LLM and hard loss limits removed the simulated amplification. In a separate fake-headline setup, the market pause did not activate in any of 300 runs, while the external controls prevented the modeled effect. These are results from a one-stock simulation, not estimates of the number of agents or the crash probability for your book; the practical lesson is to keep emergency controls outside the strategy or agent. [Agent Amplification](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19705?page=17) [External Controls](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19705?page=3)

### Strategy or signal ideas worth testing

These are **research hypotheses to test**, not trading recommendations or claims of proven profitability.

- **Microstructure-filtered futures trend:** Apply volatility-scaled fast and slow trend signals across liquid futures, but constrain sizing by tick-size tier, spread, participation, and impact. The trend paper suggests the market structure may be as important as asset class. Inputs: contract-level prices, tick size, spread/depth, volume, and execution data; futures roll calendars are essential.
- **Crypto liquidation-risk throttle:** Reduce risk or avoid new leverage when taker-flow and open-interest conditions resemble prior cascade buildup; test flow-variance compression only as one feature among several, given its weak event-level alarm performance. Inputs: venue-specific minute prices, taker buy/sell flow, open interest, funding, liquidations if available, and venue outage/depeg records.
- **Commodity expiry/roll dislocation monitor:** Track front/next-month spread, open interest, days to last trade, inventory or delivery indicators, and liquidity. A strategy could avoid carrying constrained front-month exposure or trade the spread when forced roll pressure becomes large. The WTI/nickel model motivates the signal; it does not establish a profitable spread trade.
- **Post-shock recovery or crisis-alpha re-entry:** After a gap or liquidation event, test staged re-entry into diversified trend or mean-reversion signals only after liquidity and spreads normalize. None of the cited stress papers demonstrates this as a profitable recovery strategy. Evaluate it separately by event type: endogenous buildup and sudden exogenous news should not be treated as the same setup.
- **Earnings-gap reaction:** For single stocks, compare continuation, reversal, and no-trade rules after earnings gaps, conditional on halt/reopening behavior, spread, and overnight volume. The relevant data are timestamped earnings/news, after-hours prices, auction prints, order-book depth, borrow, and the strategy’s actual fills.

## 3. Useful combinations of papers and methods

**[Tail-GAN](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2203.01664) + [SSGEN](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.13056) + execution-cost stress.** Tail-GAN trains on benchmark strategies’ P&Ls to reproduce their VaR and expected-shortfall behavior; SSGEN targets the multivariate tail law needed for rare joint losses. Run generated price/factor scenarios through the real strategy rules, then add stressed spreads, depth, slippage, gaps, and venue availability. This combines strategy-specific tail targets with a joint-tail generator; it still requires separate validation of temporal paths and liquidity, which price-return generation alone does not provide.

**[Packham–Woebbeking](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2107.06839) + SSGEN + strategy-cluster budgets.** The correlation-stress method changes dependence through interpretable factors and asks which plausible factor configurations most increase risk; SSGEN adds extreme joint configurations that static or ordinary correlation scenarios can miss. Map strategies to common market, factor, signal, and execution clusters; test losses when dependence tightens, reverses, or becomes asymmetric. Use the resulting loss distribution to budget clusters, not merely assets. This is particularly useful where several strategies appear diversified in calm-period correlations but share crisis exposure.

**SSGEN + [CAST](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.14205) + an external kill switch.** Use tail scenarios to estimate which forecast or position states generate severe portfolio loss; use CAST’s uncertainty-aware controller as a candidate sizing layer; and place hard loss, exposure, and market-data interrupts outside the strategy/LLM. Compare returns and drawdowns under the same walk-forward period with and without each control. The point is not to let the generator or predictor decide when it is safe, but to set a hard survival envelope around a flexible controller.

**[*TradingAgents*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2412.20138) + [FARSIGHT](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19705) + Tail-GAN/SSGEN.** TradingAgents provides a multi-role LLM trading workflow; FARSIGHT tests correlated decisions, misleading news, external interrupts, and hard controls; Tail-GAN or SSGEN can supply stress paths and joint-loss cases. Keep agents in proposal or simulation mode, run their proposed decisions through shared-book stress tests, and reject or cap actions that breach limits. Add shared-news-feed and same-model scenarios: individually cautious agents can still herd. The original TradingAgents experiment was a short, three-month stock backtest and should not be read as validation of performance under stress or in a 24/7 multi-asset book.

**[Packham–Woebbeking](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2107.06839) + FARSIGHT + a market simulator.** Apply dependence shocks to a simulated market with multiple strategies or agents, and measure whether synchronized rebalancing, forced exits, or common signals amplify a shock. FARSIGHT itself uses a simulated market to test such amplification; extending that experiment to your actual strategy and execution mix would be more informative than assuming standalone stress losses add linearly. Calibrate the simulator against order-book and fill data before trusting its impact estimates.

### Sizing and limits that leave room for growth

Use the same scenario library to calculate loss vectors for every strategy and the combined book. Then:

1. **Set hard portfolio survival gates** for total stressed loss, drawdown, margin, and time/cost to exit. Include at least one scenario in which spreads gap, depth disappears, or a venue is unavailable.
2. **Set cluster budgets** for shared exposures—such as index beta, short volatility, carry, trend, common news response, or venue/liquidity dependence. If several strategies lose together, charge their joint loss to the shared cluster budget rather than granting each a standalone allowance.
3. **Allocate within each cluster by incremental portfolio contribution.** A strategy that lowers combined stress loss or adds genuinely different behavior can earn more budget; a near-duplicate signal consumes the existing cluster’s budget. Standalone Sharpe should not override a material deterioration in portfolio stress loss.
4. **Shrink allocations when edge estimates are uncertain**, rather than using a permanent blanket haircut. Require robust out-of-sample evidence after costs, scale capital gradually, and increase it only when performance and scenario contributions remain stable across different regimes.
5. **Enforce the hard gates outside the strategy and LLM.** On a breach, pause new risk, block increases, or reduce positions according to a pre-approved procedure; re-enable only after an explicit check. This helps avoid both uncontrolled cascades and a permanent risk-off setting that suppresses growth during ordinary conditions.

Maintain the severe historical and hypothetical scenarios as a fixed core; refresh rolling market estimates and execution costs as conditions change. A reasonable operating cadence is daily limit monitoring, monthly updates to rolling dependence/liquidity estimates, and a quarterly full validation—sooner after a structural break, major venue or contract change, strategy modification, or serious loss.

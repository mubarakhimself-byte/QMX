## What the evidence says about measured returns

| Result | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Perpetual costs can reverse apparent profitability.** In AutoQuant’s BTC/USDT cost-ablation case, annualized return was 272.6% with fees, slippage and funding; 430.8% with fees only; and 522.5% with zero costs. The authors describe the figures as diagnostic and specific to that strategy and setup—not scalable return expectations. [Cost Sensitivity](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476?page=52) | Report net returns under realistic costs and cost stress; don’t infer that subtracting a fixed fee from gross returns captures the effect. |
| **Maker fills can have negative selection.** In the Binance experiment, filled orders had an average roughly **−0.8 bp** five-second return, or about **−0.3 bp net of the assumed maker rebate**. The paper also reports a fill-probability/post-fill-return trade-off; average fill rates alone hide that selection. [Fill and Markout](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=20) | Model fill probability and post-fill markout jointly, conditional on the order-book state, queue position and cancellation policy. Do not assume passive orders fill randomly. |
| **Execution rules can change rankings as well as returns.** The 2026 synthetic benchmark reports Kendall rank agreement of 0.21 between near-ideal and default-stress execution in its high-volatility, two-asset setting, versus 0.82 in the calm setting. The authors caution that the low-agreement result belongs to that short, compressed experiment. [Ranking Sensitivity](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.05077v1?page=3) | Re-run strategy comparisons under alternate execution assumptions. Don’t apply one estimated cost correction to a leaderboard and assume its ordering survives. |
| **Last look is a spread–rejection trade-off.** The FX model finds that last look can reduce quoted spreads, while also rejecting trades—including slow traders’ most profitable ones. Therefore a tighter quoted spread is not, by itself, evidence of lower effective trading cost. [Last Look Effects](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1806.04460v1?page=17) | Track acceptance/rejection rates and the outcomes of rejected requests, alongside quoted spread and realized fills. |

The retrieved research is much stronger on **crypto maker-fill selection and FX last-look mechanisms** than on portable retail-broker estimates of spread by hour, weekday, news or rollover. Those costs vary by venue, account type, symbol and broker policy. For a retail-access system, build those schedules from the actual account’s quote and order logs rather than transplanting interbank quotes or one exchange’s statistics.

## Recommendations for the cost model

**Use executable, account-specific FX data.** Interbank or institutional ECN quotes are not a retail execution record. A retail broker may add a markup, expose the account to different depth, or apply last look and other acceptance rules; the FX paper describes how last look can make fills non-guaranteed even where quoted spreads are tighter. [Retail Execution](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1806.04460v1?page=2) Collect the broker’s bid and ask and the strategy’s own order requests, fills, rejections and timestamps. Keep account type and broker separate in calibration.

**Estimate spread distributions by venue, symbol and time context.** Store spread observations in UTC and in relevant local-market time; estimate conditional distributions—not just means—by hour, weekday, session overlap, news window, holidays and rollover. Rollover deserves its own treatment for FX because financing changes there; avoid assuming the ordinary intraday spread schedule applies. Use wider cost scenarios when observations are sparse or markets are stressed. Avoid a universal “news multiplier”: estimate one only where your venue data supports it.

**Model each order type according to how it actually executes.**
- **Market orders:** charge the observed side of the spread, commission, and slippage relative to the decision-time executable quote. Let slippage depend on volatility, latency, size and available depth; record partial fills and rejects.
- **Limit orders:** estimate fill probability conditional on queue or book state, time at price, order size and cancel policy. For fills, include maker/taker fees as actually charged and measure markouts at multiple horizons. A filled passive order can be more expensive than an unfilled one because fills are selected when prices move against it. In the Binance experiment, fill probability depended on queue conditions, and the authors caution that the attractive fee or rebate does not make maker execution automatically profitable. [Queue and Markout](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=16)
- **Stops:** model the trigger and the subsequent executable order separately. A stop is not a guaranteed fill at its trigger price: include gaps, spread widening and latency after activation.

**Charge financing at the correct timestamps and positions.** For FX, include the broker’s swap/rollover schedule, including the applicable weekday treatment and account-specific rates. For crypto perpetuals, apply the realized funding rate at the venue’s funding timestamp to the position held then, with the correct sign. AutoQuant’s implementation applies funding only using information available under its next-bar convention and keeps funding separate from trading fees and slippage. [Funding Alignment](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476?page=34)

**Make the backtester and live monitor use the same cost components and accounting rules.** Have both call one versioned cost-model interface. For each order or position interval, log a reconciliable ledger: decision-time quote, requested and filled quantity, execution price, fee, spread cost, slippage, impact estimate, reject/partial-fill status, swap or funding, and net P&L. Compare predicted versus realized costs by instrument, venue, order type, hour and strategy; alert when residuals or rejection rates drift outside calibrated ranges. AutoQuant’s auditable per-bar ledger and accounting-invariant checks are a useful pattern for keeping simulation and execution consistent. [Audit Artifacts](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476?page=60)

Finally, because many strategies trade simultaneously, compute costs from the **combined orders reaching the same venue**, not only each strategy’s isolated hypothetical order. Preserve strategy-level attribution in the ledger, but let shared liquidity, queue competition, fee tiers and capacity constraints reflect the aggregate flow.

## Turn 2 (follow-up a: top three methods, a shared cost-model schema and a drift alert, model: Smart) - answer

For a shared backtest-and-live cost model, I’d combine three complementary approaches: **AutoQuant’s auditable cost ledger**, **the Binance live-order evidence on maker fills and adverse selection**, and **the FX last-look model**. None is a complete retail-venue model: the first uses simplified costs, the second is one exchange and mostly minimum-size maker orders, and the third is theoretical rather than an empirical retail-broker calibration.

## Three papers and what to take from them

### 1. [AutoQuant: An Auditable Expert-System Framework for Execution-Constrained Auto-Tuning in Cryptocurrency Perpetual Futures](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476) (2025): the shared accounting and replay method

**What it models, step by step.** AutoQuant takes a signal, applies it with a strict next-bar delay, translates the resulting position change into turnover, charges fees and a per-trade slippage allowance on that turnover, then charges funding on the position held over the relevant interval. It produces a net return series and exports a per-bar ledger so the same cost components can be reconciled through backtest, replay and live-style execution. Its conceptual strength is the *shared, auditable accounting path*; its actual fill model is much simpler than a retail execution simulator. [Cost Model](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476v1?page=33) [Funding and Ledger](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476v1?page=34)

**Inputs and outputs.** The paper’s inputs include a position or exposure change, taker-fee rate, fixed slippage in basis points, an aligned eight-hour funding-rate series, bar duration and leverage/notional limits. It supports a maker/taker mix but uses taker execution in its main case. It does **not** take a live quote, book depth, order queue, order-specific fill probability, or hour-of-day spread schedule as inputs. Its outputs are turnover-based fee and slippage charges, funding charge, and net per-bar return—not a predicted fill probability or a venue-specific slippage distribution. Its BTC case uses 4 bp taker fee, 2 bp slippage, realized funding when available, and a conservative fallback funding rate. [Baseline Costs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476v1?page=34)

**Reported return impact.** In a BTC/USDT four-hour cost-ablation case, the fully costed version reported CAGR 2.726, Sharpe 2.049 and maximum drawdown 0.265; the fee-only version reported 4.308, 2.506 and 0.262; zero cost reported 5.225, 2.711 and 0.260. Those are decimal CAGRs—272.6%, 430.8% and 522.5%—from a single strategy-family stress test, not estimates of typical retail returns. [Cost Ablation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476v1?page=45)

**Where it fails for your use case.** The authors explicitly do not model market impact, and the cost ablation is a fixed-per-turnover slippage model. It doesn’t capture broker requotes or last-look rejection, stop execution through gaps, intraday spread states, queueing, partial fills, or funding-rate spikes beyond applying the realized rate series at the correct timestamp. A fallback funding rate is not a substitute for a live forecast of a spike. [Limits](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476v1?page=45)

**Later comparison.** [Machine Learning-Based Bitcoin Trading Under Transaction Costs: Evidence From Walk-Forward Forecasting](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00060) (2026) is a useful challenge to *fixed proportional costs as a sufficient execution model*. It charges a constant 10 bp per position change and finds that naïve sign-based strategies turn sharply negative; its cost-aware filter improves performance, but the paper also acknowledges that it does not simulate order-book depth, partial fills, maker/taker routing, variable spreads or funding. In its long-only XGBoost example, return changes from −64.00% under the unfiltered costed strategy to 65.40% with the cost-aware filter, while trades fall from 10,619 to 251. That illustrates both the power of trading less and the danger of mistaking a cost hurdle for a fill model. [Cost-Aware Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00060v1?page=18) [Limitations](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00060v1?page=31)

### 2. [The Market Maker’s Dilemma: Navigating the Fill Probability vs. Post-Fill Returns Trade-Off](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625) (2025): the maker-fill and adverse-selection method

**What it models, step by step.** The study submits maker orders at the top bid and ask, records whether each fills or is cancelled when the price level ceases to be at the touch, and tracks queue conditions and subsequent price drift. It estimates empirical fill frequency conditional on near-side and opposite-side queue size, then studies markout conditional on fill and queue position. The core lesson is that fill chance and fill quality are linked: a resting order may be most likely to fill precisely when the price is moving against it. [Order Experiment](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=10) [Fill-Rate Inputs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=13)

**Inputs and outputs.** Inputs are order side, top-of-book queues and the order’s place in its queue, plus the subsequent price path. The outputs are empirical fill probability and post-fill markout at chosen horizons. It does not produce a retail-market-order slippage model, stop-fill model, or funding charge. Its experiment used 232,897 minimum-sized Binance BTC perpetual maker orders over one week; 127,051 filled. Its public-feed method could identify queue position for those fills, but the authors note that Level 3 data needed for full order-level queue information is unavailable on most major crypto exchanges. [Data and Fees](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=10) [Queue Data Limits](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=16)

**Reported return impact.** Filled maker orders had an average return around **−0.8 bp**, or **−0.3 bp net of the assumed maker rebate**, over the reported short horizon. Its simple balanced-inventory market-making case lost almost **60%** over roughly three days and seven hours, with an annualized Sharpe of **−109**. A selected reversal-filtered configuration reported **+0.71 bp per round trip** and Sharpe **11.97**, but this was a short experiment, depended on minimum-size orders and the best assumed maker rebate, and should not be read as a stable strategy return. [Basic Strategy Loss](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=22) [Reversal Strategy](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=30)

**Where it fails for your use case.** Results are from one venue, instrument and short period; most observations are minimum-size maker orders, and the fill policy is specific to the experiment. It does not model retail FX broker acceptance, stop slippage in gaps, perpetual funding, or every maker/taker order-routing case. Sparse own-fill data is a real issue: public quote replay may not reveal the retail broker’s queue, internalization or cancellation behavior. The paper itself warns that fills inferred from other traders’ orders can be biased by their unknown cancellation policies. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2502.18625v2" title="Fill Data Bias" page="3" first="The latter question examines" last=">95% of submitted maker orders are cancelled;" />

**Later related work.** [Model-Free Passive Execution via Order-Level Shadowing](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.18019) (2026) cites this study for the adverse-selection inherited by resting orders, then proposes a passive execution method that shadows observed orders rather than predicting their fill probabilities. It is a related benchmark, not a retail-broker validation. The later [Machine Learning-Based Bitcoin Trading Under Transaction Costs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00060) is a contrasting bar-based approach: it demonstrates large cost sensitivity but explicitly omits queue-level fills, so it does not overturn the live-order evidence.

### 3. [Foreign Exchange Markets with Last Look](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1806.04460) (2018): the FX reject/quote mechanism

**What it models, step by step.** This is a theoretical dealer model. A broker posts a bid/ask spread; prices can update before a client’s order is processed; if the venue supports last look, the broker can accept or reject the trade during a short post-trade window based on a rejection threshold. The model distinguishes slow traders from latency arbitrageurs and solves for spreads consistent with the broker’s expected losses and the rejection rule. It also accounts conceptually for a customer’s costs when rejected: missed profit, immediacy, returning to the market, and possible exposure while re-entering. [Last Look Setup](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1806.04460v1?page=4) [Rejected Trade Costs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1806.04460v1?page=19)

**Inputs and outputs.** Its inputs are price volatility and dependence between price revisions, proportion of latency-sensitive flow, the broker’s last-look threshold, and trader response assumptions. It returns theoretical equilibrium quoted spreads, slow-trader execution probability and expected costs. It does **not** take retail account quote histories, a real order’s size or time-of-day bucket, a stop trigger, swap rate or observed broker rejection log as input. Its quoted spread is therefore not a retail fill-cost quote.

**Reported return impact.** This paper does **not** report a measured change in a strategy’s return, Sharpe or drawdown. Its contribution is about spreads, execution probability and who bears the cost of rejected trades. It finds that last look can tighten quoted spreads while rejecting some customer trades that moved in the customer’s favor; thus a tighter displayed spread can coexist with a worse effective execution outcome. [Slow Trader Outcomes](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1806.04460v1?page=17)

**Where it fails for your use case.** It is a stylized model, not an empirical calibration of today’s retail FX brokers. It does not give account-specific rejection or requote rates, retail news/rollover spread curves, or stop-loss slippage distributions. It also does not model perpetual funding. For actual retail execution, estimate acceptance, rejection and requote behavior from the broker’s own order-request and fill records; interbank or ECN quotes alone cannot reveal that account’s outcomes.

**Later extension.** [Dynamic slippage control and rejection feedback in spot FX market making](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.07752) (2026) explicitly cites the last-look literature and extends the mechanism with a slippage tolerance chosen jointly with quotes and an EMA rejection-feedback state. It is a theoretical market-making extension, not a retail-broker fill dataset, so it helps identify useful state variables but does not supply retail calibration. Another later paper, [Strategic OTC market making with reputation feedback](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.11328) (2026), models how rejection/fill ratios can affect future OTC flow; again, it is a model, not a retail execution-quality study.

## Live drift alert

After every fill, compare **realized cost** with the model’s prediction using the same benchmark, units and component definitions. For each venue/account/symbol/order-type/time bucket, track (1) residual slippage in bp, (2) total-cost residual, (3) limit fill-probability calibration, (4) rejection/requote rate, and (5) funding/swap reconciliation error.

A practical initial policy—not a threshold established by these papers—is to emit a **cost-drift warning** when the 20-fill EWMA of realized-minus-predicted total cost exceeds both **1 bp and 3 standard errors** for that bucket, or when observed limit-fill/rejection calibration falls outside its 99% binomial prediction interval. Escalate to a **trading gate** if the breach persists for two consecutive windows, the broker’s rejection/requote rate jumps materially, or the model cannot reconcile charged swap/funding cashflows. For rare events like gaps, news and rollover, use event-specific thresholds and immediate alerts rather than waiting for a 20-fill sample.

Keep the response operationally separated: a cost alert should first raise the strategy’s modeled hurdle and mark the calibration stale; it should not rewrite past costs or auto-promote a strategy. The accounting should reconcile across backtest and live execution, as AutoQuant recommends, while the event-level calibrations supply the venue detail its simple cost model lacks. [Full-Chain Invariants](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476v1?page=22)

## Turn 3 (follow-up b: other classes, edge lost to costs, cost-aware ideas and combinations, model: Smart) - answer

**Use one cost ledger, but calibrate it by instrument, venue, account type and order type.** The biggest structural mistake would be to apply one “bps per trade” assumption across futures, CFDs and cash stocks: some costs are paid at order entry, some accrue while a position is held, and some appear only when the contract is rolled or a short position is borrowed.

## 1. How the model should differ by asset class

The table describes typical cost channels, not a universal ranking: the actual order depends on broker, account, contract and trading hours. “Scalping” means the expected edge is especially sensitive to a few ticks and execution latency; intraday positions usually trade less often, so overnight financing may not apply, but session changes and event gaps can still matter.

| Instrument and access route | Costs and mechanics the model should represent | Likely dominant costs: scalping vs. intraday |
|---|---|---|
| **Stock indices**: index futures, or broker CFDs | For futures: bid–ask spread in ticks, exchange/clearing/broker commissions per contract, tick value, depth and impact, plus calendar-roll execution when moving contract months. The futures price embeds financing and expected dividends, so don’t add a CFD-style overnight charge to a futures position. For index CFDs: spread or commission, broker financing/swap for positions held across the broker’s cutoff, and cash adjustments for dividends. | **Scalping:** spread, commissions and slippage/latency; tick value and minimum tick make small gross edges vulnerable to fixed per-contract costs. **Intraday:** spread and execution still matter; financing is generally absent if the position closes before the broker’s cutoff, but a late close or gap through a stop can change that. |
| **Metals**: futures or spot/CFD | For futures: tick/spread, exchange and broker fees, depth/impact, and calendar roll. For gold/silver CFDs or spot-like products: spread/commission, overnight financing/swap, and possibly instrument-specific contract multipliers. Do not treat the CFD’s financing charge as if it were a futures roll cost. | **Scalping:** spread, fees and slippage, especially when the book is thin or the market is moving quickly. **Intraday:** execution costs remain important; financing matters for positions crossing the broker’s cutoff, rather than for every intraday round trip. |
| **Energy**: futures or CFD | For futures: tick and contract value, exchange/clearing/broker fees, depth/impact, expiry and roll execution, and the price difference between delivery months (calendar spread/term structure). A continuous back-adjusted series is not an executable price series and can hide roll effects. For CFDs: spread/commission, overnight financing and the broker’s contract/expiry or adjustment rules. | **Scalping:** spread, fees and impact; event-driven moves can make the executable price diverge sharply from a bar close. **Intraday:** execution plus scheduled inventory/news volatility; for positions held through expiry, rolling and term-structure exposure matter more than a generic “roll fee.” |
| **Single stocks**: direct shares or stock CFDs | For direct shares: spread, commissions, exchange/regulatory charges where applicable, market impact, minimum tick, and **stock borrow** for shorts (including availability, borrow rate and recalls). For CFDs: spread/commission, financing on long exposure, borrow/short charges where applicable, and dividend cash adjustments. Corporate actions and trading halts need explicit handling. | **Scalping:** spread, tick size, fees and depth/impact; on low-priced or thin names, a single tick can be a large fraction of the expected edge. **Intraday:** the same costs apply, while short borrow and financing matter if the position crosses the relevant charging time; event gaps, halts and hard-to-borrow restrictions can dominate the nominal spread. |
| **Listed futures across all classes** | Model actual tradable contract months and the roll order, not just a synthetic continuous price. Store tick size, tick value, contract multiplier, exchange and broker fees, initial/maintenance margin limits, spread, depth and partial fills by contract month. Separate price P&L from roll execution and calendar basis. | **Scalping:** spread + per-contract fees + adverse selection/slippage are usually the first hurdle. **Intraday:** these still matter, but roll dates, session liquidity and holding-period exposure can become material, especially if the system carries positions into expiry. |

A useful implementation rule is to maintain **two different cost calculations** for futures and CFDs. Futures have explicit exchange/broker charges and expiry/roll mechanics; their financing and carry are reflected in the futures curve. CFDs typically have a broker quote and explicit financing or swap cashflows, and the broker’s own cutoff and dividend-adjustment rules matter. A CFD quote is not an exchange order book, so exchange depth should not be presented as the account’s guaranteed executable liquidity.

## 2. How much reported edge disappears—and which ideas might become strategies

There is no single cross-asset percentage. The strongest quantitative evidence retrieved is concentrated in crypto and MNQ futures, and it shows both outcomes: costs can erase a strategy’s edge, while a cost-aware decision rule can suppress enough weak trades to improve net performance. That improvement is not proof that the underlying signal is stable or deployable.

- In [Structural Limits of OHLCV-Based Intraday Momentum Signals in MNQ Futures](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.04004) (2026), the assumed round-trip friction was **2.0 MNQ points**. Eleven of 14 tested signal families had maximum gross return of just **0.07–1.50 points per trade**, below that hurdle. Three families cleared the cost hurdle but still failed other out-of-sample tests. This is a direct example of a gross pattern that is too small to trade under the study’s friction assumptions. [Gross Edge Ceiling](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.04004v3?page=12)

- In [Machine Learning-Based Bitcoin Trading Under Transaction Costs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00060) (2026), a **10 bp per unit of turnover** assumption changed the long-only XGBoost result from **73.50% annualized return with zero costs to −64.00%** under the paper’s costed baseline. A simple cost-aware forecast threshold then changed its long-only XGBoost example from **−64.00% and 10,619 trades** to **65.40% and 251 trades**. The method’s primary effect is to trade much less when the forecast is small relative to the assumed cost. It is important that this paper uses a flat proportional cost, not a queue, depth, variable-spread or funding model. [Naive Cost Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00060v1?page=16) [Filtered Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00060v1?page=18)

- In [AutoQuant](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476) (2025), one BTC perpetual stress test reported CAGR / Sharpe / maximum drawdown of **2.726 / 2.049 / 0.265** under fees, slippage and funding; **4.308 / 2.506 / 0.262** under fees only; and **5.225 / 2.711 / 0.260** with zero costs. These are decimal CAGRs in a single case study, and the authors explicitly say they are diagnostic, not scalable performance claims. Here costs reduce reported return and Sharpe materially, but the example’s drawdown changes much less. [Cost Ablation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.22476v1?page=45)

- For maker trading, [The Market Maker’s Dilemma](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625) reports that filled Binance BTC perpetual maker orders averaged about **−0.8 bp** over the short post-fill horizon, or about **−0.3 bp net of the assumed maker rebate**. Its simple balanced-inventory market-making example lost almost **60%** over approximately three days and seven hours, with an annualized Sharpe of **−109**. That is a warning against treating a maker rebate as guaranteed alpha: the fills themselves can be selected by adverse price movement. [Maker Markout](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=20) [Market Making Result](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.18625v2?page=22)

A few cost-aware ideas are reasonable **candidate strategies or gating signals**, but the literature here does not establish them as generally profitable:

1. **Trade only when forecast edge exceeds expected round-trip cost plus a risk buffer.** Use the forecast in the same units and horizon as the predicted cost; charge twice the relevant one-way cost when the position reverses, and include expected markout for passive fills. This is the most directly supported strategy-design idea: the BTC forecasting paper’s threshold reduced turnover sharply. A more robust variant should use a high cost quantile, not a mean, when the edge is small or the venue’s recent calibration is weak.

2. **Condition participation on execution state, not just “low spread.”** A low-spread period can also have poor depth, high adverse selection or bad latency. A sensible candidate is a state classifier using spread, depth, volatility, event time, queue imbalance and recent fill quality; trade only when the expected *net* value is positive. The MNQ study’s near-miss and positive-control findings suggest that multi-feature regime transitions and longer holds may be more promising than single-bar OHLCV triggers, but its positive-control results have stated selection and timing caveats. [MNQ Interpretation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.04004v3?page=12)

3. **Use maker-only execution selectively, with a fill-and-markout hurdle.** Require estimated probability of fill × expected post-fill value to outweigh the cost of missing, waiting, being adversely selected and later crossing the spread to complete the trade. The Binance study’s evidence argues against maker-only as a blanket low-cost setting; it is best treated as an order policy that the system can choose only in states where expected fill quality is favorable.

4. **Treat funding as a state variable, not just a backtest debit.** [Funding-Aware Optimal Market Making for Perpetual DEXs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.06405) makes inventory value and quote choice depend on the current funding state. It reports cross-asset simulations, but the result is under official-fill proxy calibrations, not a queue-position simulator; its funding diffusion also underrepresents jumps. So a funding signal is a plausible input to position sizing or quote skew—not yet evidence that simply buying or shorting based on a high funding rate is profitable. [Funding State](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.06405v1?page=10) [Execution Limits](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.06405v1?page=18)

5. **Make a strategy’s cost hurdle time-varying.** For an instrument with known event times, scheduled news can be used as a pre-event state; for rollover, funding and broker cutoffs, use the actual timestamp rules. These are model inputs and possible strategy gates—not a finding that “avoid news” or “trade rollover” has positive expected return.

### Operational rules I would add

- **Admit trades on expected net edge, not gross forecast.** Compare forecast value with expected spread, fees, slippage, impact, carry and expected adverse markout. Use the risk-relevant cost quantile for fragile states; don’t let a “low average cost” hide rollover, news or gap tails.
- **Size down when either the edge estimate or cost estimate is uncertain.** The forecast and the cost model both have estimation error. A thin own-fill sample should widen the cost distribution and reduce permissible size, not default to zero slippage.
- **Detect alpha decay separately from cost drift.** Keep one live residual for realized-versus-predicted strategy returns *before costs* (signal decay), and another for predicted-versus-realized execution costs (execution deterioration). Otherwise the system cannot distinguish a failing signal from a failing venue model.
- **Stress scenarios must change fills and trading decisions.** Widen spreads, worsen slippage, lower limit-fill rates and increase rejection/partial-fill rates; then rerun orders and the strategy path. Applying a flat extra deduction after the simulation misses the fact that higher costs can change whether a strategy trades at all.
- **Use a slippage kill switch on execution evidence.** If realized slippage, rejection rates or cost residuals breach calibrated bounds persistently—or a stop executes far outside its modeled gap distribution—pause new orders or cut size pending human review. A stop price is a trigger, not a guaranteed execution price.

The practical priority is therefore **not** to select a favorite universal cost formula. It is to combine auditable accounting, order-level execution evidence where obtainable, explicit financing/roll rules, and walk-forward net-edge gates—and to mark the data gaps clearly when the retail broker does not expose enough fill detail to estimate them.

## Turn 1 (topic question, model: Smart) - answer

The evidence points to a clear rule: **an approval gate helps only if it is outside the agent’s control, shows the complete effect, and authorizes exactly the version that later executes.** Papers report real security and process benefits from those properties—but also measurable delay, and little direct evidence that human approvers avoid fatigue or rubber-stamping at scale.

### What the gate experiments actually show

In [Approve What, Exactly?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21063), binding an approval to the exact effect blocked the tested post-approval substitutions and replays. For forged narration in AgentDojo, attack success fell from 0.54 with a narrated card to 0.03 with a trusted card for an approver that compared the card with the task. Automated approvers caught 0.30 of attacks from the agent’s narration versus 0.98 from a trusted card—but the trusted-card condition refused 16% of harmless near-misses, mostly because of artifacts in the test stimuli. These are simulated or model approvers, **not human-subject results**. The paper’s fatigue results are modeled too: flooding still succeeded 45–60% of the time against ASU with a modeled human approver. [Model Approvers](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.21063?page=1) [Flooding](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.21063?page=1)

[I-HITL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35835) supplies a complementary warning: an OTP is not independent approval if the agent can read the inbox that receives it. That study finds agents can use the apparent gate to escalate their own authority; it motivates a separate trusted approval channel rather than claiming that a simple confirmation prompt is protective. [Self Escalation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.35835?page=2)

[AllPath](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26662) gives the most direct gate-versus-delay comparison, but only for one operator and one deployment. Six human-gated strategy revisions took a median of **8.8 hours** to resolve; autonomous revisions applied in about **two minutes**. The human vetoed two of the six gated proposals, even though the mechanical checks passed them. In the autonomous period, the operator later removed 11 of 19 agent-added rules, at a median lag of eight days. The code gates rejected no proposals in the reported period. This is evidence that human review caught judgments the mechanical checks did not—and that it delayed the workflow—not proof of improved trading returns or of reviewer fatigue. [Latency and Vetoes](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26662?page=6) [Later Removals](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26662?page=7)

Financial-agent evaluations also show why approval alone is an inadequate safety test. [QuantHarness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26101) scores task execution, requirement fidelity, and runtime evidence; it reports **90.72% task success**, versus 74.23%–84.54% for comparison systems. [AStockArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00515) finds at least one audit gap at **72.99% of annotated trading points**. Its retrospective replay finds better mean returns after removing some flagged trades, but that does not establish that a live approval gate would produce those gains. [FinRelBench Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26101?page=1) [Audit Findings](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.00515?page=2)

### Design recommendations for your system

- **Keep proposal separate from authority.** Agents should submit versioned strategy proposals; only a distinct promotion service or human role can authorize live deployment. Give proposal agents no broker credentials and no tool that can change approval status.
- **Bind approval to the exact artifact and effect.** Show a complete, machine-generated diff against the current strategy, its base hash and version, affected instruments, risk limits, and intended order behavior. At promotion—and again at execution—reject or re-queue if any material field or underlying version has changed. A call ID or an “approved” status alone is not enough. [Use-Time Binding](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.21081?page=12)
- **Give approvers decision-grade evidence.** Include the data snapshot and timestamps, signal and order changes, cost-aware backtest results, drawdown and exposure effects, stress tests, validation failures, and what changed since the last approved version. Make stale evidence and unresolved risks prominent; don’t rely on the agent’s persuasive summary as the approval view.
- **Scope tools and permissions by stage.** Allow research agents read-only market/data access and sandboxed backtests. Restrict promotion, live orders, limits, and kill-switch control to separate services or principals. Enforce hard position, loss, notional, and aggregate portfolio limits outside the model, with a fast stop path that does not wait for inference. [FARSIGHT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19705) reports that the studied designs often lack such model-independent controls. [Controls Outside the Model](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19705?page=2)
- **Defend the data path, not just the prompt.** Record source, publication time, and provenance; corroborate market-moving claims across independent sources; treat retrieved text as untrusted data, never instructions; and quarantine or review agent-written memory before it can influence live strategy proposals. Add red-team cases for fake headlines, poisoned stored context, cross-agent propagation, and malicious changes between approval and execution.
- **Make actions auditable and replayable.** Preserve append-only records of the proposal, displayed diff, evidence packet, approval identity and scope, tool calls, policy decisions, fills, and broker/account reconciliation. For evaluation, replay identical decision tapes under different fill and cost assumptions; keep timestamps point-in-time; include realistic costs and execution constraints; test leakage and repeated strategy selection; and report false positives, exact audit accuracy, activity, and risk alongside returns. [Measurement Boundaries](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32379) finds that independently rerunning an agent can confound execution effects with changed model responses and portfolio feedback. [Fixed-Tape Findings](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.32379?page=1)
- **Design approval queues for sustained use.** Rate-limit requests, cap pending approvals, merge duplicates, expire stale requests, and prioritize by risk. Measure approval latency, queue age, rejection and override rates, and later reversals. Avoid broad “approve all” grants; if batching is necessary, bound it by strategy hash, scope, size, and expiry.

**Bottom line:** use human gates for judgment—strategy promotion is a good candidate—but make deterministic controls responsible for hard risk limits and exact action binding. The papers show gates can catch decisions machines’ rule checks miss, while adding delay; they do **not** yet establish that human approval improves financial outcomes at scale or quantify real-world rubber-stamping.

## Turn 2 (follow-up a: top three papers and an approval workflow for one operator, model: Smart) - answer

For your setup, the three most useful papers are **QuantHarness** for governing strategy development, **AllPath** for a trading-specific human-review queue, and **Approve What, Exactly?** for making approval resistant to bypass or substitution. Their evidence is complementary: QuantHarness measures development reliability, AllPath reports real queue delay and human vetoes, and Approve What, Exactly? red-teams approval mechanics. None establishes that human gates improve live trading returns at scale.

## 1. [Reliable Before Profitable: QuantHarness for Governing Financial Strategy Development with LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26101) (2026)

**Design, step by step.** The agent turns a request into a task contract, then a strategy specification, data bindings, an output protocol and an implementation plan. It builds and tests a candidate, then submits its results for review. The agent can recommend the next workflow step; a separate runtime, Harness Core, checks the required artifacts and decides whether that transition may be committed. It controls which context and tools each stage receives, and stores stage inputs, outputs, tool results, and lifecycle decisions. A candidate is frozen only after the acceptance transition. [Runtime Mediation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26101?page=5)

**What the reviewer sees and what is logged.** The design’s key review material is not just a performance summary: it links the frozen strategy to its specification, data binding, implementation and test evidence. Run artifacts retain rejected or superseded outputs as well as the accepted result, supporting audit and replay. But “review” here is a workflow/Judge mechanism—not a study of a human operator reviewing a promotion packet.

**Results and evaluation.** On its 97-task FinRelBench, QuantHarness reports **90.72% task success**, compared with 74.23%–84.54% for the comparison systems. The benchmark checks executability, requirement satisfaction and runtime evidence. Its protocol separates agent-visible material from evaluator-only settings and rubrics; the financial evaluator applies fixed execution rules and costs. The authors also report that structured run records improve evidence retrieval and support replay under their protocol. These are development-reliability results, not evidence of higher live returns or of human approval improving outcomes. [Benchmark Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26101?page=1) [Benchmark Protocol](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26101?page=6)

**Where it falls short.** FinRelBench is mainly explicit rule-based strategy-development tasks in A-share equities; it does not establish robustness across your FX, futures, crypto, or multi-strategy portfolio. It does not measure human approval latency, reviewer agreement, fatigue, or rubber-stamping. Its audit and replay support is a strong design pattern, but it is not a human-factors result.

**Related work that extends or challenges the evaluation.** [AStockArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00515) challenges the idea that strategy correctness alone is enough: it audits whether reported actions match orders and account changes, and whether rationales use contemporaneous evidence. [Measurement Boundaries in LLM Financial Agent Evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32379) challenges overly broad readings of benchmark scores: it argues for fixed-response replay, multi-defect tests, and reporting precision and false positives alongside recall.

## 2. [AllPath: Layered Verification for a Deployed, Self-Revising LLM Trading Agent under Human-Gated and Autonomous Regimes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26662) (2026)

**Design, step by step.** The agent can propose orders and strategy revisions but cannot directly execute orders, edit strategy files, or approve its own proposals. A separate nightly reflection agent receives a code-generated briefing and read-only tools; it has an iteration cap and can only propose strategy changes. Its proposed revision cannot change the rule’s authorization or status. Proposals enter a single queue. Deterministic checks run both when a proposal is submitted and when it is applied: the target must exist, the version must advance, the base file must still match, and protected fields must remain unchanged. For human review, the interface regenerates the diff against the current strategy, shows a stale-base warning when necessary, and displays current price and other order context. Strategy files are versioned; proposal and reflection records are retained in an append-only history, with replayable transcripts. [Review Interface](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26662?page=5) [Revision Loop](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26662?page=5)

**Results: benefits and delay.** In the human-gated period, the operator approved four of six reflection proposals and vetoed two, although code checks found no fault in either rejected proposal. The six decisions took a median of **8.8 hours** (range 11 minutes to 80.7 hours). In the following autonomous period, 56 reflection revisions passed the mechanical gates and were applied in about two minutes; the gates rejected none. The operator later removed 11 of the 19 rules the agent had added, at a median lag of eight days. This suggests that human judgment caught something those integrity checks did not, but it is not a controlled comparison of returns: the two periods were sequential, and the human was one operator who was also an author. [Queue Latency](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26662?page=6) [Rule Reversals](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26662?page=7)

**How it was evaluated, and where it fails.** This is a single-operator production case study on a live **paper** brokerage account, with a shadow account mirroring real holdings but not routing orders. It logs proposals, gate activations, latency and incidents. The paper does not measure fatigue or agreement across approvers; six reflection decisions and twelve queued stock orders are too few for broad estimates. It also documents important failures that passed the gates: strategies left in draft and unmonitored, and an order response that looked successful but lacked an order ID. The operator found them by hand; interface changes made those states visible. A well-formed proposal can still be wrong, and an approval card cannot compensate for missing or misleading operational state. [Production Incidents](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26662?page=7)

**Related work that challenges the gate assumption.** [Loopjacking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21081) shows why a correct diff at review time is not enough if mutable state can change before application: bind the decision to the complete operation and re-check it at use time. [I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35835) challenges the assumption that a gate is independent if the agent can access the approval channel; it reports agents retrieving email OTPs to raise their own spending caps. [Agent Approval Laundering: Transitive Effects Beyond the Approved Invocation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28586) adds another concern: an approved invocation can trigger downstream effects not apparent in the invocation itself.

## 3. [Approve What, Exactly? Effect Binding and Trusted Rendering for Agent Step-Up Authorization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21063) (2026)

**Design, step by step.** The agent proposes a tool action but does not get to redefine what the human approved. A gateway converts the call into a canonical effect descriptor, renders the approval card from that descriptor, and has a passkey sign its digest. Execution uses the stored descriptor—not a fresh, potentially altered agent submission. The design also calls for single-use, expiring approvals scoped to the approved arguments, rate limits and caps on pending approvals. The agent’s explanation, if shown, is marked untrusted and kept separate from the trusted rendering.

**Results and evaluation.** In **27,108 scripted trials** and **609 AgentDojo attack pairs**, the reference gateway blocked tested substitution, replay and grant-stretching attacks that unbound approval allowed. In AgentDojo forged-narration cases, attack success fell from **0.54** with a narrated card to **0.03** with a trusted card for an approver that compared the card with the task. With 15 language models acting as approvers, the attack catch rate rose from **0.30** on agent narration to **0.98** on a trusted card; the trusted-card condition also refused 16% of harmless near-misses, mostly due to artifacts in the test stimuli. A second device did not improve measured attack outcomes in these experiments. [Attack Evaluation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.21063?page=19) [Trusted Rendering](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.21063?page=21)

**Where it fails.** These were mock-resource, scripted and benchmark tests—not human-subject experiments. The paper’s “careless” and “fatigued” approvers are simulated, so it does not estimate actual human rubber-stamping. Flooding still succeeded **45–60%** of the time against the reference system with a modeled human approver. Trusted rendering cannot stop a person who approves without reading, a policy that already permits the action, effects hidden outside the descriptor, or a compromised trusted display. The authors do not establish that a long evidence packet will be read; their finding is closer to “render the material effect clearly” than “give the approver more text.” [Flooding Limit](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.21063?page=1) [Human Evidence Limit](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.21063?page=4)

**Related work that sharpens the threat model.** [I-HITL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35835) emphasizes that the approval channel itself must be outside the agent’s reach. [Loopjacking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21081) demonstrates effect-binding failures in released products. [Approval Laundering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38983) and [Agent Approval Laundering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28586) extend the concern from changed arguments to approval/workflow continuation and downstream effects. These papers make the same practical point from different angles: check the whole effect at the final execution boundary, not just the button click or tool-call label.

## A one-operator promotion workflow

Treat a strategy proposal as a **release candidate**, not as a permission to trade. The operator should decide whether to promote that exact, tested version; the runtime should enforce hard limits before, during, and after promotion.

**1. Agent submits a frozen proposal.** The agent may research, read approved data, run sandboxed backtests, and propose a strategy. It cannot promote, edit approval status, change portfolio-wide risk limits, access broker credentials, or submit live orders. Submission creates an immutable candidate ID and hash. The packet includes:

- a concise strategy thesis and explicit entry, exit, sizing, and instrument rules;
- a machine-readable diff from the last approved version, plus base version and hash;
- the data snapshot manifest: source, timestamps, universe, corporate-action treatment and checksums;
- backtest and validation results: costs, slippage, execution assumptions, turnover, drawdown, exposures and comparisons;
- point-in-time and leakage checks, including look-ahead checks and the chronological train/validation/test windows;
- stress and red-team results, known failure cases, and any unresolved warnings;
- the proposed deployment scope, expiry or review date, and rollback version.

Keep the summary short and decision-oriented; put detailed traces and charts behind links. The papers do **not** show that operators can reliably digest arbitrarily long packets, so do not make “more evidence” synonymous with “more pages.”

**2. Deterministic checks run before the proposal reaches the queue.** Reject or hold the candidate automatically if it is not reproducible from its frozen inputs; fails compilation or rule-contract tests; uses data later than its decision timestamp; has suspicious evaluation-window reuse; misses realistic transaction costs; violates configured per-strategy or portfolio limits; or changes protected permission fields. Check aggregate exposure across the whole strategy fleet, not only one agent at a time. Verify that the exact diff, evidence and test outputs are bound to the candidate hash, and that the approver sees a trusted rendering of that same object. Run a simulation/red-team suite for poisoned news or other untrusted inputs, changed state after approval, stale bases, and attempted permission escalation. These checks should be deterministic where possible; a second LLM reviewer is not a substitute for them.

**3. Operator decides the judgment questions.** Present a one-screen summary: what changed, why it is expected to work, what the evidence says after costs, the worst observed loss or exposure, and the most important failure modes. The operator chooses **promote**, **reject**, or **request changes**—not “approve this general strategy family.” Promotion is bound to the candidate hash, permitted instruments and account scope, configured risk envelope, and a limited canary period. If the candidate or evidence changes after review, invalidate approval and require a new decision. If it is not legible or the evidence is inadequate, defer it.

**4. Runtime enforces the promotion and watches for drift.** Promotion grants only the runtime a narrowly scoped capability to activate that immutable version. The live executor applies independent order, strategy and aggregate-portfolio limits; a fast kill switch must work without model inference. A material strategy update, data-source change, risk-limit change, stale approval or integrity failure returns the candidate to the queue or disables it. Agents can recommend changes to limits but cannot change them.

**5. Record the decision and make it replayable.** Append an audit record containing candidate and base hashes; evidence-manifest hash; automatic checks and versions; the exact approval screen rendered; approver identity, timestamp, scope, decision and any reason; subsequent runtime actions, fills, risk checks, and rollback events. Replay should reconstruct the evidence and approval view from the recorded snapshot, then verify that the deployed artifact and executed actions matched the approved hash and scope.

That workflow borrows QuantHarness’s stage boundaries and artifact trail, AllPath’s regenerated diffs and apply-time checks, and Approve What, Exactly?’s effect-bound trusted rendering. The practical split is: **the human judges whether the strategy merits promotion; code decides whether it is permitted to execute and whether it is still the strategy that was approved.**

## Turn 3 (follow-up b: class-specific checks, measured effect of gates, combinations, model: Smart) - answer

## 1. Asset classes: common gate, different evidence requirements

Keep **one approval and authorization architecture**, but make the packet and pre-queue checks asset-class aware. A strategy should not reach the operator until the system can reproduce its signals using only information available at the decision time, price its intended trading size under plausible execution conditions, and establish that the relevant data may be used for the proposed purpose.

One important boundary: the recent papers I found do **not** provide a validated checklist spanning FX, indices, metals, energy, crypto, futures, and single stocks. The checks below are operational recommendations, not class-by-class results established in those papers. [QuantHarness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26101) studies A-share strategy development under specified timing, cost and market constraints; [SwingGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12988) provides a crypto example with point-in-time input checks and execution at the next five-minute open. [Trading Constraints](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26101?page=46) [Crypto Protocol](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.12988?page=5)

| Asset class | Class-specific checks before queueing a packet |
|---|---|
| **FX** | Identify spot, forward, NDF, or futures exposure; encode the actual venue/broker calendar, time zone, daylight-saving changes, holidays, rollover/swap points, value dates, fixing windows and weekend gaps. Stress spreads, slippage and liquidity around session transitions and scheduled releases. |
| **Stock indices** | Specify cash index, ETF, or futures implementation; use point-in-time index membership and corporate-action/dividend treatment for proxies; check market hours, opening/closing auctions, halts, futures basis and expiry/roll. Do not treat an index level as a directly executable price. |
| **Metals** | Distinguish spot, ETF and futures exposure; include contract month, settlement and roll mechanics for futures, and financing/storage assumptions where relevant. Stress venue-specific spreads and depth in thin sessions or around event shocks. |
| **Energy** | Include contract month, delivery/expiry, roll and settlement details; handle price limits, contract-specific trading hours, and the possibility of unusual or negative prices where relevant. Stress around inventory releases and delivery periods; make sure continuous-series adjustments do not create tradable “prices” that never existed. |
| **Crypto** | Name exchange, instrument and custody path; include venue outages, 24/7 calendar, funding, borrow, liquidation rules, mark/index prices, spot–perpetual basis, maker/taker fees and any chain or withdrawal constraints. Check that risk controls use exchange/account data, not just agent-retrieved text. The [SwingGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12988) study’s risk controller governs positions on a five-minute cadence, but it is a BTC/ETH study, not evidence that this cadence is right for every crypto strategy. |
| **Futures** | Specify the exact contract and exchange, tick value, margin, expiry, first-notice/delivery constraints where applicable, settlement, price limits and roll schedule. Evaluate the executable contract series separately from any back-adjusted continuous series; show roll yield and roll costs. |
| **Single stocks** | Use point-in-time constituents and delisted names where possible; check splits, dividends, ticker changes, earnings and corporate-action timestamps, halts, borrow/locate availability and short-sale constraints. Estimate capacity against liquidity in the actual execution window, not daily average volume alone. |

For **every class**, require a cost model for the proposed venue and order style: commissions and exchange fees, spread, market impact, slippage, funding/roll/borrow costs as applicable, and partial-fill or latency assumptions. Show estimated participation and capacity at the proposed size, plus stressed liquidity and exit capacity. [Measurement Boundaries](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32379) shows why performance comparisons can change when execution assumptions change: its fixed-response replay estimated a return decrease of 0.017 under its stressed execution setting, while independent runs confounded execution with changed model responses. That number is specific to its synthetic setup, not a universal haircut. [Execution Replay](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.32379?page=1)

Make **data-licence clearance a hard pre-queue check**: record source, permitted use, retention/display restrictions and whether the packet may include raw data or only derived summaries. A human approval must not become an accidental redistribution channel for licensed material. The recent trading-agent papers do not report empirical results on data licensing; this is a governance requirement rather than a measured paper finding.

## 2. Performance, promotion delay, fatigue and packet size

The evidence is uneven. There is one small trading deployment with measured human queue times, some controlled results for **automated risk governance** rather than human approvals, and no robust estimate of the return benefit of human promotion gates across asset classes.

- **Human-gated strategy revisions: [AllPath](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26662).** Six reflection proposals were reviewed: four approved and two vetoed. The median resolution time was **8.8 hours**, with a range from 11 minutes to 80.7 hours. In the later autonomous period, 56 revisions applied in about two minutes; the operator subsequently removed 11 of the 19 agent-added rules, at a median lag of eight days. This is one operator, sequential periods, a paper-brokerage account and a small sample—not a causal estimate of returns gained or losses avoided. The human did catch disagreements the mechanical checks did not; the paper does not show that the vetoes improved P&L. [Latency and Vetoes](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26662?page=6) [Later Removals](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.26662?page=7)

- **Guardrails that changed returns: [SwingGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12988).** This is not a human approval-gate study. It compares a crypto strategy whose risk context influences proposal formation and whose controller governs exposure every five minutes with post-hoc risk control and with no risk controller. In its six-month BTC/ETH evaluation, the joint portfolio reported Sharpe **2.98**, return **37.16%**, and maximum drawdown **−6.44%** for full SwingGuard; post-hoc risk reported **2.79**, **35.03%**, and **−8.62%**; no risk controller reported **1.87**, **23.02%**, and **−14.19%**. Those are results from one particular simulated/backtest protocol and system, not a forecast of gains from adding approval gates. [Ablation Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.12988?page=7)

- **Promotion evidence rather than approval evidence: [Can We Stop the Backtest Yet?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03384).** This new paper tests an anytime-valid, cost-aware statistical promotion rule for adaptive candidate generation. In its simulation, repeated unadjusted tests falsely promoted at least one null candidate on **94.5%** of 3,000 paths, versus **0% observed** for Promote; under adaptive candidate births, the funded rule had a **1.77%** false-family crossing rate. But power came with delay: for a 15-basis-point injected effect, power was 70.1% and median promotion was session 2,327 of 2,868. Its market replay promoted **zero of 24** candidates. This is a statistical release gate, not a human approval study, and the paper explicitly says its certificate does not establish that a historical edge remains profitable at deployment. [False Promotion Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.03384?page=1) [Promotion Delay](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.03384?page=5)

- **Fatigue and rubber-stamping.** I found **no 2025–2026 human-subject evidence** measuring the effect of approval queues on real traders’ fatigue, rubber-stamping, or vigilance. [Approve What, Exactly?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21063) models careless and fatigue-sensitive approvers and finds that flooding can still succeed in its modeled setup, but it explicitly reports no human-subjects data. Its language-model approvers are not proxies that establish how a real operator will behave. [Approver Limitation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.21063?page=4)

- **Best packet size.** No paper establishes an optimal page, word, or evidence-item count, nor tests whether operators read long packets. The defensible design choice is a short, fixed summary of decision-critical facts with expandable, hash-bound supporting evidence—not an empirically optimized packet length. Measure your own operator’s review time, missed seeded defects, deferrals and reversals before making claims about fatigue or packet effectiveness.

## 3. Useful combinations for your approval system

These are proposed combinations, not systems whose combined results have already been demonstrated.

1. **[QuantHarness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26101) + [What survives honest evaluation?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27734) + [Can We Stop the Backtest Yet?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03384): governed development, complete search accounting, and an anytime promotion rule.** QuantHarness supplies stage-specific permissions and durable run artifacts. What survives honest evaluation supplies a structural leakage-safe tool registry and a search ledger indexed to every trial; it also demonstrates that statistical deflation alone does not catch leakage. Promote adds lifetime testing-wealth allocation and promotion at a valid sequential crossing. Together: freeze the candidate and its data/cost definition at birth; log every variant, including rejected and abandoned candidates; block look-ahead structurally; require point-in-time and cost checks; then let the sequential test decide whether evidence crosses the declared bar. Only after that does the operator review the exact candidate and decide deployment scope. This helps prevent “best of many backtests” from being presented as a single hypothesis. The tradeoff is real: Promote’s detection can take a long time, and its market replay promoted no candidates, so pair it with a clearly labeled canary/forward-test stage rather than interpreting non-promotion as proof of no edge. [Leakage and Search](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.27734?page=1) [Ledger Protocol](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.03384?page=3)

2. **[AllPath](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26662) + [Approve What, Exactly?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21063) + [Loopjacking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21081): human queue with exact effect binding.** AllPath provides versioned strategy files, regenerated diffs, and apply-time integrity checks. Approve What, Exactly? contributes canonical effect descriptors and trusted rendering; Loopjacking supplies the attack test: can the action change after the approval view? Combined flow: render the diff from the sealed candidate hash; bind operator approval to that hash, account, instruments, risk envelope and expiry; reconstruct and compare the deployed artifact at commit time; reject and re-queue any mismatch. This directly addresses proposal-versus-authority separation and stale or substituted state. It does **not** prove the operator reads the card; keep queue caps, deduplicate alerts, and measure review time and seeded-error detection locally. [Use-Time Binding](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.21081?page=12)

3. **[AllPath](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26662) + [SwingGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12988) + [FARSIGHT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19705): promotion gate plus continuous risk governance and independent kill path.** AllPath gates strategy revisions; SwingGuard separates slower strategy generation from faster risk-state updates; FARSIGHT argues for a model-independent fast path, hard loss limits and kill switches. Combined flow: approve a strategy version once within a limited scope, but require deterministic per-order, per-strategy and aggregate portfolio checks on every action. A separate risk service monitors exposure, loss, liquidity and market shocks; it can freeze new orders or cancel resting orders without asking the strategy agent. After a halt, recovery is staged and requires new checks rather than letting the strategy agent self-reactivate. The empirical anchor is FARSIGHT’s simulation: with 400 agents, its fast path and hard limits removed modeled crash effects; that is market-simulation evidence, not a measured effect in live markets. [Kill Switch Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19705?page=3)

4. **[Approve What, Exactly?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21063) + [Contagion on the Trading Floor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19789) + [AStockArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00515): action authorization plus input-contagion and decision-process testing.** The first tests whether the human approved the exact effect. GMATS tests whether admissible poisoned social evidence shifts analyst and coordinator beliefs; AStockArena audits whether reports, orders, fills, account states and cited decision-time evidence agree. Combined pre-promotion red team: inject controlled fake or conflicting inputs at different rates; record which agents consume them and how they propagate; audit intended action against submitted order, fill and account state; test whether provenance checks and independent-source corroboration stop or damp the effect. GMATS found that a wide coordinator damped some attacks relative to a single-agent topology, but its setup is narrow—AAPL, offline social data, and scripted, non-adaptive attackers—so don’t interpret its results as proof of poisoning resistance across assets. [Poisoning Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19789?page=10)

For your multi-strategy system, I would combine **1 + 2 + 3** as the promotion backbone, then use **4** as a recurring pre-promotion red-team suite. Keep the distinction clear: a statistical promotion test asks whether evidence is strong enough; the operator decides whether this exact version is acceptable to run; the independent runtime enforces whether each proposed action remains inside the approved authority and portfolio risk limits.

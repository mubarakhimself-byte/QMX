## What the results say about “safe in backtest, unsafe in simulation”

The strongest evidence is for **false safety under replay or fixed-impact assumptions**:

- **Fixed historical replay cannot respond to your orders.** A strategy’s simulated fills and risk may look benign because the background tape is unchanged after the strategy trades. The 2021 GAN/ABIDES work demonstrates why a reactive “world agent” matters; the 2026 impact benchmark shows that even learned generators can still get the response badly wrong.
- **A fixed impact curve can miss opponent adaptation.** ABIDES-MARL reports that schedules optimized against exogenous impact can underperform once market makers change quotes strategically.
- **Stops can become synchronized market orders.** FARSIGHT’s agents sold entire positions on a common shock; price-triggered selling amplified a downturn. A stop-loss that limits an isolated strategy’s loss may therefore worsen portfolio-level execution if many strategies hit it together.
- **The reverse result is less established.** The papers reviewed do not show a general case where a risk rule that looked unsafe in backtests became safe in multi-agent simulation. CrashGym does show that controlled experiments can improve intervention choices in its synthetic setup, but that is not the same claim. Treat such a “reverse” result as something your own paired experiments must test.

## How to inject your strategies and kill switches

Use the **production strategy code with a simulated broker/exchange adapter**, so orders, cancellations, fills, fees and positions follow the same interface as live trading. In ABIDES, a strategy is an agent communicating with an exchange over the simulator’s message kernel; its trading-agent layer tracks portfolio and open orders. Put your risk rules in a separate risk-service or order-gateway layer, not inside the strategy agent. [ABIDES Architecture](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1904.12066v1?page=7)

Model each scalper or intraday strategy as its own agent, including its real wake-up schedule, latency, order sizing, inventory logic, and cancellation behavior. Add a **shared portfolio-risk agent** that sees consolidated exposures and can reject orders, cancel working orders, freeze strategies, or switch them to reduce-only. Test the system at both levels: a rule that protects one strategy may still create a correlated liquidation across the portfolio.

For kill switches, make the control path deterministic and independent of model inference. Simulate triggers on measurable conditions such as loss limits, net/gross exposure, order-rate bursts, stale data, missing heartbeats, abnormal spread/depth, execution slippage, or cross-asset drawdown. Test the *action on trigger* too: pause and cancel, reduce-only, or orderly liquidation. Do not assume “liquidate immediately” is always safer in a thin book; compare it with freezing new risk and staged unwind. Record trigger delay, unfilled orders, residual exposure, slippage and false halts.

Because your system spans FX, indices, metals, energy, crypto, futures and stocks, use venue-appropriate trading sessions, tick sizes, contract rolls, funding/financing and margin rules. Couple the markets through shared portfolio limits and common stress factors, but don’t assume a single equity LOB simulator validates those other venues.

## Stress tests and acceptance criteria

Build scenarios that combine **herding, liquidity withdrawal and forced selling**, rather than testing each rule only against isolated shocks:

- Put many strategies on the same signal, news feed, model version or price threshold; vary how synchronized their decision times are.
- Have market makers widen, reduce depth or cancel quotes as volatility and inventory stress rise.
- Add leverage and margin calls so price declines cause forced sales, which further reduce liquidity.
- Test gaps, halts, reopenings, stale prices, delayed/corrupted feeds, execution latency and cross-asset contagion.
- Run paired seeds with and without a strategy or kill switch, and vary the shock, market depth, background-agent mix and trigger thresholds. Include generated tail scenarios as well as historical-day initializations.

Measure more than P&L: maximum drawdown, expected shortfall, loss concentration, impact and recovery, fill quality, residual exposure after a halt, time to trigger, missed triggers, and false halts. Require two distinct validation gates: **stylized-fact checks**—return tails, volatility clustering, spread/depth and volume relationships—and **closed-loop intervention checks**—impact scaling, liquidity replenishment, recovery after order flow stops, and the effect of your own orders. The recent papers support a demanding standard: matching ordinary market statistics is necessary, but not sufficient evidence that risk rules will hold during a cascade.

### Calibration and realism: what the numbers do—and don’t—establish

**ABIDES is the best integration starting point, but its realism must be earned through calibration.** It supplies exchange mechanics, agents and latency modeling; it does not automatically calibrate a realistic population for FX, crypto, futures or retail flow. The ABIDES realism audit compared two configurations: 100 zero-intelligence agents and a mixed configuration of 1 market maker, 50 zero-intelligence, 25 heuristic belief-learning and 24 momentum agents. The mixed population was closer to historical data on some measures, but both configurations missed important facts: neither captured the return-autocorrelation pattern well, neither matched the volume–volatility correlation distribution, and the mixed configuration had an inverted intraday volume pattern and poor order-interarrival fit. That audit compared 2011 US equities for return measures and JPM order-book data from June 2019 for order-flow measures—not your cross-venue universe. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="1912.04941v1" title="ABIDES Configurations" page="7" first="In this paper we present" last="and momentum trading agents)."> <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="1912.04941v1" title="Stylized Facts" page="7" first="We confirm experimentally that" last="closer to historical data (see Figure 2)."> <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="1912.04941v1" title="Model Gaps" page="8" first="The simulation data for" last="resulting in a poor fit to the Weibull distribution.">

**ABIDES-MARL tests a structural failure mode rather than crisis realism.** Its contribution is to let liquidity respond strategically, and its execution results show that a fixed impact assumption can mislead. It does not demonstrate reproduction of historical flash crashes or a broad suite of stylized facts. Use it as an adversarial opponent-adaptation test, not as your only market generator.

**CrashGym is a controlled cascade laboratory, not a calibrated market replica.** It can turn stress channels on and off and score choices against counterfactual outcomes, but it uses one synthetic asset, fixed construction rules and two intervention designs. Its authors explicitly limit transfer claims to that construction; utility is not calibrated to money. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2610.29245v1" title="Limitations" page="11" first="The evidence covers one" last="not calibrated to monetary outcomes.">

There is also a newer challenge for *all* learned generators: [**LOB-Impact Benchmark: Emergent Market Impact in Generative Limit Order Book Models** (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27100) injects metaorders into five neural generators across five NASDAQ stocks. It reports impact exponents of 0.63–1.21, compared with the roughly 0.5 square-root reference, and no credible post-execution relaxation. Its point is practical: a simulator can look convincing on generated-data statistics yet teach your strategies the wrong execution-risk response. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2610.27100v1" title="Impact Results" page="5" first="On every stock the" last="and it does not relax.">

## Concrete test plan for your system

Use **ABIDES as the host for your production strategy agents and portfolio controls**, then run selected stress cases in ABIDES-MARL and CrashGym. The risk layer should be a distinct simulated component between your strategies and the exchange: it sees consolidated positions and working orders, can reject new orders, cancel resting orders, freeze strategies or enter reduce-only mode. ABIDES’ message-based exchange and portfolio/order tracking make it a reasonable base for this integration. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="1904.12066v1" title="Order Management" page="7" first="The base trading agent" last="end of the day.">

1. **Port strategies without changing their decision logic.** Put each production strategy behind an adapter for the simulator’s market data, order, cancel and fill interfaces. Reproduce its cadence, sizing, latency, venue rules and portfolio constraints. First verify the adapter against known order traces and simple deterministic cases; don’t tune risk limits until order and fill behavior is correct.

2. **Create calibrated and deliberately adverse background populations.** For each venue/instrument, fit order arrivals, size, spread, depth, cancellations, intraday/session patterns and volatility to held-out real data. Keep separate populations for market makers, noise/flow traders, momentum or trend traders, and fundamental/value traders. Then add stress populations with shared signals, common thresholds or common model versions. ABIDES’ classic mixed population is a useful starting point, but its documented stylized-fact gaps mean it should not be treated as a default calibration. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="1912.04941v1" title="Calibration Gaps" page="3" first="Agent modeling typically relies" last="simulator calibration.">

3. **Run paired scenarios with common random seeds.** For every baseline path, replay the same initial conditions and random streams with: (a) your full strategy book and controls; (b) controls disabled; and (c) one control disabled at a time. Sweep strategy participation, quote depth, latency, and trigger levels. This isolates whether a rule changed the outcome rather than merely producing a different random path.

4. **Exercise three required stress families.**
   - **Herding:** synchronize strategies on the same price, volatility, news or drawdown trigger; vary how many respond and how tightly their actions cluster in time.
   - **Liquidity withdrawal:** reduce depth, widen spreads, increase cancellations, and delay replenishment as volatility or inventory stress increases.
   - **Cascades:** combine an initial shock with price-triggered selling, leverage/margin liquidation and cross-asset moves. Test both exogenous shocks and self-generated ones. CrashGym’s switchable impact/withdrawal/liquidation channels are a good template for controlled ablations, while its single-asset setup needs extension for your portfolio. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2610.29245v1" title="Amplification Stages" page="5" first="Mechanisms are enabled cumulatively" last="the configuration of every target.">

5. **Test the *response* of each control, not just its threshold.** For each drawdown cap, stop, exposure limit and kill switch, record trigger time, order-cancel latency, residual open orders, residual exposure, executions and slippage. Compare “freeze/cancel,” “reduce-only,” and staged liquidation; immediate market liquidation can worsen stress if multiple strategies fire together. The external fast-path control should not depend on the strategy agent or a model inference call.

6. **Report explicit rule-level failures.** Predefine your production limits, then label a rule as failed if, for example, portfolio drawdown exceeds its cap before the rule acts; the kill switch does not cancel or block orders within its latency budget; exposure exceeds the hard limit; a halted strategy re-enters without authorization; or an unwind violates its slippage/loss budget. Report outcomes by scenario and asset—drawdown and expected shortfall, time-to-trigger, fill quality, worst residual exposure and false-halt rate—rather than a single blended Sharpe or P&L.

Finally, treat market realism as two separate gates: **does the simulator reproduce ordinary stylized facts on held-out data, and does it respond plausibly to interventions?** Check returns and volatility clustering, spreads, depth, order sizes and arrival/cancellation patterns; separately inject your own orders and verify impact, replenishment, and post-shock recovery. The published results make the reason clear: stylized-fact resemblance alone did not ensure correct impact or relaxation in later generative LOB tests. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2610.27100v1" title="Impact Benchmark" page="1" first="Existing metrics assess generated" last="largely unexplored.">

For FX, crypto, futures, metals, energy and equities, calibrate each venue separately before coupling them through portfolio limits and cross-asset shocks. The three picks above are largely equity/LOB-oriented; they do not establish calibration to retail FX or crypto venues, and market hours, contract specifications, funding, margin and liquidity behavior will need venue-specific models.

But it does **not** model agent wealth, dynamic order sizing or a liquidation engine, and uses a generated spot signal rather than calibration to a live multi-venue crypto crisis. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2501.09404v1" title="Perpetual Futures Results" page="20" first="The simulation bears out" last="another empirical phenomenon which is observed empirically."> |
| **Single stocks** | Model per-symbol books, venue sessions and auctions, halts/reopenings, corporate actions, earnings and news shocks, plus cross-stock and sector correlation. Test name-specific depth and order flow rather than applying one “equity” calibration everywhere. | ABIDES is the clearest fit for custom single-stock agents and NASDAQ-like price-time matching. EvoMarket adds explicit auctions, daily price bands and cross-day handling, demonstrated on China A-shares. Neither paper establishes validated earnings/news arrivals and halt/reopening behavior across US single stocks. |

So the current credible core is **equity-style order-book mechanics and intervention experiments**, plus narrower demonstrations for **A-share institutional rules** and **a stylized perpetual-futures peg**. FX last look, energy inventory shocks, futures rolls, and crypto liquidation cascades require purpose-built venue modules and calibration.

## 2. Performance: interactive markets can change the answer

The classic replay-versus-interaction comparison is [**How to Evaluate Trading Strategies: Single Agent Market Replay or Multiple Agent Interactive Simulation?** (2019)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.12010). In a replay experiment, an injected large order caused a price spike that eventually converged back toward the historical baseline. In its interactive-agent experiment, the order changed the book and the behavior of background agents; prices settled at a new level rather than returning to baseline. The tests used 100 paired trials with 100 zero-intelligence agents and a single equity. The authors’ interpretation is that an order can change agents’ holdings, valuations and future actions, so replay can miss persistent feedback. This demonstrates an impact mechanism, **not** a general measured reduction in a strategy’s Sharpe or profit. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="1906.12010v1" title="Interactive Impact" page="8" first="In the IABS experiments, however," last="different equilibrium that could not be realized in the backtest environment.">

[**ABIDES-MARL**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02016) makes the strategy consequence explicit for optimal execution: an analytical schedule optimized for fixed, exogenous impact underperformed once market-maker liquidity adapted strategically. Under constrained opponents, TWAP was the most robust baseline; under more expressive opponents, that robustness weakened, and opponents could adapt against the jointly trained execution policy. That is a concrete warning that a schedule judged optimal under a fixed impact curve may not remain optimal in an interactive market. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2511.02016v2" title="Execution Results" page="21" first="The analytical schedule under-performs." last="once liquidity is endogenous."> <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2511.02016v2" title="Nonlinear Opponents" page="22" first="(iv) TWAP loses robustness." last="to strategic adaptation.">

The lesson is not simply “agent-based is more realistic.” A later challenge, [**LOB-Impact Benchmark** (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27100), injected metaorders into five neural limit-order-book generators across five NASDAQ stocks. Their estimated impact exponents were **0.63–1.21**, versus the roughly **0.5** square-root reference, and the models did not show credible post-execution relaxation. A generator can therefore match ordinary data distributions but still make your order impact too steep or too persistent—the exact mistake that can alter strategy ranking or make an unwind rule appear safer than it is. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2610.27100v1" title="Impact Results" page="5" first="On every stock the" last="and it does not relax.">

There is also evidence that **performance can change even without a causal claim about impact**. [**Can AI Make Money in Crypto? Measuring the Gap from Backtests to Real Markets** (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34510) evaluated 32 ML, RL, LLM and agent methods through historical backtests, prospective OKX paper trading and a short live-trading period. Among selected methods, the median return for ML moved from **+36.0%** in the 2025 backtest to **−9.8%** in paper trading; for non-ML methods it moved from **−29.9%** to **+13.2%**. In same-period backtest-versus-paper comparisons, the average absolute cumulative-return gap was **6.25 percentage points**, alongside differences in drawdown, Sharpe and volatility. The gap did not track any one execution measure cleanly: across the tested methods, its correlation with latency was about **0.05**. Live trading further narrowed the sample to four methods; none beat Bitcoin buy-and-hold during that period, and the average execution-price discrepancy rose from about **0.31‰** in paper trading to **1.86‰** live. These are a staged-evaluation result in crypto—not proof that market impact caused the reversals or that the sample predicts other regimes. <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2609.34510v1" title="Paper Trading Reversal" page="6" first="These results reveal a markedly" last="from −29.9% to 13.2%."> <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2609.34510v1" title="Performance Gap" page="7" first="We first observe substantial discrepancies" last="risk characteristics of a trading method."> <alphaxiv-paper-citation paper="2609.34510v1" title="Live Results" page="8" first="Only one of the four" last="once deployed with real capital.">

A useful *trading* insight from the perpetual-futures model is the mechanism of basis/funding trading: long positional traders and short basis traders can produce a perp premium while funding incentives help keep perp prices linked to spot. That is a candidate mechanism to test against real venue data, **not** a guaranteed profitable carry trade; fees, funding variability, liquidation and basis convergence risk must be included before evaluating it.

## 3. Combinations worth building

The strongest bench would combine a **venue-faithful exchange, interactive strategy agents, controllable stress scenarios and an external risk-control/promotion layer**. These combinations are complementary; some components are papers with demonstrated simulators, others are designs I recommend joining.

### Promotion and pass/fail protocol

Keep strategy **proposal**, **simulation**, **approval**, and **live promotion** as separate states. For each candidate strategy and each proposed change to a risk rule:

1. **Freeze the artifacts:** strategy version, model/prompt version if applicable, input snapshot, venue adapter, cost assumptions, risk limits and random-seed set. Use historical data for calibration and a held-out period for realism checks; do not tune to the stress outcomes used for final evaluation.
2. **Run a three-way paired comparison:** strategy plus full controls; the same strategy with the control under test disabled; and a baseline portfolio. Use common initial states and paired randomness where the simulator supports it.
3. **Inject portfolio-level crowding:** synchronize a growing share of strategies on common signals and thresholds. Then sweep depth withdrawal, spread widening, cancellation bursts, gaps and cross-asset shocks; add funding/margin liquidation for perps and venue-specific event shocks where modeled.
4. **Test the *whole kill-switch lifecycle*:** trigger, block new risk, cancel working orders, set reduce-only or hold, alert the human, and require an explicit re-enable condition or approval. Test stale data, unavailable risk service, duplicate triggers, partial fills, restarts and accidental re-entry. A stop that fires but leaves orders working is a failed control.
5. **Report rule-level outcomes, not just portfolio return:** max drawdown and expected shortfall; trigger time and trigger misses; exposures before/after intervention; unfilled orders; liquidation and unwind slippage; false halts; recovery time; and strategy P&L net of venue-specific fees, funding and execution costs. State how many scenarios breached each predeclared limit.
6. **Promote only after two gates:** (a) market validation—stylized facts, session/venue mechanics and intervention response against held-out data; (b) risk validation—the strategy and controls meet precommitted limits across base, stressed and model-variant runs. Require human approval for live promotion; keep mechanical exposure and order gates outside the strategy/LLM, and make kill and re-enable paths auditable.

That staging follows the evidence: interactive market response can change impact and execution rankings; learned simulators can still misrepresent impact; and crypto results can reverse between historical backtest, prospective paper and live execution. None of those findings supports trusting a single simulator—or a single performance metric—as the promotion decision.

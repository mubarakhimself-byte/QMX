## Turn 1 (question with both papers attached via @, model: Smart) - answer

**Neither paper is a live risk-and-sizing layer.** [AlphaAgent](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789) mines predictive factors; [MASS](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278) turns simulated investors’ stock selections into a portfolio. Both can contribute signals or candidate strategies to your human-promotion workflow, but neither supplies the independent, hard position limits, exits, and loss controls I’d put between a strategy and live capital.

## AlphaAgent: generate and test formulaic signals

**Method, step by step.** A human or research process supplies an initial market idea. The *idea agent* turns it into a hypothesis; the *factor agent* translates that hypothesis into candidate formulas using a library of financial operators; and the *evaluation agent* checks execution and numerical stability, backtests candidates, and feeds performance and failure feedback into the next round. Candidate formulas are represented as syntax trees. The system favors formulas that are structurally distinct from an existing factor library, consistent with their stated hypothesis, and not unnecessarily complex. In the paper’s implementation, new factors are combined with four base factors and fed into a LightGBM model to predict next-day stock returns. [Workflow](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=2)[Factor Controls](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=4)[Backtest Setup](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=6)

**Inputs and outputs.** The tested factor inputs are daily stock OHLCV data—open, high, low, close, and volume. The broader method can start from hypotheses about candlestick patterns, fundamentals, or market microstructure, but those are not all tested as inputs in this study. Its output is a set of formulaic signals, then stock return predictions and a ranked portfolio—not a complete trading instruction with an entry, stop, and exit rule. [Hypotheses](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=3)[Test Inputs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=6)

**What could become strategies?** The paper suggests translating ideas such as breakouts, candlestick patterns, and value–momentum relationships into parsimonious formulas. With the tested OHLCV inputs, plausible *research directions* include price-range or breakout behavior, return momentum or reversal, and volume-relative-to-price activity. These are not presented as turnkey scalping signals: the evaluated target is next-day return, and the paper does not provide a validated intraday entry/exit playbook. [Formula Construction](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=4)[Prediction Target](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=3)

**Reported evaluation.** The paper reports annualized *excess* return of **11.00%** on CSI 500 and **8.74%** on S&P 500, with information ratios of **1.488** and **1.0545** and maximum drawdowns of **9.36%** and **9.10%**, respectively. The paper prints drawdowns as negative returns. Its setup uses 2015–2019 for training, 2020 for validation, and 2021–2024 for testing; it reports transaction fees, but not a market-impact or slippage model. The portfolio ranks stocks, holds the top 50, and excludes the five lowest-ranked names; the paper does not specify a general sizing or exit policy suitable for your system. [Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=7)[Splits](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=6)[Portfolio Setup](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=6)

**Risk-layer value and gaps.** Useful pieces are formula simplicity, executable/numerical checks, duplicate-signal screening, and feedback-based research iteration. Those are candidate-admission controls—not account risk controls. The paper does not report stop-loss or take-profit rules, volatility-scaled sizing, per-symbol or correlated-exposure caps, intraday monitoring, or a kill switch. ## MASS: aggregate investor views into a stock-ranking portfolio

**Method, step by step.** MASS initializes investor types with different styles and data access, plus multiple agents per type that each see a limited stock pool. Each type reads current macro data to formulate a strategy; each individual agent then selects stocks from its assigned pool. The system aggregates selections by type into a weighted **consensus** and **disagreement** score for each stock. At the end of each day, simulated annealing adjusts the weights on investor types to maximize recent historical signal–return rank correlation over a five-day lookback. The resulting score ranks stocks for the portfolio. [Agent Simulation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=4)[Aggregation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=5)[Weight Update](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=5)

The core signal is:

$$
\mathrm{Signal}(s,j)=\alpha\,m_s(j)-(1-\alpha)\,\sigma_s(j)
$$

Here, $m_s$ is weighted agreement among investor types and $\sigma_s$ is their weighted disagreement. MASS rewards agreement and penalizes disagreement; its experiment uses a lower agreement weight for ChiNext 100 than for the other two pools. [Consensus Signal](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=5)[Hyperparameters](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=7)

**Inputs and outputs; strategy ideas.** Inputs include stock price–volume features, fundamentals, news and financial reports, industry information, macroeconomic indicators, historical returns, and agent-style descriptions. The output is a stock-selection score and a daily portfolio, rather than a reusable symbolic formula. The investable idea is **consensus versus disagreement**: rank stocks by agreement across differently informed investor styles, while testing whether disagreement predicts underperformance. The agent styles also suggest research hypotheses around valuation, risk appetite, industry preference, market capitalization, and holding horizon. But those descriptions do not automatically become measurable or validated short-horizon rules. [Data Inputs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=16)[Investor Styles](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=16)

**Reported evaluation.** The main test is on Chinese A-share indices through 2023. The backtest rebalances **weekly**, excludes limit-up/limit-down stocks at the stated execution point, equally weights the top 20% of ranked stocks, and charges a **0.1% round-trip cost**. Reported 2023 annualized returns are **2.16%** for SSE 50, **4.95%** for CSI 300, and **1.17%** for ChiNext 100; maximum drawdowns are **11.98%**, **14.04%**, and **19.06%**. A separate Q1 2025 experiment on SSE 50, CSI 300, and the new CSI A500 pool is offered to address concern that the LLM may have memorized the 2023 market; it reports positive returns and lower drawdowns, but is a short additional period rather than a broad multi-year, multi-asset walk-forward. [Backtest Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=20)[Execution](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=20)[Leakage Test](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=7)

**Risk-layer value and gaps.** MASS’s disagreement penalty is a signal-level risk idea, and its portfolio test gives a concrete weekly equal-weight construction. Neither is equivalent to sizing positions against portfolio volatility or enforcing hard exposure and loss limits. The paper does not supply stops, per-trade sizing, leverage controls, intraday exits, or an external halt mechanism. Weekly rebalancing on Chinese stocks is a poor direct match for around-the-clock, short-horizon trading across FX, indices, metals, energy, crypto, futures, and stocks. The paper also excludes a next-day label that depends on the first 15 minutes of trading, a useful timing safeguard; however, evidence is concentrated in Chinese equities and the evaluation horizon is short. [Label Timing](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=6)[Rebalance Rule](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=20)

## What I’d borrow for your system

Use AlphaAgent as a **human-reviewed signal-generation and validation module**: hypotheses become constrained candidate formulas; formula similarity, complexity, and execution checks help prioritize what a researcher inspects. Treat MASS as a **portfolio-ranking or strategy-diversification research idea**: agent-style views can produce consensus/disagreement signals, but test whether those signals add value for each asset and horizon rather than transplanting its weekly stock-selection procedure.

For both, put independent controls after signal generation and before any promotion: position and exposure limits, strategy-specific sizing, explicit exit rules, drawdown and loss limits, execution-cost tests, and live monitoring with a halt path outside the LLM. That boundary matches your human-promotion design: agents can propose and test candidates, but cannot authorize capital or override risk controls.

## Newer work that builds on or challenges them

- **AlphaAgent:** [QuantaAlpha](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.07085) takes a trajectory-level evolutionary approach and reports stronger results than AlphaAgent in its own CSI 300 comparison—an alternative to refining factors one candidate at a time. [FactorBench](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.06947) evaluates factors from multiple mining paradigms under a shared portfolio-aware protocol and finds that no paradigm consistently dominates, challenging simple rankings from different bespoke backtests. [AlphaEval](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.13174) broadens factor screening beyond backtests and correlation to stability, robustness, logic, and diversity.
- **MASS:** [Hierarchical AI Multi-Agent Fundamental Investing](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2510.21147) explicitly identifies lack of risk scaling in MASS-style portfolio construction as a gap and adds a risk-control agent. [PortBench](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.27887) challenges the narrow equity-only evaluation common in LLM portfolio work by testing cross-asset correlations and stress periods. [ContestTrade](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.00554) includes MASS among its comparisons for short-term, multi-stock trading, making it relevant to the distinction between MASS’s portfolio-selection idea and a more short-horizon trading system.

## Turn 2 (follow-up a: liftable components, asset classes, growth vs protection, model: Smart) - answer

The main distinction is **factor discovery versus portfolio aggregation**. [AlphaAgent](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789) offers a useful *admission and search discipline* for proposed signals; [MASS](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278) offers a way to turn varied opinions into a ranked portfolio signal. Neither paper validates its method for around-the-clock, short-horizon trading across your asset classes, and neither should control live sizing or exits.

## AlphaAgent: lift the three regularisers, not its backtest as a risk guarantee

The portable core is a pipeline around each candidate strategy: express it in a constrained, auditable representation; check it against a library of existing signals; verify that its stated rationale matches its actual computation; and penalize unnecessary formula structure and parameters. The paper uses abstract syntax trees (ASTs) to represent formulas, compares their common subtrees to assess similarity, and has an LLM score consistency between the market hypothesis, factor description, and formula. [AST Representation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=4)[Regularizers](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=5)

**What each component protects—and what it costs in exploration**

- **Originality / duplicate control:** Compare a proposed signal’s AST with your accepted and rejected library, then penalize or queue close structural matches. This helps limit redundant strategies and the risk of crowding your own book with near-duplicates. It costs some *useful* local search: an existing family may work well in a new market, instrument, or horizon even if its formula resembles an old one. AST novelty is not economic novelty; equivalent signals can have different syntax, while structurally similar formulas can behave differently when their inputs or holding periods change. AlphaAgent describes its common-subtree similarity and an originality score against a factor library such as Alpha101. [Novelty Metric](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=4)

- **Hypothesis–formula alignment:** Store a concise rationale with every candidate and check both that the formula implements that rationale and that its written description actually matches the formula. This protects against plausible-sounding but unsupported strategies and makes human review easier. It can suppress discovery when the hypothesis is too narrow or when an LLM judge rewards a persuasive story rather than measurable evidence. In the paper, the consistency score combines two LLM assessments with equal weighting. There is also a notable implementation detail to check: the paper defines its combined regularisation score as lower-is-better but prints a *positive* consistency term, even though higher consistency is described as better. Reproducing the intended penalty requires resolving that sign convention in the implementation, not just copying the displayed equation. [Consistency Score](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=5)[Regularization Score](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=5)

- **Complexity control:** Penalize formula length, free parameters such as rolling windows, and excessive numbers of input features. This reduces the space for brittle, backtest-specific constructions and makes candidates easier to audit. The cost is less capacity to represent interactions or regime dependence that may genuinely matter. The paper proposes those penalties, but its displayed formulation is an example rather than a complete, deployment-ready specification with fixed weights or universal thresholds. [Complexity Control](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=4)

The system around those checks is also liftable: a human supplies a research direction; an idea generator proposes hypotheses; a factor generator produces candidates; an evaluator checks executability and numerical stability and returns test results; and the next research round uses that feedback. For your setup, keep that loop in a *research sandbox*: every candidate’s data snapshot, code, hypothesis, test window, and evaluation result should be versioned, and only your human promotion process can approve live deployment. [Research Loop](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=6)

**Inputs and how to adapt them by market.** AlphaAgent’s reported experiments use daily OHLCV features; their research framework is broader, but the results do not establish performance from order-book, macro, or news inputs. For your system, keep the same *regularisers* but make the signal language and input contract asset-specific:

| Market | Inputs to consider for candidate generation and validation |
|---|---|
| FX | Point-in-time bid/ask and executable spread, quote and trade flow, volatility, session/time-of-day, and relevant rate differentials. Define the instrument and venue explicitly; a generic daily “price” formula ignores fragmented liquidity and rollover. |
| Stock indices | Index and constituent returns, breadth, realized/implied volatility, futures basis, and session features. Avoid applying a single-stock formula unchanged to an index product. |
| Metals | Spot and futures prices, spreads, futures basis/curve, trading volume/open interest where reliable, USD and rate context, and session. |
| Energy | Contract-specific prices and spreads, curve/calendar spreads, volume/open interest, inventory and scheduled-report timestamps, plus relevant weather or demand data when timestamped and point-in-time. |
| Crypto | Venue-specific trades and order books, spread/depth, funding, open interest, liquidation flow, and continuous-time/session features; include venue and fee tier in the test contract. |
| Futures | Contract identifier, tick value, volume/open interest, term structure, expiry/roll calendar, and session; test both continuous-series construction and executable contract-level orders. |
| Single stocks | Point-in-time corporate-action-adjusted data, spread/depth, volume, auction/session features, and fundamentals only when their actual publication timestamps are available. |

These are adaptation requirements, not evidence that the paper tested those inputs. In particular, a feature such as “spread” must mean an observable, tradable spread at the decision time—not a future or daily summary that would be unavailable to the strategy.

**What factors did AlphaAgent actually find?** The paper discusses hypothesis examples such as candlestick patterns, breakouts, value–momentum dynamics, and market-structure anomalies, and its operator examples include rolling minima/maxima and moving averages. Its experiment uses four base alphas—intraday return, daily return, 20-day relative volume, and normalized daily range—and says it mined 15 alphas for a yearly decay comparison. But the paper does **not** give a readable catalogue of those 15 discovered formulas in the material reported here, so those examples should not be mistaken for the validated mined set. [Hypothesis Examples](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=5)[Base Signals](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=6)[Decay Analysis](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=8)

**Short-horizon cost prospects.** Of the signals explicitly named, intraday return, normalized daily range, and relative volume are the closest raw-material families for short-horizon research; daily return is a broader momentum/reversal input. But the reported prediction target is **next-day stock return**, and the portfolio evaluation is daily stock ranking, not scalping. The paper’s fee accounting does not establish that any named signal survives spread, slippage, market impact, funding, or high turnover at minute-level horizons. I would treat these as *candidate families to test*, not as paper-backed short-horizon strategies. For fast trading, emphasize hypotheses tied to observable microstructure inputs and require net-of-execution-cost, latency-aware tests before promoting them. [Prediction Target](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=3)[Base Signals](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=6)

The paper’s claimed protection is principally **against formula redundancy, over-complexity, and semantic drift**—not against portfolio drawdown. In its own daily-equity setup it reports maximum drawdowns of 9.36% on CSI 500 and 9.10% on S&P 500, but those are outcomes of its backtest portfolio, not a risk-control guarantee supplied by the regularisers. [Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.16789?page=7)

## MASS: lift the population and aggregation, but separate portfolio insight from stress testing

MASS represents heterogeneous investor styles with LLM agents, gives each agent a limited candidate stock pool, and records which names each agent selects. It weights investor *types* using recent signal performance, then aggregates their selections into a consensus score and a disagreement score. A tunable parameter decides how much to reward consensus versus penalize disagreement. The paper updates the type weights by simulated annealing against a five-day window of historical RankIC. [Agent Population](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=4)[Signal Aggregation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=5)[Weight Learning](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=5)

**What to lift for portfolio or stress use**

- **As a portfolio signal:** Create distinct, explicitly specified views—such as trend, value/carry, volatility-aware, event-sensitive, or liquidity-aware—and aggregate their *scores or proposed exposures*. In addition to MASS’s consensus-minus-disagreement idea, log the distribution of each view’s proposal, its calibration, and its contribution to net returns. Use the aggregate as a ranking or allocation *input*; let a separate deterministic risk layer convert it into positions and limits.
- **As a stress or fragility monitor:** Track whether views suddenly converge, whether the aggregate depends on one investor type, or whether the preferred names change sharply under small perturbations to inputs, weights, or regime assumptions. This is a reasonable extension of its heterogeneous population and disagreement signal, but it is **not a stress-testing result established by MASS**: the paper does not simulate fills, liquidation cascades, correlated live positions, or portfolio losses under prescribed shocks. Do not label the simulated investors a drawdown control.
- **As a search tool:** Compare the aggregate with each type’s individual output and with simple, non-LLM baselines. If the ensemble only looks good after the optimizer selects weights on the same short window used to judge it, the ensemble is fitting the evaluation window rather than demonstrating durable diversification.

**Inputs and cross-market changes.** MASS’s tested data are Chinese-equity focused: daily stock features, firm-level news/reports and fundamentals, macro inputs, and historical returns. Its example archetypes include style, risk appetite, holding period, consistency, rationality, and stock-pool preference; the paper gives a value-oriented, longer-horizon investor as a prompt example, not as an independently validated persona. [Market Inputs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=6)[Persona Fields](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=16)[Persona Example](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=17)

The architecture can be adapted, but each market needs different point-in-time inputs and a different definition of an “investor view”:

| Market | Useful agent views and inputs for an adaptation |
|---|---|
| FX | Trend/carry, session-specific liquidity, volatility, and event/rate views; use executable quotes, spreads, and rate/carry data with release timestamps. |
| Stock indices | Breadth, sector leadership, volatility, macro, and futures-basis views; aggregate at the traded index or contract level, not by pretending an index is an individual stock. |
| Metals | Trend, curve/basis, macro/FX, and inventory/event views; align spot and futures contract timestamps and roll handling. |
| Energy | Curve, inventory, weather/demand, and event views; scheduled inventory releases and contract expiries need strict timestamp controls. |
| Crypto | Momentum, funding/basis, liquidity, and liquidation-risk views; use venue-specific books and fees, and account for the 24/7 clock. |
| Futures | Separate views for outright direction, calendar spreads, basis, and roll/expiry; require contract-level prices, tick values, margin, and liquidity. |
| Single stocks | Fundamental, news/event, sector-relative, liquidity, and price–volume views; prevent look-ahead from filings, constituents, and corporate actions. |

For a true **portfolio** use, the shared output should also carry instrument correlations, existing book exposures, and risk constraints. MASS’s stock-selection aggregation alone does not measure whether two agents’ picks are already highly correlated with the rest of your live strategies.

**What signals did MASS actually find?** The concrete signal in the method is **weighted selection consensus minus weighted disagreement**. The paper’s motivating market hypothesis says high disagreement can be associated with overpricing and weaker future returns, particularly under short-sale constraints; this is the paper’s rationale for penalizing disagreement, not a separately discovered, portable formula for every asset. The named value-oriented persona is a prompt example, not a finding that “value” was the source of its returns. MASS does not report a catalogue of interpretable, reusable agent-generated rules comparable to a formula library. [Disagreement Rationale](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=16)[Aggregation Formula](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=5)

**Short-horizon cost prospects and the growth trade-off.** The consensus/disagreement score could be a useful *meta-signal* for your strategy ensemble—especially a measure of whether independent models agree on an instrument—but the paper tested daily agent choices and a **weekly-rebalanced** portfolio, not rapid trading. It equally weights the top 20% of ranked stocks and reports a 0.1% round-trip cost. That is not enough to infer survival at scalper horizons, where spread, queue position, adverse selection, fees, funding, and latency can dominate. For your system, test whether consensus adds incremental net value after those costs and whether high disagreement predicts bad outcomes at the actual holding horizon. [Portfolio Backtest](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=20)

Scaling the agent population increases the number of views and the reported RankIC, but adds operational cost and does not itself protect against drawdown. The paper reports **512 agents** in its scale study; its cost appendix reports average daily API costs of **$2.265 and 378 seconds for CSI 300**, with lower costs for its smaller pools. Those are paper-specific figures, not forecasts for your multi-asset system: costs could grow with asset universes, refresh frequency, and prompt size, unless you cache decisions or use cheaper models. The potential benefit is broader exploration and less dependence on one view; the corresponding risk is more opportunities to fit short-window weights, plus more correlated or confidently wrong agents. [Scaling Study](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=8)[Operating Costs](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10278?page=18)

## Practical division of labor

I’d use **AlphaAgent-style regularisation to control what enters the candidate strategy pool** and a **MASS-style ensemble to measure how candidate strategies agree or disagree**. Keep the aggregate descriptive or advisory until it passes asset-specific, point-in-time, walk-forward testing. Then let an independent risk service—not either paper’s LLM or its score—set sizing, gross/net exposure, correlated-risk limits, stops, drawdown limits, and emergency halts.

For signals, start by testing AlphaAgent’s named OHLCV families—intraday return, range, and relative volume—at the horizons and instruments where the data are actually available. Treat MASS consensus/disagreement as a possible *confidence or crowding feature* over your strategies, not as an entry signal already shown to work for short-horizon, multi-asset trading. The papers do not identify a specific mined formula or MASS persona that can be claimed to survive your execution costs.

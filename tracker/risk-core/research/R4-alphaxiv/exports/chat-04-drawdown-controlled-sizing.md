## Aurelle

Cutting size after losses is not automatically good risk control: it helps long-run growth only when losses predict a weaker strategy or when reducing risk is worth the drawdown protection; with noisy or mean-reverting returns, it can instead shrink exposure just before the rebound. The robust distinction is between **reducing size because the strategy’s estimated edge has fallen** and **reducing size mechanically because its equity has fallen**.

### What the methods do—and when they help

- **CPPI and TIPP** are floor-and-cushion rules, not forecasts of whether a strategy is about to lose again. CPPI scales risky exposure to the cushion above a floor. TIPP ratchets the floor upward as the portfolio reaches new highs, so it can protect more of the gains. Both can trade away upside participation as the cushion narrows. A floor or drawdown limit is a genuine guarantee only under the assumptions used to enforce it: gaps, jumps, slippage, and execution failures can defeat a rule calibrated to ordinary returns.
- **Anti-martingale sizing** reduces exposure after losses and increases it after wins. It can restrain risk during a deteriorating regime, but also compounds exposure into a winning streak that may simply be noise. “Anti-martingale” describes the sizing rule; it does not establish that the strategy’s edge persists after wins.
- **Equity-curve filters**, such as trading only when strategy equity is above a moving average, are another form of performance-based de-risking. They can help if weak strategy performance tends to persist. If returns are independent noise, the filter mostly adds lag and missed exposure; if returns mean-revert, it can reduce size at the point expected returns are improving. A moving-average crossover is evidence about past performance, not proof that the edge has changed.
- **Drawdown-constrained growth-optimal sizing** is more principled than a generic “cut after losses” rule. [Grossman and Zhou’s classic analysis](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Grossman+Zhou+Optimal+Investment+Strategies+for+Controlling+Drawdowns+1993) and [Cvitanić and Karatzas’s extension](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Cvitanic+Karatzas+On+Portfolio+Optimization+under+Drawdown+Constraints) study growth optimization subject to a path-dependent drawdown constraint. Later work shows how a constrained long-run growth strategy can be built from an unconstrained growth-optimal portfolio using a drawdown transformation. The result is a state-dependent policy tied to the drawdown cushion—not a universal instruction to de-risk after every losing trade. [Main Result](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1110.6289?page=2)
- **Maximum-drawdown optimization** directly constrains or minimizes peak-to-trough loss. It addresses a risk measure closer to the one a trading operation may actually care about than variance does, but optimizing the worst historical path can overfit that path. Use walk-forward testing and costs; don’t treat a backtest maximum as a future bound.
- **Capital allocation across strategies** should respond to both estimated edge and portfolio-level risk contribution. Cutting capital to every strategy below its own moving average can strand capital after correlated losses—or shift capital toward strategies that have only recently looked good. Measure drawdown on the combined system as well as each sleeve: diversified sleeves can still fail together in a common shock.

### What the evidence says about the trade-off

The theoretical work establishes how to optimize *subject to* a drawdown constraint under specified models; it does not provide a universal percentage of growth given up for a given drawdown limit. Nor do these examples establish a general reduction in ruin probability. A strictly positive floor can prevent ruin in a model where the return bounds and execution assumptions really hold; ordinary backtests report observed drawdown, not the probability of ruin under future tails.

### Practical design for this system

1. **Use a portfolio-level floor as the hard safety layer.** Define it against combined system equity, for example as a chosen fraction of the system’s high-water mark. Scale aggregate risk to the cushion above that floor, with caps for leverage, liquidity, and correlated exposures. A TIPP-like ratcheting floor is appropriate if the goal is to lock in gains; a less aggressive floor avoids ratcheting risk down after every small high.
2. **Make per-strategy sizing edge-aware, not loss-count-aware.** Base it on conservative, shrinkage-adjusted estimates of net edge and volatility. Don’t increase size merely because an equity curve is above its moving average, or cut it solely because it is below one. Require evidence of a persistent change in net performance before changing the strategy’s risk budget.
3. **Treat equity-curve filters as a tested overlay.** Compare the filter with unfiltered trading on the same walk-forward periods, including fees, slippage, turnover, and time out of market. Test both persistent and mean-reverting return regimes; set the lookback and re-entry rule before evaluating the holdout. Hysteresis or gradual scaling can reduce whipsaw, though it cannot make a weak signal predictive.
4. **Keep a hard maximum-drawdown constraint distinct from recovery logic.** The constraint limits further risk as the cushion shrinks; a restart or re-entry rule decides when to take risk again. [Hsieh’s restart study](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2303.02613) suggests restart can improve historical outcomes, but its BTC example also shows that an observed drawdown can exceed the nominal limit.
5. **Allocate across strategies using marginal portfolio risk, not just individual drawdown rank.** Reduce a sleeve when its evidence of edge deteriorates or its contribution to portfolio tail risk becomes too large; avoid mechanically transferring its budget to whichever sleeve recently won. A strategy-level loss may be diversification at the portfolio level, while several individually modest drawdowns may signal a shared factor shock.

For a pathwise drawdown guarantee under explicit return-support assumptions, [Hsieh and Barmish’s drawdown-modulated feedback work](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1710.01503) is a useful control-oriented starting point. For long-run growth under drawdown constraints, read [Cherny and Oblój (2013)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1110.6289) alongside [Kardaras, Oblój, and Platen’s numeraire-portfolio treatment](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1206.2305). [Chekhlov, Uryasev, and Zabarankin (2005)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Chekhlov+Uryasev+Zabarankin+Drawdown+Measure+in+Portfolio+Optimization+2005) is a classic reference on drawdown measures in portfolio optimization. For recent finite-horizon methods, see [Dorador (2024)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2401.02601) on constrained maximum-drawdown optimization and [Hsieh (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272) on robust drawdown control.

## Aurelle

For a book of many small strategies, I would use **a whole-book drawdown floor as the hard safety layer, cluster limits for shared risks, and strategy-level sizing only as a softer overlay**. The three most useful research anchors are [Grossman and Zhou’s 1993 drawdown-constrained growth model](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Grossman+Zhou+Optimal+Investment+Strategies+for+Controlling+Drawdowns+1993), [Hsieh’s 2026 robust drawdown-control method](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272), and [Hsieh’s 2023 drawdown modulation with restart](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2303.02613). They answer different questions: growth under a floor, a finite-horizon pathwise safety rule, and how a restart changes backtested participation.

### The three methods and their trade-offs

| Paper or method | What it returns | Reported growth versus drawdown |

**1. Grossman–Zhou: optimize exposure above a moving floor.** The setup tracks current wealth $V_t$ and its running peak $H_t$, and imposes a floor such as $V_t \geq \alpha H_t$. The model takes market dynamics, investor preferences, and the floor as inputs; it returns a risky exposure that is proportional to the cushion above that floor. When wealth is close to the floor, risk exposure falls; after making a new high, the cushion grows and so can exposure. The foundational [Cvitanić–Karatzas extension (1994)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Cvitanic+Karatzas+On+Portfolio+Optimization+Under+Drawdown+Constraints+1994) generalizes the portfolio-optimization treatment. Later, [Cherny and Oblój (2013)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1110.6289) show how nonlinear drawdown constraints can be transformed into an unconstrained problem with modified utility; [Kardaras, Oblój, and Platen (2017)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1206.2305) develop long-run growth optimality in the drawdown-constrained class. [Main Result](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1110.6289v3?page=2) [Long-Run Growth](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1206.2305v2?page=4)

This is the strongest conceptual basis for linking a risk budget to a wealth floor rather than to the number of consecutive losses. But the classic continuous-time prescription does not automatically remain optimal in discrete time: [Klass and Nowicki (2005)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Klass+Nowicki+The+Grossman+and+Zhou+Investment+Strategy+is+Not+Always+Optimal+2005) explicitly challenge that transfer. Later discrete-time work uses dynamic programming instead, including [Hernández-Bustos and Hernández-Hernández (2023)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Hernandez-Bustos+Hernandez-Hernandez+Portfolio+Management+under+Drawdown+Constraint+in+Discrete-Time+Financial+Markets+2023). In short: the floor-and-cushion idea is powerful; the continuous-time optimal control formula is not a plug-in rule for a gappy, discrete, multi-strategy book.

**2. Hsieh 2026: enforce safety against a specified set of returns.** At each step, the method takes the return support, transaction-cost vector, current equity and high-water mark, and a maximum percentage drawdown. It computes the available drawdown cushion; it then restricts exposures to those whose worst loss over the specified support fits inside that cushion. Every safe exposure can be written as cushion × feasible direction. With stagewise-independent returns, a Bellman recursion chooses the direction that maximizes expected terminal wealth. The paper reports an SPY/TLT empirical-return-support illustration over 21-, 63-, and 252-day horizons, using 0.5-basis-point per-asset stage costs and 300,000 simulated paths; the plotted results show higher expected returns than the LTI benchmark under each positive tested limit, but do not give a table of exact return or Sharpe values. [Safety Condition](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272v1?page=4) [Numerical Setup](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272v1?page=10) [Return Comparison](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272v1?page=12)

Its key virtue is an exact finite-horizon drawdown guarantee when the return-support assumption is true: wealth stays at least $(1-d_{\max})$ times the running peak, and therefore remains positive. That is not a measured probability-of-ruin result; within the model, ruin is excluded. An empirical min/max return range is not a guarantee against a future gap outside that range, so robust support must include credible jump, spread, and liquidation scenarios. The expected-terminal-wealth optimization also assumes independent stage returns; the safety constraint itself is more general than that optimizer. This paper cites and extends the line of drawdown-modulated control developed by [Hsieh and Barmish (2017)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1710.01503), while distinguishing its finite-horizon robust method from the older continuous-time solution. [Prior Methods](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272v1?page=2)

**3. Hsieh 2023: restart to resume trading after a local floor is reached.** The method first estimates/tunes a modulation gain from historical returns. It then reduces exposure as drawdown approaches the limit. Near the threshold, it resets the *local* drawdown reference and resumes with a smaller, time-decayed gain. In the reported VT example, with a 10% limit and restart threshold 1%, modulation without restart returned −8.422% at 10% maximum drawdown; modulation with restart returned +20.97% at 10% maximum drawdown, with reported N-period Sharpe ratios −1.3375 and 1.0462, respectively. In the BTC example, using a 20% limit and 2% restart threshold, modulation without restart returned +17.41% at 20.00% drawdown and Sharpe 0.6970; with restart it returned +101.82% at 20.18% drawdown and Sharpe 2.1363. The BTC restart result therefore **slightly exceeded the nominal limit** on the reported overall path. [ETF Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2303.02613v1?page=5) [BTC Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2303.02613v1?page=6)

Restart addresses a real failure mode of a pure cushion rule: at the floor, exposure can go to zero just as opportunities return. But it changes the reference point. Resetting the local drawdown clock does not erase losses relative to the book’s original high-water mark; if it is implemented as if it did, the manager can hide cumulative deterioration. These are historical, selected tests—not proof that restart improves expected growth. The paper builds on [Hsieh and Barmish’s 2017 modulation rule](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1710.01503), which reported a TSLA 60-day out-of-sample comparison: drawdown modulation ended at $10,050 with 5.0% drawdown, versus $11,360 and 22.5% for classical Kelly. That example makes the protection/participation cost visible: less drawdown, but also less return over that particular interval. [Out-of-Sample](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1710.01503v1?page=5)

### Equity-curve trading on noisy strategies

A moving-average equity filter is a **signal about past strategy performance**, not a drawdown guarantee and not evidence by itself that expected returns have turned negative. It helps only if weakness tends to persist long enough to avoid losses; with noisy or mean-reverting returns, it can cut exposure after a losing patch and re-enter after the rebound has already occurred. It also adds lag, turnover, and an extra parameter-selection problem.

A broad, recent practitioner study offers a useful caution, though it is not a peer-reviewed paper: [AllocateSmartly’s “Surfing the Equity Curve” (2026)](https://allocatesmartly.com/surfing-the-equity-curve-using-trend-following-to-switch-strategies-on-and-off/) tested moving-average filters on 100+ tactical-allocation strategies from 1973. The strategies were switched on/off independently at month-end. Filtered portfolios spent about 69%–96% of the time invested. Nearly all variants improved maximum drawdown, but all tested combinations failed to beat the average strategy on annual return and Sharpe; only the 6/12-month filter improved the Ulcer Performance Index, and the authors regarded that isolated result as likely noise. Their switching-cost estimate also omitted the additional cost of turning strategies on and off. This is relevant evidence against assuming that an equity-curve filter will help noisy strategies; it does not settle the question for intraday systems or all market regimes.

### Where to apply control in this book

- **Whole book:** Put the non-negotiable floor here, since survival and realized drawdown are ultimately portfolio outcomes. Use combined marked-to-market equity and preserve its high-water mark across strategy additions, removals, and reallocations.
- **Clusters:** Apply additional limits to groups sharing a driver—such as similar FX carry, equity momentum, or energy trend exposures. This is the layer that catches correlated strategies masquerading as diversification.
- **Individual strategies:** Keep local sizing and monitoring for strategy-specific deterioration, but treat a moving-average or loss-trigger rule as a tested overlay, not the primary risk guarantee. Use a persistent, unitized “shadow” equity curve so changing allocated capital does not itself reset or distort the performance signal.

For this system, I would **not** impose a hard, independent drawdown stop on every strategy by default: many small strategies can be noisy, and independent stops can cut several at once after a shared transient shock. Instead, use strategy-level risk estimates to shape allocations, cluster-level caps to control common exposures, and a whole-book cushion rule for hard drawdown control. Keep a separate global high-water mark even if a local strategy restart is allowed. That combination addresses both path dependence and the main restart hazard without confusing a reset local signal with recovery of book equity.

## 1. Asset classes: adjust the risk model to the trading mechanics

A drawdown cap controls loss only to the extent that returns, execution, and financing stay inside its assumptions. In a leveraged book, the operational risk may be a margin call or forced liquidation *before* a smooth equity drawdown rule can respond. Stress the sizing model with session gaps, weekend reopenings, limit moves, spread widening, and the time it takes to cancel or reduce exposure—not just daily volatility. A generic risk framework would look like this:

| Market | Drawdown-control emphasis | Session, gap, and leverage concerns | Return behavior to allow for |
|---|---|---|---|

Leverage makes small price moves material to equity and margin. | Carry and short-volatility trades can have quiet gains punctuated by sharp losses. Contract specifications, delivery months, and roll behavior matter. | Gold and industrial metals can have different drivers. | **Futures** | Control risk at the contract and portfolio level using notional, volatility, and stressed margin—not a uniform contract count. Aggregate related contracts and calendar spreads. | Leverage is intrinsic. Variation margin, exchange price limits, liquidity breaks, expiry, and roll can turn a market loss into a liquidity event. A stop or target cannot guarantee a fill at its assumed price. | Trend-following and spread/reversion strategies have quite different loss paths. Explicitly model roll and intraday/overnight holding behavior. |
| **Single stocks** | Add issuer, sector, and event concentration limits; budget for correlated holdings within the same theme. | Earnings, news, halts, and overnight gaps can jump through stops. For **all** of these, calibrate the drawdown rule to the strategy’s actual holding period and marks. For fast scalpers, include intraday loss accumulation, spread/slippage, and execution outages; for overnight strategies, include close-to-open moves; for slower strategies, include persistent drawdowns and financing. Use different stress envelopes where the market’s worst loss can arrive faster than the system can trade.

A robust support-based rule can keep simulated wealth above a floor if every realized return remains within its specified support. That is a conditional guarantee—not a guarantee against an unobserved gap, halt, bad mark, missed fill, or margin liquidation. [Hsieh’s finite-horizon robust drawdown-control paper](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272) makes the support assumption explicit; the system should widen its scenario set for jumps and execution stress rather than mistake historical minima for physical bounds. Exact values are not tabulated in the retrieved text. | The constraint is pathwise under the modeled return support; under that support, wealth stays above the floor. | Evidence that a dynamic cushion rule can use a specified drawdown budget more efficiently than fixed proportional exposure in that setup—not a reported cross-market growth premium. [Return Comparison](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272v1?page=12) |

For context on *which* drawdowns should be expected, [Landolfi (2026), *Drawdown Risk Beyond Brownian Motion*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.00127) simulated three-year return paths with the same assumed Sharpe of 1 and annual volatility of 1, but different return structures. Its 90th-percentile maximum-drawdown measure was **1.32×** the Gaussian benchmark for its stylized mean-reversion/short-volatility archetype, versus **1.05×** for trend/breakout; the trend example instead had longer recovery and time-under-water measures. With Sharpe uncertainty included, its maximum-loss measure rose to **1.48×** the Gaussian benchmark. These are model-based stress comparisons, not observed strategy returns, but they show why a single Gaussian drawdown table can miscalibrate different sleeves. [Archetype Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.00127v1?page=11)

For the **long-run growth cost of drawdown constraints**, [Grossman and Zhou (1993)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Grossman+Zhou+Optimal+Investment+Strategies+for+Controlling+Drawdowns+1993) and [Cvitanić and Karatzas (1994)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Cvitanic+Karatzas+On+Portfolio+Optimization+Under+Drawdown+Constraints+1994) provide foundational theory, while [Cherny and Oblój (2013)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1110.6289) and [Kardaras, Oblój, and Platen (2017)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1206.2305) extend the analysis. Their conclusions are model- and utility-dependent; they do not supply a universal “X% less CAGR for Y% less drawdown” rule. Later work also cautions that the classic Grossman–Zhou policy need not remain optimal in discrete time: [Klass and Nowicki (2005)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Klass+Nowicki+Grossman+Zhou+investment+strategy+not+always+optimal+2005).

### Strategy ideas suggested by the studies

These are research leads, not recommendations to promote them directly to live.

- **Cushion-modulated strategy sizing:** Set allowable exposure to the fraction of the book’s risk cushion remaining; estimate the exposure direction separately from the cushion. Candidate instruments: any liquid futures, FX, index, metals, energy, crypto, or stock strategies. Required data: synchronized equity and exposure paths, peak equity, contract-level returns, realistic transaction costs, and plausible stressed return bounds. [Hsieh and Barmish (2017)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1710.01503) supplies the basic modulation idea; [Hsieh (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272) formulates robust safe actions and a finite-horizon optimizer.
- **Restart as re-entry logic:** After exposure is stopped or reduced near a floor, define a separately tested re-enable rule rather than permanently leaving the strategy inactive. Candidate instruments in the cited backtests: a global-equity ETF and BTC; needed inputs include strategy P&L, restart threshold, new risk budget, costs, and a permanent book-level high-water mark. [Hsieh (2023)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2303.02613) reports favorable ETF and BTC test outcomes, but also the BTC limit overshoot.
- **Trend/breakout strategies with expected droughts:** Test return-path calibration that includes positive skew, fat tails, and long recovery periods. Instruments could include diversified futures or FX trend sleeves; the needed observations are daily P&L and exposure, enough history across regimes, and block-bootstrap or explicit tail scenarios. [Landolfi (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.00127) is a drawdown-calibration study, not a strategy backtest; [Lempérière et al. (2014), *Two Centuries of Trend Following*](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Lemp%C3%A9ri%C3%A8re+Two+Centuries+of+Trend+Following+2014) is a strategy-history reference.
- **Single-stock ranker with health gate and uncertainty cap:** Trade only while a point-in-time strategy-health gate passes; use a position-level tail cap rather than continuously shrinking every position by inverse uncertainty. The 2026 study is specific to an AI-exposed U.S. equity ranker; it needs matured live-style prediction errors, PIT-safe features, returns, turnover, and realistic costs. [Sanderink (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.13252) reports holdout Sharpe improvements for its gate-plus-cap configuration over its gate-plus-volatility baseline, but that is a narrow-universe deployment experiment, not evidence for FX or futures.

### Equity-curve trading: caution for noisy strategies

The most directly relevant broad test I found is [AllocateSmartly’s 2026 study](https://allocatesmartly.com/surfing-the-equity-curve-using-trend-following-to-switch-strategies-on-and-off/), a practitioner analysis rather than a peer-reviewed paper. It switched more than 100 tactical-allocation strategies on or off independently using monthly moving-average crossovers over a history beginning in 1973. Filters improved maximum drawdown in nearly all tested combinations, but all tested combinations underperformed the average strategy in annual return and Sharpe; only one crossover improved its Ulcer Performance Index, and the authors judged that result likely noise. The tests did not include the *additional* cost of switching strategies. Its market and frequency differ from an around-the-clock automated book, but the result argues against assuming a moving-average rule is a free risk improvement.

That caution is especially important for mean-reverting sleeves: a filter that reacts to a losing equity patch can cut just before the rebound. For trending sleeves, a trend filter may align with return persistence, but parameter search can select a lucky lookback. I would require pre-registered walk-forward tests with costs and report exposure time, return, Sharpe, drawdown, turnover, and rebound missed—not just the best filtered equity curve.

## 3. Combinations: build a layered controller, not one universal trigger

The most useful combinations pair a **hard drawdown constraint** with **separate evidence about strategy quality, uncertainty, and common exposures**. The following are design syntheses; the papers do not jointly validate these full systems.

1. **Hard safety + conservative edge sizing + restart:** Combine [Hsieh (2026), robust drawdown control](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272), [Hakobyan and Lototsky (2025), risk-penalized/fractional Kelly sizing](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2503.17927), and [Hsieh (2023), restart mechanism](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2303.02613). Estimate edge and its uncertainty; size from a conservative edge estimate rather than a point estimate; cap aggregate loss using the book’s drawdown cushion; when constrained, reduce risk; then re-risk in steps under a restart rule. This separates “is the edge credible?” from “how much risk remains before the floor?” and “when can exposure resume?” Stress it with costs and return shocks outside the calibration sample. The restart study’s BTC result exceeding its nominal drawdown is a reason to keep the original portfolio high-water constraint active through every restart.

2. **Drawdown floor + model-specific health gate + position tail cap:** Combine [Hsieh (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23272) with [Sanderink (2026), *When Alpha Breaks*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.13252). The first restricts exposures to keep the book inside a drawdown-safe region under a support model; the second distinguishes whether a strategy should trade at all from which particular predictions deserve caution. In its equity-ranker test, the model-specific gate had AUROC **0.721** overall and **0.750** in its final period, while VIX percentile had **0.449** and **0.504**; the paper’s capped-uncertainty rule improved its reported final-period Sharpe from **0.375** for Gate + Vol to **0.925** for Gate + Vol + cap. Those figures are from one model/universe and should not be carried over to other asset classes without testing. [Gate Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.13252v1?page=1) [Deployment Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.13252v1?page=14)

3. **Portfolio drawdown control + regime-aware cluster allocation:** Combine [Chekhlov, Uryasev, and Zabarankin (2005), drawdown measures in portfolio optimization](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Chekhlov+Uryasev+Zabarankin+Drawdown+Measure+in+Portfolio+Optimization+2005) with [Nystrup et al. (2017), regime-based dynamic allocation](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Nystrup+Hansen+Madsen+Lindstr%C3%B6m+Dynamic+Allocation+or+Diversification+A+Regime-Based+Approach+to+Multiple+Assets+2017), and [Alexander and Fabozzi (2026), minimum regime performance](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.08356). Use drawdown-aware optimization for the total book; allocate first across clusters representing common economic exposures; and use regime performance as a *durability diagnostic* when setting each cluster’s capital budget. That way, historical diversification is not mistaken for distinct risk, and a weak strategy is not automatically killed just because a noisy short window is below its moving average. MRP should be treated cautiously: it is the minimum Sharpe across candidate regime splits, which is deliberately conservative and can be strongly affected by the minimum window and the search over splits; the paper describes it as diagnostic, not predictive. [MRP Definition](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.08356v1?page=4) [MRP Limitation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.08356v1?page=22)

4. **Risk-off monitor + global kill switch + staged re-enable:** Combine a validated market or liquidity stress detector with the model-health gate above, then apply the drawdown floor at portfolio level. A market-wide shock can justify a broad risk reduction; one failing strategy should usually trigger a narrower response. Do not make an irreversible kill decision from one drawdown threshold alone: use a kill switch for operational or extreme-risk conditions, then require recovery of data integrity, liquidity, strategy health, and portfolio cushion before re-enabling at small size. [Hsieh (2023)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2303.02613) offers one restart mechanism; [Sanderink (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.13252) shows why model health and market stress need not be the same signal. The exact kill and re-enable ladder is an operational design proposal, not a result validated by those studies.

### Per strategy, cluster, or whole book?

Use **all three levels with different jobs**:

- **Whole book:** The hard limit belongs here, because the investor experiences total portfolio drawdown and ruin. Maintain an unreset global high-water mark.
- **Cluster:** Put caps on shared exposures—such as USD, equity beta, short volatility, or related energy contracts—so many small strategies cannot collectively create a large hidden position.
- **Per strategy:** Track local drawdown, edge uncertainty, execution, and alpha decay. Use these for soft throttling or review, not automatic universal shutdown. If a local restart is permitted, it must not reset the whole-book loss record.

For noisy and mean-reverting strategy returns, mechanically cutting size after losses or below an equity moving average can reduce exposure at the wrong time. The evidence is not that equity-curve trading *never* works; it is that broad tests have not shown a reliable free Sharpe improvement, and tailored filters are vulnerable to overfitting. The safer default is **hard portfolio and cluster risk control, conservative sizing under uncertain edge, and selective strategy-level gates only when walk-forward evidence supports them**.

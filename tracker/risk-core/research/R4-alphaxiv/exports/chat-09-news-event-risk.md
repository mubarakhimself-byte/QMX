## Aurelle

For a scalper, the safest default is **flat through the release and the first post-event liquidity reset—not simply running a wider stop**. Announcement returns can be large and directional, but the move is concentrated in a short interval when spreads, slippage, and gap risk can overwhelm a strategy’s ordinary edge.

### What the evidence says

**Scheduled macro releases and central-bank decisions.** Classic high-frequency studies find that unexpected news—not just the fact that an announcement is due—drives abrupt price adjustment. In FX, *The Micro Effects of Macro Announcements: Real-Time Price Discovery in Foreign Exchange* (Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold & Vega, 2003) studies how prices respond to the surprise component of US macro releases. In Treasury markets, *Price Formation and Liquidity in the U.S. Treasury Market: The Response to Public Information* (Fleming & Remolona, 1999) documents announcement-time changes in prices and liquidity. *How Markets Process Information: News Releases and Volatility* (Ederington & Lee, 1993) is a foundational study of volatility around scheduled releases. Taken together, this work supports a useful distinction: **the initial repricing is fast, while abnormal volatility and impaired liquidity can outlast the first price move**. A market may have “reacted” before it is cheap or safe to trade again.

There is no universal recovery time. A sensible starting assumption for a liquid FX pair or index future is a sharp-risk window around the timestamp, followed by a **10–15-minute minimum post-release cooldown**; use a longer window for major central-bank decisions, press conferences, or releases that produce continuing repricing. Treat the decision and the subsequent press conference as separate events. A short-horizon strategy should not infer that normal execution has returned merely because the first candle has closed.

**Earnings and corporate events.** *The Intraday Speed of Adjustment of Stock Prices to Earnings and Dividend Announcements* (Patell & Wolfson, 1984) studies how quickly prices incorporate earnings and dividend news; *Spreads, Depths, and the Impact of Earnings Information* (Lee, Mucklow & Ready, 1993) focuses on liquidity around earnings. The broader earnings literature also finds post-earnings-announcement drift, but that is a longer-horizon return pattern—not evidence that a scalper can reliably capture the announcement move after transaction costs. For short-horizon systems, earnings create overnight-gap and spread risks that ordinary stop placement cannot control. Flatten before a known report if the strategy is not explicitly designed to trade it; after the release, wait for continuous trading and liquidity to normalize. For halts, guidance changes, mergers, and other material company news, assume that a stop may execute well past its trigger or not execute until trading resumes.

**Unscheduled news shocks.** There is no reliable pre-event window to schedule around. The operational analogue is a circuit breaker: act on a trustworthy news or market-data signal, suspend new entries, cancel stale orders, and reduce or close exposure if spreads, gaps, or volatility breach limits. A delayed or ambiguous headline should trigger caution, not a directional trade. Stops are not a substitute for a maximum-loss control when prices can gap.

**Session opens and closes.** These are recurring liquidity transitions, not news events. Opening auctions, the first minutes of continuous trading, closing auctions, and thin regional handoffs can all produce unusual spreads and price moves. Use each instrument’s own exchange calendar and session data: a US equity open is not the same event as a Tokyo FX handoff, and continuously traded crypto has no equivalent cash-market open. A practical initial rule is to suppress new scalps for the first **5–15 minutes** after a major open and around the close or auction, then tune that window from instrument-level execution data.

### A practical event-window policy

| Event and instrument | Starting policy for a strategy not built to trade the event |
|---|---|
| High-impact macro release for a currency or index future | Stop initiating trades shortly before the timestamp; flatten or sharply cut risk through the release. Resume only after the initial jump and spread/volatility dislocation pass—start with a 10–15-minute post-event wait. |
| Central-bank decision and press conference | Treat the statement and press conference as separate risk windows. Flatten or reduce before the statement; keep the reduced-risk state until after the press conference and market normalization. |
| Single-stock earnings or material corporate event | Do not carry a short-horizon position through a known earnings release unless that strategy is explicitly event-tested. For after-hours or premarket news, wait for regular-session liquidity to re-form; protect against halts and gaps. |
| Unscheduled shock | Trigger a market-data/news circuit breaker: block entries, cancel stale orders, and reduce risk under predefined loss and liquidity limits. |
| Regional open, close, or auction | Suppress entries around the transition; use instrument-specific time windows and auction rules rather than one global clock setting. |

**Do not use “widen the stop” as the default event response.** A wider stop increases the amount at risk precisely when execution is least predictable. Prefer flattening or reducing position size; if trading through an event is an intentional strategy, size against a stressed gap or volatility estimate, use event-aware execution limits, and define a maximum loss that does not depend on a stop filling at its trigger. “Trade the event” should require separate out-of-sample results net of spread, fees, slippage, and missed fills—not merely a directional signal.

Calibrate windows by **instrument, event type, and session**. Track realized volatility, quoted and effective spreads, depth, slippage, and fill rates in event time against matched non-event periods. Resume only when the strategy’s own execution conditions—not just a fixed timer—have recovered. For a multi-strategy system, apply the restriction at the shared risk layer as well as to individual agents, so many small strategies cannot collectively rebuild a large position into the same event.

## Aurelle

**The strongest policy lesson is to estimate event *risk* separately from event *alpha*.** The best three fits here are a sparse event-volatility model for 24-hour FX, a high-frequency order-book study of macro releases in equity-index futures, and a noise-robust earnings-jump study. ## Three papers that matter most

### 1. [*What Events Matter for Exchange Rate Volatility?* (2024)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2411.16244) — Martins & Lopes

**What it does, step by step:**  
1. It takes five-minute AUD returns across the 24-hour FX day and aligns them to a Bloomberg calendar of 117 US and Australian macro releases.  
2. It models return volatility as a combination of persistent stochastic volatility, a five-minute time-of-day pattern, and event effects. For each release, it allows up to six five-minute post-release lags—30 minutes total—and uses spike-and-slab selection to distinguish relevant events from noise.  
3. It returns posterior event inclusion probabilities and volatility multipliers by event and lag, plus out-of-sample volatility forecasts. It does **not** return a directional trading signal, stop distance, or position-size rule. The data cover 2017–2023, with the later portion held out for forecasting. [Data and Model](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2411.16244v1?page=4)

**What it reports:** Nine releases had more than 95% inclusion probability at the first post-release lag: FOMC decision and minutes, US CPI, nonfarm payrolls and retail sales, and several Australian releases including the RBA decision, employment, GDP and retail sales. Effects differed by event and decayed over the 30-minute horizon; the estimated volatility multiplier for the US FOMC rate decision was about 4.8 at five minutes and fell substantially thereafter. [Selected Events](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2411.16244v1?page=7) This is a **risk forecast**, not evidence of profitable event trading. In a separate AUD–CHF allocation exercise, the model’s out-of-sample portfolio had an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.81 versus 0.69 for the stochastic-volatility comparator; that supports its use for risk forecasting, not a claim that event-window scalping earns those returns. [Portfolio Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2411.16244v1?page=14)

**Why it helps policy—and where it fails:** It gives a principled way to learn which scheduled releases matter, how long their volatility footprint lasts, and how time-of-day patterns relate to market openings. It is much closer to around-the-clock FX than a US cash-hours study. But it uses one currency pair for the main event analysis, prices rather than executable broker quotes, and scheduled events rather than surprise magnitude or unscheduled shocks. It does not model spread widening, slippage, or crypto’s venue fragmentation. Its use of a Bloomberg calendar also assumes access to a dependable event feed. The authors position the method against earlier work that preselects a small set of events by researcher judgement; its contribution is to let the data select among many candidates rather than hard-code that list. [Motivation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2411.16244v1?page=2)

### 2. [*Returns and Order Flow Imbalances: Intraday Dynamics and Macroeconomic News Effects* (2025)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.06788) — Takahashi

**What it does, step by step:**  
1. It uses CME best-bid-and-offer data for the S&P 500 E-mini from 2008–2013, calculating one-second mid-quote returns, order-flow imbalance, spread, depth and order-book activity.  
2. It fits a structural vector autoregression, identified through heteroskedasticity, separately across intraday intervals. Its macro-event analysis focuses on eight scheduled releases, mostly at 9:00 Chicago time.  
3. It outputs estimates of price impact, flow feedback, and return/flow volatility around releases. Those are useful **execution-risk and liquidity-state features**, not a directional release-surprise signal or an event-window position-size rule. [Data](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.06788v4?page=3)

**What it reports:** Around announcements, order-flow price impact rises, flow impact falls, return volatility spikes, and order-flow volatility drops before the release. The study reports a volatility spike in the five minutes just after the announcement and evidence that price impact can rise ahead of it as liquidity providers withdraw. [Announcement Effects](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.06788v4?page=9) Its one-second impulse responses mostly dissipate within a second, but that is the response of the *return–flow system* to shocks—not proof that volatility or execution costs are back to normal after one second. [Impulse Responses](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.06788v4?page=12) The paper does not report a net event-trading return strategy.

**Why it helps policy—and where it fails:** It gives a practical reason to gate entries on observable liquidity conditions—spread, depth and price impact—not solely on a timer. It also shows that scheduled announcements alter the execution environment, not just the price path. However, it is one futures contract, a 2008–2013 sample, and selected US releases; it cannot establish today’s broker CFD/FX spreads, retail slippage, or crypto behavior. Its calendar labels releases but it does not build the surprise-consensus signal needed to predict direction. The paper explicitly situates itself alongside earlier FX announcement price-discovery work and Treasury order-book studies; its contribution is to measure the return/flow feedback in index futures at one-second resolution. [Related Work](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.06788v4?page=2)

### 3. [*Warp Speed Price Moves: Jumps after Earnings Announcements* (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962) — Christensen, Timmermann & Veliyev

**What it does, step by step:**  
1. It aligns carefully checked earnings-release times and analyst-surprise data with tick-by-tick after-hours transactions and quotes for 50 high-volume US stocks, 2008–2020.  
2. It applies a pre-averaged, microstructure-noise-robust jump test to irregular, noisy after-hours prices. This matters because conventional jump tests can give false positives and false negatives in thin after-hours trading.  
3. It estimates the incidence of jumps and spillovers, then tests a simple long/short rule based on the earnings surprise. The outputs are jump probabilities, price responses, and strategy returns under different friction assumptions—not a universal event-window length. [After-Hours Method](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962v1?page=2)

**What it reports:** The announcing stock jumped in more than 90% of earnings after-hours sessions, versus 2.95% in regular sessions and 3.67% in after-hours sessions without earnings announcements. [Jump Rates](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962v1?page=3) The surprise-based rule earned a highly significant mean of 1.80% per transaction in a no-friction scenario over the sample; crucially, in 2016–2020 even minor frictions such as the bid–ask spread made average returns statistically insignificant. [Trading Returns](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962v1?page=2)[Friction Result](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962v1?page=40) That is the clearest growth-versus-survival result of the three: a large price move is not the same as an executable edge.

**Why it helps policy—and where it fails:** It makes after-hours earnings risk concrete and warns against trusting standard jump tests on thin data. But the sample is selected for liquid US stocks, and exchange/consolidated-tape quotes still do not guarantee the spreads or fills available to a particular broker or strategy. It requires accurate earnings timestamps and subscription analyst-consensus data. It says little about unscheduled corporate shocks, illiquid small caps, or non-equity markets. Its methodological challenge is to conventional fixed-interval and noise-sensitive jump measurement: the paper argues that five-minute-style windows and standard jump tests can miss or falsely detect after-hours moves. It also finds that the apparent no-friction opportunity largely vanishes once realistic spreads are imposed. [Test Limitations](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962v1?page=17)

## What I would implement

Use the first paper to **learn the scheduled risk map**, the second to **check live liquidity state**, and the third to **require net-of-friction evidence before allowing event trading**. None supplies a universal “trade this many minutes” answer. The windows below are conservative **starting defaults for strategies not explicitly trained and validated to trade the event**, not durations claimed by those papers. Count down to the official timestamp in the instrument’s local market timezone.

| Event | Pause new entries / reduce exposure before | Minimum wait after | Default action |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Central-bank rate decision | 10 min; 30 min before if carrying material exposure | 30 min after statement; treat a press conference as a separate event, with a fresh 10-min pre / 30-min post window | Flatten scalpers or sharply cut shared exposure. Resume only when spread, depth and realized volatility normalize. |
| CPI or jobs release | 5 min; 15 min for a strategy with meaningful event exposure | 15 min | Default flat through release. Do not replace a flat rule with a wider stop. Re-entry requires acceptable live spread and slippage. |
| Oil inventory report | 5 min | 10 min | Reduce or flatten energy scalpers; contracts can react differently to crude, product and inventory components. Trade the release only with a separately validated surprise signal and execution model. |
| Single-stock earnings | 5 min before the release, if the position can be carried into the scheduled timestamp | At least 30 min after the release **and** until the stock has continuous, usable liquidity; after-hours releases may require waiting for the next regular session | Flatten by default. Treat halts, guidance, M&A and other material company disclosures as gap risk, not ordinary stop risk. |
| Crypto exchange or protocol incident | Unscheduled: no dependable pre-window. For a *scheduled* upgrade, start with 30 min before | Unscheduled: 30–60 min after a verified incident ends and venue liquidity/quotes normalize | On a credible incident trigger, block entries, cancel stale orders and cut exposure under a shared risk limit. Do not assume another venue’s price or liquidity is representative. |

For all five, impose a **live execution gate** in addition to the clock: no re-entry while broker spread is above its instrument/session percentile limit, top-of-book depth is impaired, or realized slippage exceeds the strategy’s event-tested bound. Exchange data alone may not show broker-side spread widening; measure it from the system’s own order and fill records. Keep the event lockout at the portfolio/risk layer too, so many small agents cannot independently rebuild a large correlated position into the same release.

## Aurelle

**Use events as both alpha timestamps and risk-state inputs, but do not confuse a forecast of volatility with a forecast of direction.** The evidence is strongest for FX volatility around major releases, index-futures liquidity changes around macro news, and equity price discovery around earnings. It is much thinner for short-horizon strategies in oil inventories, gold event windows, crypto incidents, token unlocks and futures expiry; the windows below are conservative starting controls, not paper-estimated optima.

## 1. Events and windows by asset class

Window numbers below are **minutes before / minutes after** the scheduled event or session boundary. “After” means the minimum wait before ordinary strategies can re-enter; resume sooner only if the strategy has been tested for that event and live execution conditions have recovered. Flat or cut correlated portfolio exposure at the shared risk layer, not only in each agent.

| Asset class | Events to put on the calendar or monitor | Starting window | Default action |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **FX** | Relevant central-bank decisions, CPI and labor releases; policy speeches; London 4 p.m. benchmark fix and other currency fixes; major regional opens and handoffs | High-impact release: **5–10 / 15–30**; central-bank statement: **10–15 / 30**, and make the press conference a second event; fix: **15–30 / 10–15**; session open: **5–10 / 5–15** | Flatten scalpers through the announcement by default. Around fixes, cut or block strategies exposed to predictable one-sided flow; resume after the fix only when spread and order flow normalize. |
| **Stock indices** | Cash-market open, close and auctions; CPI/jobs/FOMC; major index rebalance dates | Open/close: **5–15 / 10–15**; CPI/jobs: **5–10 / 15–20**; FOMC: **10–15 / 30**, with a separate press-conference window | Suppress fresh entries through opening and closing transitions. For macro releases, flatten or sharply reduce exposure unless the strategy was specifically tested on event timestamps and fills. |
| **Gold and other metals** | US CPI/jobs/FOMC; real-yield and USD repricing; exchange open/close; metal-specific supply or policy headlines | Macro: **5–10 / 15–30**; central bank: **10–15 / 30**; session transitions: **5–10 / 10–15** | Treat major USD/rate events as cross-market risk for gold, even if the metal’s own venue remains open. Reduce exposure rather than widening stops. Resume after both volatility and spreads settle. |
| **Energy** | Weekly inventory reports, OPEC/OPEC+ announcements, production interruptions, sanctions and geopolitical supply shocks | Inventory: **5–10 / 10–20**; OPEC announcement: **15–30 / 30–60**; unscheduled supply shock: trigger-based | Flatten or sharply cut positions into known inventory data if not explicitly event-tested. Treat the headline, inventory components and subsequent revisions as potentially distinct information arrivals. For unscheduled disruptions, invoke a market-data/news circuit breaker. |
| **Crypto** | Major macro releases; venue outages, hacks, depegs and protocol incidents; scheduled upgrades; large unlocks, listings, delistings and regulatory rulings | Macro: **5–10 / 15–30**; scheduled upgrade or unlock: **30–60 / 30–60**; incident: **no reliable pre-window / at least 30–60 after verified stabilization** | Block new entries and cancel stale orders on a credible incident. Reduce exposure under a shared loss/liquidity limit. Do not treat a price on another exchange as proof that the affected venue is executable. |
| **Futures** | Underlying cash-session opens/closes; macro events; contract roll, first-notice and last-trade dates; settlement and expiry | Macro: **5–10 / 15–30**; roll/expiry: **30–60 / 30–60** around the relevant roll or settlement transition | Reduce or disable strategies whose signals depend on continuous-contract prices unless roll adjustment and liquidity migration are tested. Expiry/roll windows primarily call for execution and basis controls, not a generic directional bet. |
| **Single stocks** | Earnings, guidance and conference calls; trading halts; mergers, FDA/regulatory rulings and other material disclosures | Known release: **5–10 / at least 30**, then wait for continuous liquidity; halt or unscheduled disclosure: **no dependable pre-window / until reopening and quotes stabilize** | Default flat through earnings for ordinary scalpers, especially overnight. A stop cannot guarantee an exit through a gap or halt. If trading earnings, distinguish the fast numeric release from the later call and guidance. |

The FX evidence supports **event-specific rather than one-size-fits-all volatility windows**: a 24-hour FX model selected a small subset of releases and allowed their effects to decay over as much as 30 minutes, with substantial differences by event. Its estimated FOMC volatility multiplier was largest in the first five-minute post-release interval and declined thereafter. [FX Event Effects](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2411.16244v1?page=7) A separate E-mini futures study found higher price impact before releases and a return-volatility spike just after, while showing that the return–order-flow impulse response itself dissipated within a second. That sub-second result **does not mean spreads or volatility are normal again in a second**. [Futures Announcement Effects](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.06788v4?page=9)[Impulse Responses](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.06788v4?page=12)

For crypto macro risk, a recent study found that daily Fed repricing predicted Bitcoin’s five-day volatility in-sample, while CPI repricing predicted volatility for several altcoins; some out-of-sample results were positive for volatility forecasts. It did **not** establish an intraday directional entry or a profitable event-window trading rule. [Crypto Macro Channels](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.01431v1?page=1)[Out of Sample](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.01431v1?page=9)

## 2. Returns given up versus risk avoided

Flattening sacrifices any profitable move that happens during the lockout, including a strategy’s pre-release drift or fast post-release continuation. It also sacrifices the chance to enter at the most favorable point in the move. Cutting size instead preserves some participation while limiting the damage from a surprise, gap, spread expansion or adverse fill. For a short-horizon system, the right comparison is **net, executable return with and without the event gate**, including slippage and missed fills—not the raw event-window return.

The clearest earnings evidence illustrates the trade-off. [*Warp Speed Price Moves: Jumps after Earnings Announcements* (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962) finds jumps in more than 90% of sampled announcing stocks’ after-hours earnings sessions, but its surprise-based strategy’s positive no-friction average return was no longer statistically significant in 2016–2020 after even minor frictions such as spreads. [Jump Rates](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962v1?page=3)[Friction Result](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962v1?page=40) That suggests flattening can pay for itself when the presumed opportunity is mostly a jump that is expensive or impossible to trade cleanly.

A newer earnings study reports a more differentiated opportunity. [*Fast Numbers, Slow Language: Bridging Quantitative and Qualitative Earnings Signals* (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.29734) finds that numeric EPS surprise has its strongest opportunity shortly after the first confirmed actuals, while an LLM-scored earnings-call sentiment signal performs better at the next session open. In its out-of-sample test, EPS surprise at the “FirstActual” anchor had a 137.7 bp top-minus-bottom quintile spread and Sharpe of 1.81, but at next open the EPS-only version had Sharpe −0.31. The paper reports a next-open ECT sentiment strategy with a 153 bp quintile spread and Sharpe 2.28. Its reported costs are modest (1 bp each way), not a full broker- or order-book execution simulation, so these are candidate signals to reproduce—not guaranteed returns. [Earnings Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.29734v1?page=7) The practical lesson is not “always trade earnings”; it is “match the signal to its information-arrival speed.” Numeric surprise can be stale by the open, while management language may take longer to be interpreted.

For macro events, the evidence here is stronger for **risk state and liquidity** than for exploitable short-term direction. Takahashi’s E-mini study reports price impact increasing and return volatility spiking around releases, but does not establish a profitable directional release strategy. The FX volatility-selection paper offers a way to identify which events tend to elevate risk, not whether the surprise should be bought or sold. Those results favor flattening or reducing size for strategies without demonstrated alpha; they do not prove that all event trading loses money.

Several event ideas merit testing as **separate, tightly scoped strategies**:

- **FX surprise response:** use actual-minus-consensus surprises, not event labels alone; align with rates or currency-specific direction, and test the first seconds/minutes separately from the subsequent 15–30 minutes. You need timestamped consensus and actuals, executable bid/ask data, and a policy for revisions. The retrieved FX studies measure volatility or price discovery; they do not provide a validated consensus-surprise trading rule.
- **Announcement drift versus reversal:** evaluate pre-event positioning/drift, the initial jump, and post-jump continuation/reversal as distinct horizons. Estimate net returns by surprise sign and size, but condition entries on live spread, depth and price impact. The E-mini paper reports short-lag reversal in returns in general, but it is not evidence that every news jump reverses. [Return Reversal](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.06788v4?page=13)
- **Earnings fast/slow signals:** use the release’s numeric surprise for short intraday horizons and conference-call language for the following session. The cited earnings work reports a signal-speed separation; it also warns that a direct combination of the two signals did not automatically improve performance. You need precise timestamps, pre-release consensus, release text, transcript text, and realistic fills.
- **Crypto macro volatility scaling:** prediction-market repricing or macro news can be tested as a **position-sizing signal**, not presumed directional alpha. The Kalshi study’s Fed and CPI associations are horizon-specific and partly regime-dependent. Its daily horizon does not resolve whether a 5-minute strategy should trade the announcement.

## 3. Useful combinations of papers and methods

These are **proposed integrations**, not combinations already validated by the papers.

1. **FX event control plus policy-news signal:** combine [*What Events Matter for Exchange Rate Volatility?* (2024)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2411.16244) with [*Forecasting Intraday USD/CAD Exchange Rate with News-Derived Monetary-Policy Signals* (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.32773) and the liquidity-state idea from [*Returns and Order Flow Imbalances* (2025)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.06788). The FX volatility model would set an event- and time-of-day risk multiplier; the news model would supply a policy-communication feature; the live execution gate would suppress the directional entry when spread, depth or price impact is abnormal. Kodeih et al. found that event timing and targeted central-bank communication activity contributed more reliably than broad article volume; their USD/CAD study is hourly and does not test transaction-cost-adjusted profitability. [Signal Attribution](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.32773v1?page=9)[Limitations](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.32773v1?page=10)

2. **Earnings alpha plus execution and capacity controls:** combine [*Fast Numbers, Slow Language* (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.29734), [*Warp Speed Price Moves* (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.08962), and [*Financial Language Models as Applied AI Systems for News-Based Trading under Market Frictions* (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23703). Trade numeric surprises only at the first observable timestamp; consider call sentiment later; use the jump-test work to model after-hours discontinuity and the market-friction framework to impose timestamp eligibility, spreads, turnover and participation limits. This makes “fast number” alpha compete against a meaningful execution hurdle rather than a frictionless price return. It still needs broker-specific after-hours data: MFAST notes its execution evaluation is not a full order-book simulator. [System Limits](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.23703v1?page=36)

3. **Crypto macro risk plus exit layer and incident circuit breaker:** combine [*Do Prediction Markets Forecast Cryptocurrency Volatility?* (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.01431) with [*Optimal Stop-Loss and Take-Profit Parameterization for Autonomous Trading Agent Swarm* (2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150). Use matched macro repricing to reduce exposure when predicted volatility rises; calibrate exits on trade paths; add an independent exchange/protocol-incident kill switch at the shared risk layer. The stop-loss paper is a useful calibration template, **not strong proof of a universally optimal stop**: it uses over 900 historical trades, performs a large parameter search, and its final randomized evaluation weakens the temporal forward-test interpretation. [Exit Study Limits](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.27150v1?page=2)

4. **A common event gate across all agents:** apply the above event calendars alongside regime detection, intraday cost models and stress scenarios in one portfolio-level service. For every instrument, replay the same strategy with event gating on and off, with realistic bid/ask, slippage, gaps, halts, rejected orders and missed fills. Report both net return and tail loss by event type and regime. Do not let an agent’s confident signal override a global liquidity kill switch.

**Data gaps matter most for the requested class-by-class windows.** The papers retrieved here directly support scheduled FX volatility, index-futures macro microstructure, earnings jumps and some macro-linked crypto volatility. They do not establish optimal windows or net returns for oil inventory/OPEC, gold real-rate surprises, FX fixing, token unlocks, crypto exchange incidents, or futures roll/expiry. For those, begin with conservative gates, collect venue- and broker-level fills, and graduate an event strategy from “risk lockout” to “trade enabled” only after it demonstrates positive net results across multiple event cycles and market regimes.

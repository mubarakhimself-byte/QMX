ReguSim also shows how misleading an explanation can be: for one model, adding the trader’s rationale increased false acceptance of rejected orders from 6.2% to 14.8%; an explicit checking instruction reduced it to 1.6%. **For your system, I’d build a deterministic risk monitor with an LLM explanation layer, not an LLM risk controller.**

- **Keep measurement and alert triggering outside the LLM.** Reconcile positions, fills, open orders, realized/unrealized P&L, and exposures from authoritative broker and internal ledgers. Calculate limits, drawdowns, concentration, and cross-strategy/cross-asset exposure with deterministic code. The LLM can summarize a triggered event; it should not calculate the canonical risk state.
- **Alert on defined events, with evidence attached.** Use stable strategy, instrument, account, and timestamp IDs; show the breached threshold, observed value, source records, and relevant fill/log references. For “why are we losing?”, have the system first compute attribution—such as slippage, fees, sizing, exposure, or adverse market move—then ask the LLM to explain those measured components. Require it to distinguish confirmed facts from hypotheses and to abstain when evidence is missing.
- **Keep limit authority outside the agent.** Give the LLM read-only access to monitoring data and no brokerage, order, or risk-configuration write tools. It may draft a report or propose a *tighter* limit for human review; it must not edit the active limit. Enforce immutable hard caps and loss stops in a separate risk service that rejects agent-originated changes. Any exceptional relaxation should require a separate, authenticated human control-plane process—not an agent-generated approval.
- **Evaluate it as an alerting system, not a persuasive writer.** Replay timestamped incidents and inject known faults: stale prices, missing fills, duplicate orders, exposure aggregation errors, limit breaches, and strategy-specific P&L deterioration. Report event-level precision and recall, false alerts per strategy/day, detection lead time, and end-to-end latency (including data ingestion and delivery). For diagnosis, score root-cause accuracy and whether each claim is supported by cited records; track unsupported-claim rate. Measure operator outcomes—time to acknowledge and investigate, missed incidents, and losses or drawdown in controlled replay—against the same workflow without the agent. The papers above do **not** establish improvements on those operator or loss outcomes.

The clearest measurable benefits so far are narrower: trace systems make decisions easier to inspect, benchmarks expose specific reasoning and detection failures, and simulated external controls can contain certain failures. The evidence does not yet show that LLM agents reliably give operators earlier warning or avoid losses in live, multi-asset trading. ## Aurelle

**The three most useful 2026 papers are [ReguSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19974) for alert-quality evidence, [Oculi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28944) for separating LLM analysis from deterministic computation, and [NEXTFUND](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11141) for traceable portfolio decisions.** None establishes that an LLM monitor catches live trading problems earlier or prevents losses. ## 1. ReguSim: the clearest test of LLM alerts

**[ReguSim: Evaluating LLM Agent Rule Grounding in Financial Compliance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19974) (2026)** tests two distinct tasks: recognizing suspicious synthetic trading records and independently checking whether a submitted order violates executable rules.

The monitoring path is: show the model a marked transaction with up to four neighboring records on either side; provide timestamps, actor IDs, trade details, and available order status; ask it to classify whether the marked record belongs to a manipulation pattern; then compare its binary judgment with the scenario generator’s label. It tests five patterns, including spoofing, wash trading, and churning. The model returns a positive/negative judgment, pattern type, severity, rationale, and evidence. In the separate order-audit task, the model sees rule, state, and order information, optionally with the trader’s rationale, an instruction to check carefully, or the execution engine’s decision. The audit calls are independent; they do not share the trader’s conversation history. [Monitoring Setup](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.19974v2?page=3)

On 900 synthetic marked targets, the best overall LLM F1 scores were 70.9% for Kimi K3 and 70.0% for Claude Fable. Kimi had 91.7% recall but 59.5% precision: roughly 40% of its positive alerts were false positives, measured as the false-discovery share, not the false-positive rate over all negatives. Claude’s precision and recall were more balanced at 75.1% and 75.3%. A logistic-regression detector trained on separate, labeled examples from the same visible evidence achieved 89.2% F1; that is not a like-for-like zero-shot comparison, but it is a strong reason to benchmark an LLM against simpler detectors. [Detector Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.19974v2?page=6)

The bridge test also exposes a specific failure: Claude Fable’s false acceptance of rejected orders was 6.2% when shown state and action, 14.8% when the trader’s rationale was added, and 1.6% when an explicit checking instruction was added. [Rationale Effect](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.19974v2?page=8) The paper reports no monitor latency, 24/7 inference cost, or live trading losses avoided. Its records are synthetic or historical test traces, not continuous multi-asset portfolio surveillance. Its name-control experiment also shows that detection can depend on presentation cues: removing role-revealing actor names lowered F1 by 8–18 percentage points. [Identity Sensitivity](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.19974v2?page=1)

## 2. Oculi: a useful compute/LLM boundary, but not a live alerting system

**[Oculi: A Conversational Agentic Platform for Automated Credit Risk Analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28944) (2026)** is the clearest design example of an LLM directing analysis while deterministic tools do the data work. It is a credit-risk analytics system, not a trading monitor.

Its workflow starts with an analyst asking a question about mortgage-portfolio risk. A ReAct agent uses conversation memory to keep the metric, filters, and prior analysis in context, then calls tools through Model Context Protocol servers. The tools query portfolio data and calculate segment metrics, period-over-period changes, z-scores, and materiality. For candidate segments that merit deeper analysis, the LLM suggests which features to examine next; a validator checks feature names and drops invalid suggestions. Deterministic code computes and ranks the resulting segments, while the interface presents analysis cards, charts, and explanatory text. The LLM does not calculate the risk metrics or execute arbitrary code. [Agent and Tools](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.28944v1?page=4) [Segment Discovery](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.28944v1?page=6)

Its headline result was 63% coverage of material bivariate segments found by a constrained brute-force reference, while evaluating 162 of 220 candidate combinations. But the evaluation used a nine-feature mortgage dataset, and the paper says its evaluated “LLM-guided” feature selector was a simulated heuristic matching production priority logic—not live LLM inference. It is therefore evidence about the discovery design, not a measured LLM alert recall rate. [Coverage Result](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.28944v1?page=9) Invalid feature suggestions were 23.8% of suggestions in a 20-response test, and the validator dropped them; that is a useful input-validation result, not a general hallucination rate for diagnoses. [Suggestion Validation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.28944v1?page=10)

The prototype’s measured pipeline latency was 20.3 seconds on its evaluation setup; the authors estimate 10–15 seconds in a parallelized production setup, with 1–3 seconds for an LLM round trip. It was analyst-triggered, not a 24/7 alert service. The paper reports no precision/recall for alerts, no false-alert rate, no avoided losses, and no continuous-run cost. It also explicitly describes its segment findings as associative rather than causal and leaves temporal trend detection for future work. [Latency Estimate](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.28944v1?page=10) [Limitations](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.28944v1?page=11)

## 3. NEXTFUND: strong trace infrastructure, no LLM alert evaluator

**[NEXTFUND: A Unified Performance Tracking Platform for Agentic Portfolio Management](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11141) (2026)** is the most relevant of the three for watching decisions and reconstructing what happened. It is a tracking platform, not an LLM risk-monitoring agent.

It creates a point-in-time market view from prices, macro snapshots, and timestamped news; specialist agents produce structured signals; a decision manager combines them under explicit portfolio constraints; and the resulting proposals become trades in the evaluation layer. The platform writes observations, analyst outputs, decisions, justifications, execution records, and portfolio snapshots to persistent, time-aligned traces. Reviewers can move from aggregate performance to a day, ticker, signal, decision, and execution record. [Pipeline and Traces](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.11141v1?page=3) [Persistent Records](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.11141v1?page=4)

The paper compares eight model backbones across U.S., China A-share, and Hong Kong equities in Q1 and Q2 2026, reporting return, Sharpe, volatility, drawdown, and turnover. Its diagnostic case study found about 92.9% agreement between comparable analyst signals but only 36.4% agreement on final ticker-day actions—evidence that traces can reveal divergence between analysis and action. It does **not** evaluate an alert classifier, alert precision/recall, time-to-detect, or whether operator inspection reduced losses. The authors also warn that textual rationales may be incomplete or only loosely aligned with model computation. [Signal and Action Agreement](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.11141v1?page=5) [Rationale Limitation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.11141v1?page=7)

## What newer work challenges

These papers are contemporaneous work, not a documented chain of direct follow-on citations. Two later 2026 papers are especially useful checks on what the three establish:

- **[RiskReason: A Benchmark for Verifiable Financial Risk Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01236) (2026)** challenges the idea that strong financial reasoning scores alone establish reliable monitoring. Its synthetic benchmark tests changing financial states, conditional rules, constraints, and composed scenarios. The best configuration scored 90.2% strict accuracy, yet the paper still identifies failures in rule application, state propagation, and feasible remediation. It does not test live monitoring or alert timing. [Benchmark Findings](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.01236v1?page=1)
- **[Trading Agents or Market Crashers? Dissecting Robustness and Security Failures in Academic Financial LLM Trading Schemes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19705) (2026)** challenges reliance on the LLM as the safety mechanism. Its survey and simulated-market experiments argue for an external fast halt path and hard limits outside the model. In its simulation, those controls removed crashes caused or amplified by the tested agents; that is evidence for external containment in that simulated setup, **not** evidence that an LLM monitoring agent itself prevents losses. [External Controls](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19705v3?page=20)

## A concrete workflow for one operator

I would make the deterministic risk service the source of truth and invoke the LLM on **events or scheduled summaries**, rather than on every tick. Let the service reconcile positions, fills, open orders, P&L, exposure, and active limits. It emits a structured incident whenever a deterministic threshold, reconciliation check, or data-quality condition fires. The LLM receives a read-only, timestamped incident bundle—not raw unrestricted database access.

1. **Gather the incident bundle.** Include the triggering limit and measured value; positions and exposure snapshots; relevant fills and open orders; realized and unrealized P&L; strategy and instrument identifiers; timestamped logs; market-data freshness; and the deterministic service’s reconciliation results. Preserve source IDs and timestamps so every explanation can link back to evidence.

2. **Use narrow, read-only tools.** Let the agent retrieve referenced records, query approved aggregates, and compare incident-window values with a baseline. Keep canonical calculations—PnL attribution, exposure aggregation, limit evaluation, and fill reconciliation—in deterministic services. The LLM may request a re-computation, but it cannot replace the calculated result with its own arithmetic.

3. **Separate facts from diagnosis.** Require a structured response with: incident summary; facts with evidence IDs; likely causes ranked as hypotheses; missing or conflicting evidence; the next check for the operator; and an optional draft report. Root-cause categories should be bounded—such as slippage, fees, sizing, market move, stale mark, missing fill, or concentration—and unsupported causes should be labeled unknown, not narrated as facts.

4. **Make proposals non-executable.** The agent can draft a proposed **tighter** limit with a rationale, but cannot call the risk-configuration or broker APIs. Do not expose any tool that edits live limits. A separate human workflow may review a proposal; any actual limit change should be authenticated, logged, and independently validated outside the agent.

5. **Treat logs and news as untrusted input.** Quote them as data, never as instructions. Do not let text found in logs select tools or alter the system prompt. Validate all tool arguments against schemas; escape or isolate retrieved text; and test explicitly for prompt injection in log lines, news, and strategy-generated messages. Keep an immutable audit record of the input bundle, model output, tool calls, human decision, and final disposition.

6. **Measure operational value before trusting it.** Replay timestamped incidents and report event-level precision and recall, false alerts per strategy-day, time from first detectable evidence to alert, p50/p95 end-to-end latency, and cost per incident and per day. Score diagnosis separately: verified root-cause accuracy, evidence citation correctness, and unsupported-claim rate. Compare operator time-to-acknowledge and investigate against the same cases without the agent. Only a controlled replay or shadow/live study against a defined baseline can establish earlier detection or avoided losses.

For an around-the-clock trading system, the design implication is straightforward: **use deterministic code to detect and contain risk; use the LLM to make verified evidence easier for the operator to understand.**

## Aurelle

**None of the 2025–2026 papers establishes a complete LLM monitor that catches live, multi-asset trading problems earlier and reduces realized losses.** The closest evidence is narrower: simulated kill switches can contain some market cascades; a crypto trading system with five-minute risk governance reports better backtested risk-adjusted outcomes; and monitoring benchmarks measure detection accuracy or diagnostic trace quality—not operators’ real-world time saved.

## 1. What to watch differently by asset class

Treat these as **design requirements to validate against your brokers and venues**, not as claims that a paper has benchmarked each asset class. A shared monitor should normalize exposure and P&L across strategies, but apply asset-specific calendars, contract conventions, data-quality checks, and event triggers.

- **FX:** Watch regional session handovers, weekend gaps, holidays, fixing windows, and thin-liquidity periods. Track currency exposure across the whole book—not only pair-level notional—and flag broker-to-broker price divergence, stale quotes, and spread widening. Include swap/rollover charges and each broker’s own rollover time. A pair’s trading hours may look continuous while liquidity and financing conditions change sharply.
- **Stock indices:** Distinguish cash-index hours from futures trading and after-hours moves; track opening/closing auctions, futures–cash basis, constituent-market closures, rebalances, and dividend effects. Make venue-specific circuit breakers and price limits explicit. An index mark can remain stale or calculated while constituent markets are closed.
- **Metals:** Separate broker “spot” instruments from exchange futures, and monitor session handovers, fixing windows, futures basis, contract specifications, and margin changes. For gold and silver, USD and rates can be useful context signals, but they should not substitute for instrument-level prices and broker position data.
- **Energy:** Give special treatment to futures expiry and roll, first-notice/delivery dates, contract-month liquidity, storage and inventory events, and exchange price limits. Validate unit, contract multiplier, and price representation carefully; the monitor must distinguish a calendar-spread or roll loss from an outright market loss. Include the possibility that delivery mechanics or abnormal prices make a seemingly ordinary mark misleading.
- **Crypto:** Assume **no quiet hours**: run reconciliation and risk checks continuously, including weekends and holidays. Track venue-specific funding rates, liquidation or margin events, mark/index/last-price differences, exchange outages, stablecoin or chain incidents, and cross-venue basis. A single exchange feed should not be treated as a universal price or position truth.
- **Futures across asset classes:** Track contract month, expiry, roll, first notice, settlement type, multiplier, tick size, variation margin, and limit-up/limit-down status. Use the exchange calendar and broker’s position/working-order records, not a generic symbol mapping. A roll can look like a sharp position or P&L discontinuity if old and new contracts are not linked correctly.
- **Single stocks:** Watch halts and resumptions, opening/closing auctions, earnings and corporate actions, splits, dividends, borrow/locate status, short-sale restrictions, and venue-specific fills. Confirm whether the price is adjusted, whether a partial fill is still open, and whether the broker’s displayed position includes pending activity.

Across all asset classes, make **data freshness and reconciliation first-class alerts**: a broker-feed disconnect, stale mark, unexplained position mismatch, late or duplicated fill, or disagreement between the risk service and broker should raise a distinct “state uncertain” alert. On an uncertain state, the LLM should explain the discrepancy—not infer a reliable exposure from incomplete data.

## 2. What the papers show about performance

The evidence splits into three categories: detection scores, system-level trading outcomes, and traceability. Those should not be conflated.

**Alert quality.** [ReguSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19974) (2026) is the most direct trading-surveillance evaluation. On 900 synthetic marked records, the highest overall LLM F1 scores were 70.9% and 70.0%. The higher-recall system had 91.7% recall but only 59.5% precision—many of its positive alerts were false positives. An evidence-matched logistic-regression detector trained on separate examples scored 89.2% F1, so the LLM should not be presumed the best detector. The study reports neither time-to-detect nor real trading losses avoided. [Detector Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.19974v2?page=6)

**False-alert triage.** A complementary, non-financial-domain paper, [Positive-Unlabeled Learning for Agent Safety False Alarm Auditing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02925) (2026), does not make the underlying safety monitor more accurate; it ranks its existing alarms for human review. On its agent-safety datasets, the method recovered 33.3% more false alarms than the strongest evaluated PU baseline at a 5% review budget, and its external validation found 42.4% more at that budget. This is a promising pattern for reducing review burden, but it is not validated on trading alerts. [Review Yield](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.02925v1?page=7)

**Traceability and diagnosis.** [NEXTFUND](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11141) (2026) records time-aligned observations, intermediate signals, portfolio decisions, rationales, and executions. Its case study found about 92.9% agreement among comparable analyst signals but only 36.4% agreement in final ticker-day actions. That makes decision divergence inspectable; it is **not** evidence that an LLM diagnosis was correct or that an operator reacted sooner. [Signal and Action Agreement](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.11141v1?page=5)

[Oculi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28944) (2026) found 63% coverage of material bivariate mortgage-risk segments while evaluating 162 of 220 candidate combinations. This suggests a way to focus analysts’ attention on plausible risk cohorts, but the test used nine features, and the paper says its evaluated feature-selection logic was a simulated heuristic rather than live LLM inference. Its 20.3-second pipeline timing is not evidence of time saved for a trading operator. [Coverage Result](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.28944v1?page=9)

**Loss and risk outcomes.** The closest quantitative evidence for improved risk outcomes comes from **trading systems with a monitoring/control layer**, rather than passive LLM monitors. [SwingGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12988) (2026) uses daily strategy generation plus a five-minute crypto risk controller. On its 2024–2025 BTC/ETH backtest, its capped joint portfolio reported a Sharpe ratio of 2.98, 37.16% return, and 6.44% maximum drawdown; the post-hoc-risk variant reported 2.79, 35.03%, and 8.62%. Removing downstream risk governance from the post-hoc variant worsened those figures to 1.87, 23.02%, and 14.19%. These are backtested strategy outcomes, not a controlled trial of operator alerts. In one illustrative event, it closed a 30% BTC position before an 8.82% decline to a subsequent local trough—an example, not an estimate of average loss avoided. [Controlled Ablation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.12988v1?page=7)

[Trading Agents or Market Crashers?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19705) (2026) measures the possible cost of **not** having an external fast path. In a simulated market, 400 agents responding to a fake headline produced an average 9.6% drawdown; the fast-path control reduced it to 1.1%. But the threshold also produced false halts in calm periods: on average 0.6 per hour for 400 DeepSeek-policy agents and 2.4 per hour for Gemini-policy agents at the tested setting. This is a simulated cascade experiment, not evidence of realized savings in a live portfolio. The paper’s main implication is that any kill action must be independent of LLM inference, with false-halt rates measured and thresholds tuned. [Control Tradeoff](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19705v3?page=19)

**Signals worth considering.** Research suggests candidates for deterministic features, not universally validated triggers. [SwingGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12988) uses drawdown, volatility/ATR, momentum/trend, exposure, on-chain/news anomalies, source diversity, and six-hour accumulation to trigger deeper diagnosis; it retrieves similar prior risk snapshots and uses staged recovery. [Risk Diagnosis](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.12988v1?page=3) [Trading Agents or Market Crashers?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19705) discusses VIX, correlated-asset drops, order-flow toxicity, liquidity stress, and ETF–underlying divergence as possible early-warning readings. These are recommendations, not proof that the signals forecast losses in the user’s cross-asset book. [Market Stress Signals](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19705v3?page=21)

**Where noise, delay, and cost enter.** ReguSim shows the alert precision/recall tradeoff; the market-crash simulation shows that a fast-path threshold can create repeated calm-market halts. Oculi’s invalid feature suggestions (23.8% in a small 20-response test) were filtered, illustrating why an LLM proposal needs validation rather than direct execution. [Suggestion Validation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.28944v1?page=10) [Write Once, Check Many](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24483) finds a way to reduce repeated audit-token cost: for eight SHADE-Arena policies, it reports about 4.9× fewer tokens using one reusable narrative ledger than rereading the full log for each policy. It also warns that ledgers can omit facts from long tool outputs or messages, and that missing evidence then affects every later check. This is a promising audit-cost technique, not a 24/7 trading-monitor cost study. [Ledger Cost](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.24483v1?page=2)

For **alpha decay**, [Can We Stop the Backtest Yet?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03384) introduces an anytime-valid, cost-aware ledger for strategy promotion. In a 15-basis-point injected alternative, its detection power was 70.1% with median crossing session 2,327 of 2,868; repeated unadjusted tests had 94.5% familywise false-promotion rate in the null replay. That controls adaptive **promotion** error, not live alpha decay. The authors explicitly warn that historical evidence does not certify that a once-profitable rule remains profitable now. Fresh, funded revalidation epochs are needed for current-regime authorization. [Power and Delay](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.03384v1?page=5)

## 3. Useful combinations

These are **proposed combinations**, not integrated systems already validated in the cited work.

- **Fast protection and staged recovery:** combine [FARSIGHT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19705) + [SwingGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12988) + [ReguSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19974). Let deterministic thresholds for drawdown, spread/volatility shock, price-feed disagreement, exposure, and stale state freeze new risk without an LLM call. Keep hysteresis, a minimum hold, and staged re-enable checks outside the LLM. Use the LLM only to assemble evidence and explain the incident; require human approval for any exceptional return from halt. This combines FARSIGHT’s simulation-backed fast-path rationale with SwingGuard’s persistent states and ReguSim’s finding that fluent explanations can mislead an auditor.
- **Control alert fatigue without suppressing the dangerous alerts:** combine [ReguSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19974) + [Positive-Unlabeled Learning for Agent Safety False Alarm Auditing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02925) + [Write Once, Check Many](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24483). Preserve the original deterministic alert; rank low-confidence or redundant cases for review using a separately evaluated false-alarm triage model; create a factual, append-only incident ledger once, then check it against multiple policies or later questions. Pair that with an explicit alert budget or “burn-rate” view—how quickly the operator’s review queue is filling—without letting that budget silence critical limit, reconciliation, or kill-switch alerts. The false-alarm ranking and reusable ledger have results in agent-safety tests, not finance, so validate them on labeled trading incidents before relying on them.
- **Monitor active strategy viability without confusing it with hard risk control:** combine [NEXTFUND](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11141) + [Can We Stop the Backtest Yet?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03384) + [FARSIGHT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19705). NEXTFUND-style traces provide strategy-versioned signals, decisions, fills, and outcomes; a Promote-style ledger tracks costs and requires a fresh, pre-registered evaluation epoch before continued strategy authorization; FARSIGHT-style external caps and kill switches still enforce immediate risk limits. The LLM can draft an alpha-decay explanation and a **tighter-limit or pause proposal**, but only a human or separate deterministic change-control service can approve changes. The e-value gate is a promotion/revalidation method, not a turnkey detector of rapid live decay.
- **Keep a reusable audit trail for every human decision:** combine [NEXTFUND](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11141) + [Write Once, Check Many](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24483) + [ReguSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19974). Record the original evidence and structured event facts once; let the operator ask new questions over that evidence; then independently check the LLM’s claims against fills, limits, and execution decisions. Retain the raw source records too: a summary ledger is efficient but cannot recover facts it failed to preserve.

In each combination, distinguish **alerting** (“a deterministic condition fired”), **diagnosis** (“these records suggest a cause”), and **authorization** (“a human approved a change”). The LLM can help with the second and draft materials for the third; it should not own the first or execute the third.

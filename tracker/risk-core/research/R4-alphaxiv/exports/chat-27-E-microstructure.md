## Aurelle

For a $500 account, use microstructure first as a **permission-to-trade gate**, then as an input to **cost-aware sizing**—not as a reason to add leverage. If a strategy’s expected edge does not clear spread, commissions, likely slippage and adverse selection, it should not enter; if minimum tradable size breaches the account’s risk budget, it should not trade.

### What to measure—and what it means

The quoted spread is only the visible part of trading cost. Track the **quoted spread** and the **effective spread** paid on fills; compare fills with the midprice shortly afterward to estimate **realized spread** and adverse selection. Spread compensation can reflect order-processing costs, inventory risk and trading against better-informed flow. A tight quote does not guarantee a cheap fill if it is stale or liquidity disappears.

Depth is executable quantity at each price, not a promise that it will remain there. Measure depth near the touch and estimate how much of it your order would consume. **Order-flow imbalance (OFI)**—including changes in best-price queues from trades, limit orders and cancellations—can help describe short-horizon pressure. But imbalance is not a standalone direction signal: it is venue-specific, can change quickly, and may be distorted by cancellations or hidden liquidity.

**Toxicity** asks whether liquidity providers are systematically being filled just before prices move against them. VPIN is one proposed volume-bucket measure of order-flow toxicity, but I would not use it as a universal alarm or size multiplier. For retail execution, a more direct check is the strategy’s own fill-to-midprice markout at several short horizons, conditioned on spread, session, volatility and venue. Intraday liquidity commonly varies by session; around scheduled news, spreads can widen and depth can withdraw. Treat those as separate regimes rather than averaging them into an all-day baseline.

The square-root impact law is a useful empirical description for some larger parent orders relative to market volume; it is not a reliable calculator for the impact of every tiny retail order. On a $500 account, **spread, commissions, minimum contract size, stop slippage and venue rules are often more consequential**. Estimate impact from your own order size, instrument and venue where possible.

### Practical rules for entry, size and stops

1. **Build a cost hurdle per instrument and venue.** Estimate round-trip spread, fees, slippage and—where relevant—funding or financing. Normalize spread by the instrument’s tick value and by a short-horizon volatility measure. Compare current costs with that instrument’s own session-specific history, not with another asset’s spread.

2. **Gate entries on executable conditions.** Allow an entry only when estimated net edge exceeds expected all-in cost with a buffer. Block or pause entries if spread is unusually wide for that symbol and hour, near-touch depth is insufficient for the intended order, or a news/liquidity event makes expected slippage unreliable. Treat a high toxicity reading as a reason to inspect or tighten these gates, not as proof of direction.

3. **Size to loss at the stop, including execution costs.** For a strategy allocation with dollar risk budget \(R\), a useful bound is:
   $$
   Q \leq \frac{R}{D V + C + S}
   $$
   Here \(Q\) is position size, \(D\) is stop distance in price units, \(V\) is dollar value per price unit per unit of position, and \(C\) and \(S\) are estimated round-trip costs and stop slippage per unit. If the stop is specified from the midprice, include the spread in the cost estimate. Use the broker’s actual contract and minimum-size rules.

   As a conservative **starting policy**, cap the combined open risk across all strategies at roughly **0.25–0.5% of equity**—about **$1.25–$2.50** on $500—and give each strategy only a fraction of that shared budget. That is a risk-policy suggestion, not a market-microstructure result. If the smallest allowable order exceeds its allocation, skip the trade; do not round up. Aggregate exposures that are economically related, such as several USD positions or multiple index strategies, rather than treating each bot as independent.

4. **Set stops from the trade thesis, then check whether they are viable.** Choose the stop from the strategy’s invalidation point and the instrument’s normal short-horizon noise. Then check whether spread and likely slippage are large relative to that distance. If normal execution noise could consume the stop, reject the trade or reduce size. Widen a stop only if the thesis supports the wider invalidation point, and recalculate size accordingly. A stop is not a guarantee of the fill price, especially around news or gaps.

5. **Calibrate by venue and time of day.** Keep separate baselines for each instrument, venue and session, including scheduled-news periods. Re-estimate them from live or representative fills; don’t transfer a threshold learned on a major futures contract to a CFD, a crypto exchange, or a different hour. Because humans promote strategies to live, make spread, depth, slippage and markout reports part of promotion review—and keep a hard account-level loss and exposure limit outside the individual agents.

**Venue distinctions matter.** Exchange depth describes orders on a particular exchange or contract; it is not necessarily all available liquidity across venues. Crypto order books are exchange-specific and fragmented. Spot FX is decentralized, so there is no single global book. CFDs are broker-issued products: their quotes, displayed liquidity, execution and financing depend on the provider, and the displayed quote may not represent a central exchange order book. Therefore, calibrate against the exact venue and instrument the strategy will trade. A signal computed from one exchange’s depth should not be treated as a measurement of another venue’s executable liquidity.

## Aurelle

**The most useful three are OFI/MLOFI for short-horizon pressure, an event-impact model for estimating adverse selection and execution effects, and the square-root impact rule as a coarse size penalty—not as a retail order-size calculator.** These methods answer different questions: OFI asks which way the book is leaning; event-impact models ask how order-book events translate into later price movement; the square-root rule asks how impact tends to grow with order size relative to market volume. None directly specifies a safe stop distance.

## 1. OFI and MLOFI: the best candidate for a short-horizon signal

The foundation is [*The Price Impact of Order Book Events*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1011.6402) (Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov, 2014); [*Multi-Level Order-Flow Imbalance in a Limit Order Book*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1907.06230) (Xu, Gould & Howison, 2019) extends it to more book levels.

**What it computes.** At the best bid and ask, OFI adds the signed size changes caused by events: limit orders added, market orders executed, and cancellations. A bid strengthening or ask weakening contributes positive buying pressure; a bid weakening or ask strengthening contributes negative pressure. MLOFI repeats this calculation at each of the first $M$ populated price levels, returning a vector rather than one scalar. A model then estimates the relation between that vector and midprice change.

**Inputs and output.** Ideally, use timestamped book updates—including price, queue size, adds, executions and cancellations—from the *same venue* you trade. The output is an OFI number, or an MLOFI vector; after fitting a model, it can produce an expected midprice change in ticks over the chosen sampling window. The 2019 MLOFI study used Nasdaq order-book data and principally 10-second windows, with regressions re-estimated in 30-minute blocks. It found that adding depth improved out-of-sample fit for all six stocks studied; for its 10-level ridge regressions, reported RMSE reductions versus best-level OFI ranged from 15% to 74%, depending on the stock. Those are improvements in explaining **contemporaneous** price changes, not proof of net profitable next-trade forecasts. [OFI baseline](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1907.06230?page=2) [MLOFI results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1907.06230?page=26) [Conclusion](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1907.06230?page=29)

**Does it carry a tradeable signal?** Potentially—but distinguish “explains a price change that just occurred” from “predicts a price change after the feature is known.” The original OFI result, as summarized in the MLOFI paper, reported mean in-sample $R^2$ of about 65% for contemporaneous price changes using 10-second windows, versus about 32% for trade imbalance alone. That is strong explanatory power, not an out-of-sample trading edge. [OFI setup](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1907.06230?page=2) For scalping, OFI or MLOFI is most plausible when calculated from a timely, sufficiently complete exchange book and tested against *future* returns at the actual decision latency, after spread, fees and fill markouts. The 2019 paper supports using multiple levels as useful explanatory features; it does not establish a universal forecast horizon or a retail-profit threshold.

**Where it fails and how to use it.** A CFD quote or market-maker quote is not an exchange order book: its queue changes and apparent depth may describe only that provider’s quoting, not market-wide supply. Crypto depth is fragmented across exchanges; FX has no single consolidated central book. A feed containing only top-of-book quotes cannot faithfully reconstruct queue adds, cancellations and executions, so label any proxy as a proxy. Thin depth or news spikes can make a lagged signal stale just when it appears largest. And at tiny order sizes, better prediction may be economically irrelevant if the spread and minimum contract size dominate.

For your system, use OFI/MLOFI as an **entry veto or modest confirmation**, not an instruction to lever up: permit an entry only when the signal survives latency and execution-cost tests; cap size using the strategy’s independent dollar-risk budget. Don’t set the stop equal to an OFI-derived price move. Set it at the strategy’s invalidation level, then reject or reduce the trade if spread and likely slippage are too large relative to that distance.

## 2. Event-impact models: useful for adverse selection and execution-aware risk

[*The Price Impact of Order Book Events: Market Orders, Limit Orders and Cancellations*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/0904.0900) (Eisler, Bouchaud & Kockelkoren, 2010) is the most useful of these three for asking whether recent flow and liquidity state make the next price move or fill more dangerous.

**What it computes, step by step.** It classifies events at the best quotes—market orders, limit-order arrivals and cancellations, including whether they change the best price—and signs them by expected price direction. It measures event-type correlations and response functions over event-time lags, then estimates each event type’s “bare” or conditional impact. For small-tick stocks, it adds a regression model for how past event flow predicts changes in gaps behind the best quotes. The result is an estimated event-impact response over future event lags, plus forecasts of expected price changes conditioned on recent event history. [Event definitions](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/0904.0900?page=7) [Impact forecasts](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/0904.0900?page=17)

**Inputs and output.** The clean input is event-level best-bid/ask prices and queue sizes, trades, limit-order additions and cancellations, with timestamps. The paper uses event time rather than a fixed clock interval; in its sample, events arrived roughly every 0.1–0.6 seconds on average across stocks. Its output is a lag-dependent expected impact in ticks, or an expected next-event price change. It explicitly notes that events on other venues and deeper in the book are unobserved; their effects are folded into “dressed” estimates. [Data limits](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/0904.0900?page=6) [Event rates](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/0904.0900?page=8)

**Signal and limitations.** The paper reports that signed event correlations and return autocorrelation are short-ranged over roughly 10–100 events, corresponding typically to about 10 seconds in its stocks. It also reports that a model using recent events and gap dynamics produced forecasts that tracked realized conditional price changes reasonably well. That is evidence for short-lived conditional signal in those data, not a retail backtest result or a universal decay constant. [Event persistence](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/0904.0900?page=8) The authors caution that the book description is incomplete and that activity on other trading platforms “dresses” observed impacts; they also separate large-tick from small-tick behavior. [Fragmentation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/0904.0900?page=6) That makes the framework especially unsuitable for blindly treating one CFD quote stream as a complete market.

For a retail strategy, the practical output is not “add $X risk when the forecast is positive.” It is: when recent event flow predicts a short-lived move against a passive fill, either disallow that entry, use a more conservative fill assumption, or reduce size. Around scheduled news, recalibrate or disable the model: the paper’s event-flow framework is not a substitute for modelling news-driven repricing, gaps or broker execution behavior.

## 3. Square-root impact: a size sanity check, not a scalper signal

The [square-root market-impact rule](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1412.0141) is associated with metaorders—large parent orders split into smaller executions—not with a single tiny retail order.

**What it computes.** In its common form, estimated impact scales as volatility times the square root of order size divided by market volume: $I \approx Y\sigma\sqrt{Q/V}$. Here $Q$ is the parent order, $V$ is market volume over the relevant execution horizon, $\sigma$ is a volatility scale, and $Y$ is an empirical coefficient to calibrate. The output is an estimated price impact, not a probability of direction. Later work continues to debate its mechanism and scope, including [*Beyond the square root: Evidence for logarithmic dependence of market impact on size and participation rate*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1412.2152).

**Inputs, fit and failure modes.** It needs consistent definitions of parent-order size, market volume, execution horizon and volatility, estimated for the instrument and venue. It is therefore a poor direct fit for an account that trades many unrelated CFDs, crypto venues, FX products and stocks unless each is calibrated separately. It can be especially misleading at tiny size, where spread, fees, minimum contract size and discrete ticks may dominate; and during news, when the historical volatility and volume relation may not describe the next fill.

Use it only as a **size sanity check**: ask whether your order is negligible or material relative to the venue’s activity, and stress the assumed slippage if the market is thin. For most $500-account entries, measured spread and actual stop/fill slippage should control the risk budget before a metaorder impact estimate does. The square-root rule supplies no directional entry signal and no stop placement rule.

## What I would deploy first

For a multi-strategy retail account, I would prioritize **venue-specific spread and fill-cost gates, then a latency-tested OFI signal where genuine order-book data exists, then event-conditioned slippage estimates**. I would not make VPIN one of the top three controls: it is a toxicity indicator, not a reliable universal entry or sizing signal. VPIN-style calculations can flag unusual buy/sell volume imbalance, but trade signing and volume bucketing are problematic when the feed is incomplete or fragmented; a broker’s CFD ticks are not exchange trade flow. A better toxicity check for this system is its own **fill-to-midprice markout**, grouped by instrument, venue, session and news regime.

Finally, neither these papers nor the measurements give a defensible universal stop distance. For each strategy, choose the stop from the thesis, size the position so the stop loss **plus spread and plausible slippage** fits its dollar-risk allocation, and skip the trade if the venue’s minimum size is too large. On a $500 account, the allocation must also be shared across correlated strategies: ten agents trading related USD or index exposure do not create ten independent risk budgets.

## 2. Performance: safety filters can also screen out winners

The empirical evidence is much stronger for **describing or predicting short-horizon price changes in exchange-book data** than for proving that a retail gate improves net returns.

For example, [*The Price Impact of Order Book Events*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1011.6402) (Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov, 2014) reports that best-quote OFI has a strong contemporaneous relationship with midprice changes in its stock sample: the later [*Multi-Level Order-Flow Imbalance in a Limit Order Book*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1907.06230) summarizes its average $R^2$ as about 65% for OFI, versus about 32% for trade imbalance. Those are explanatory fit statistics, **not** a result that an OFI entry filter earned 65% more or avoided a known fraction of losses. MLOFI’s reported improvements in out-of-sample RMSE similarly concern price-change prediction, not a complete trading strategy after costs.

The crucial trade-off is:

- **Safety benefit:** A spread/depth/markout gate can avoid trades with high expected transaction cost, fragile stop execution or adverse fills.
- **Growth cost:** It can also reject profitable opportunities, delay entry until the signal has passed, or make the strategy trade so rarely that fixed costs and estimation error dominate.
- **Evidence gap:** The papers above do not give the counterfactual for this retail system—how many trades each gate would skip, how much winning edge it would discard, and how much slippage or loss it would prevent across these instruments. I would not infer that from a regression statistic.

Measure both sides in a **shadow evaluation** before live promotion. For every candidate trade, record whether each gate would permit it, then compare permitted and blocked trades at the same decision time using realistic venue fills, fees, financing, slippage and stop execution. Report at least: number and fraction of trades blocked; net P&L and tail loss on blocked versus permitted trades; missed winners; realized spread and short-horizon markout; and performance by session and event regime. For a $500 portfolio of many agents, also test shared exposure: a gate that improves one strategy can still increase portfolio risk if other agents take the same side.

A reasonable policy is asymmetric: **hard-block objectively untradeable conditions**—such as spread or minimum size making the risk budget impossible—while requiring stronger evidence before using noisy OFI or toxicity readings to suppress otherwise valid trades. The latter should begin as a shadow label, not a live veto.

## 3. Useful combinations: make each method do one job

These are concrete combinations I would test. They are proposed integrations; the cited papers do not jointly validate them as a package.

1. **OFI/MLOFI + a session-aware cost model + a hard risk budget.** Combine [Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov’s OFI](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1011.6402) and [Xu, Gould & Howison’s MLOFI](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1907.06230) with [Admati & Pfleiderer’s intraday trading/volume model](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/1.1.3). Use book imbalance as a short-horizon feature only where exchange-level data exists; estimate execution cost against the instrument’s own session baseline; then size by stop loss plus spread, fees and slippage. The session model motivates time-dependent baselines, not a universal rule that one hour is always liquid for every product.

2. **Execution impact + sizing under uncertain edge.** Combine [Almgren & Chriss, *Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions*](https://www.math.nyu.edu/~chriss/optliq_f.pdf) with [Kelly, *A New Interpretation of Information Rate*](https://doi.org/10.2307/2332762). Use the execution model to estimate the cost and impact of an order; use a *fractional* Kelly-style allocation only after estimating the strategy’s edge conservatively and accounting for uncertainty and correlation. For this retail account, the operational cap should still be the smaller of that allocation and a fixed account-level dollar-loss budget. Kelly is highly sensitive to input error; it should not turn a noisy microstructure score into leverage.

3. **Meta-labeling + out-of-sample evaluation + execution-aware entries.** Combine the primary strategy’s proposed trades with López de Prado’s [*Advances in Financial Machine Learning*](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Advances+in+Financial+Machine+Learning-p-9781119482086), especially the meta-labeling idea: let the original strategy nominate direction, and train a second-stage filter to estimate whether a trade is worth taking. Its label should incorporate realistic fill costs and adverse markout, not simply whether the next midprice tick went the intended way. Guard the evaluation against overfitting with Bailey & López de Prado’s [*The Deflated Sharpe Ratio*](https://doi.org/10.3905/jpm.2014.40.5.094). This directly tests the skipped-trade versus avoided-loss trade-off instead of assuming every filter helps.

4. **Live alpha-decay detection + execution breaker + independent kill switch.** Use a sequential change detector such as Adams & MacKay’s *Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection* on net returns, fill markouts and costs; if performance shifts, reduce the strategy’s risk or pause it. Pair that with a hard breaker on spread, stale quotes, missing data, repeated rejects or slippage beyond limits. A statistical alarm should not be the only control: a separate account-level loss/exposure limit should be able to stop every agent. This is a sensible risk architecture, but not a result established by the OFI studies.

5. **News/event windows + microstructure regime + stop design.** Pair a calendar/news event label with venue-specific spread, depth and volatility measurements; during a predeclared event window, either disallow entries or use more conservative slippage assumptions. Set stops from the strategy’s invalidation point, not from an OFI number, and size to the stop plus plausible gap/slippage cost. Evaluate whether waiting until spreads and depth normalize preserves enough edge; do not assume a fixed “wait X minutes” rule transfers across FX, energy, equities and crypto.

6. **LLM as an order-book analyst, not an execution authority.** The safest combination is an LLM that summarizes structured, timestamped book features or explains why a deterministic gate fired, paired with a deterministic risk engine that alone controls size and orders. Do not ask an LLM to infer executable liquidity from screenshots or to override spread, position, loss and stale-data limits. Recent work such as [*OpenPM: Auditable Point-in-Time Evaluation for LLM Portfolio-Management Agents*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.09988) and [*Execution Realism and Reproducibility in LLM-Based Trading Systems: A Systematic Scoping Review and Evidence Audit*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.08285) is relevant to evaluation discipline; it does not establish that an LLM reading a retail order book improves execution.

**My priority for your system:** build venue- and time-conditioned execution-cost estimates first; validate OFI only on feeds that actually represent the traded venue; then test an optional meta-label or regime gate in shadow mode, measuring both blocked winners and avoided costs. Keep position sizing, exposure aggregation and the kill switch deterministic and outside the strategy agents.

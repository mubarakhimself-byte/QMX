Abstract
In 1976, Lai constructed a nontrivial confidence sequence for the mean µ of a Gaussian
distribution with unknown variance σ2. Curiously, he employed both an improper (right
Haar) mixture over σ and an improper (flat) mixture over µ. Here, we elaborate carefully
on the details of his construction, which use generalized nonintegrable martingales and an
extended Ville’s inequality. While this does yield a sequential t-test, it does not yield an
“e-process” (due to the nonintegrability of his martingale). In this paper, we develop two
new e-processes and confidence sequences for the same setting: one is a test martingale in a
reduced filtration, while the other is an e-process in the canonical data filtration. These are
respectively obtained by swapping Lai’s flat mixture for a Gaussian mixture, and swapping
the right Haar mixture over σ with the maximum likelihood estimate under the null, as
done in universal inference. We also analyze the width of resulting confidence sequences,
which have a curious polynomial dependence on the error probability α that we prove
to be not only unavoidable, but (for universal inference) even better than the classical
fixed-sample t-test. Numerical experiments are provided along the way to compare and
contrast the various approaches, including some recent suboptimal ones.

1 Introduction
The classical location tests for Gaussian data fall into either of the two categories: the Z-test,
where the population variance is assumed to be known a priori , and a sample average is
rescaled by the sample deviation to create a standardized test statistic; or the t-test [Student,
1908], where the population variance is unknown, and a plug-in estimator of the variance is
used in lieu of the variance, accounting for the heavier (“regularly varying”) tail of the t-test
statistic than the Gaussian Z-test statistic.
This paper concerns sequential analogs of t-tests. Sequential Z-tests have been widely
established since Wald [1945]. One-sided or “power-one” variants have even been generalized
nonparametrically to subGaussian data [Robbins, 1970, Howard et al., 2021], and recently to
any data in the square-integrable class [Wang and Ramdas, 2023b].
Sequential extensions of the t-test were studied by Rushton [1950], Hoel [1954], Ghosh
[1960], Sacks [1965], who were interested in calculating the approximate thresholds to control
the type 1 error at a particular predefined stopping time. Inspired by the sequential Z-
test work of Robbins, Lai [1976] constructed a sequential t-test that controls a different
type of error that will be of particular interest to us: nonasymptotic, conservative (i.e. non-
approximate) time-uniform control of the type 1 error at all arbitrary stopping times, possibly
not specified in advance. We will define this formally later, but we mention at the outset that
the filtration will become an interesting matter as different sequential methods are defined on
different filtrations. Studying this phenomenon in the context of t-tests will be an important
part of this paper.
Lai and more recent authors [Gr¨ unwald et al., 2024, P´ erez-Ortiz et al., 2024] situate the
Gaussian t-test problem into the broader framework of group-invariant tests; indeed, the
t-test null is invariant under rescaling of observations by a constant.
The tests are mostly constructed using variants of the sequential likelihood ratio statistic
by Wald [1945], utilizing the fact that the likelihood ratios are nonnegative martingales (see
Section 2.6). Notably, P´ erez-Ortiz et al. [2024] shows the group-invariant extension of the
likelihood ratio statistic to be optimal (in the “e-power” sense to be defined in Section 2.3)
primarily in the point-versus-point ( µ/σ = θ0 versus µ/σ = θ1) setting. We shall closely
discuss their contributions later. Building upon these group invariance tools, Lindon et al.
[2022] construct a sequential F-test for the regression set-up, which coincides with one of the
t-tests that we shall present when no covariate exists. A slightly different approach is taken by
Jourdan et al. [2023] who study the same problem in the bandit context, sequentially bounding
the empirical mean and variance separately using existing Z-test-type bounds and obtaining
via a union bound a sequence of confidence “boxes” for mean and variance simultaneously.
We shall return to and compare our bounds with some of these results later.
We shall focus in this paper primarily on the construction of e-processes, which are se-
quences of test statistics whose realized values reflect the evidence against the null in favor of
the alternative. These e-processes can be thresholded and/or inverted to yield nonasymptotic,
conservative, “anytime-valid” hypothesis tests and confidence intervals (called “confidence se-
quences”), but we emphasize that they have clear interpretations without thresholding them
to yield tests. Indeed, these e-processes directly measure evidence against the null: values
below one are expected under the null, and large(r) values indicate (more) evidence against
the null.
Nonnegative supermartingales are the prototypical examples of e-processes [Ramdas et al.,
2022]. We emphasize that we are testing a composite null hypothesis, and it is not a priori
obvious how to construct such “simultaneous” or “composite” nonnegative supermartingales
or e-processes, or to compare various options. We shall formalize these concepts in greater
detail in Sections 2.2 and 2.3. The main contributions of this paper are listed as follows:
2
1. We apply the method of “Universal Inference”, a variant of the likelihood ratio test by
Wasserman et al. [2020], to construct a t-test e-process for the point null 1 µ = µ0, as
well as for the one-sided composite null µ ⩽ µ0, implying respectively a two-sided and
a one-sided confidence sequence for the mean µ, both of which are new.
2. We fill in the missing details of Lai’s [1976] sequential t-test, looking into the curious
question of why his approach yields a confidence sequence without an e-process, using
the theory of non-integrable nonnegative supermartingales [Wang and Ramdas, 2023a].
3. We introduce a simple variant of Lai [1976], deriving instead closed-form e-processes
for the null µ = µ0. The resulting confidence sequence enjoys improved tightness, and
unlike Lai’s original, does not require numerical inversion or thresholding.
4. We derive the information-theoretic lower bound on the width of confidence intervals,
and the upper bound on the growth of e-processes for the t-test, both of which are
attained, or almost attained, by our results (Contributions 1 and 3 in this list). We also
show that other conservative sequential t-tests available in the literature (Lai’s original
method, the plug-in method by Jourdan et al. [2023], the median method by Howard
and Ramdas [2022]), and very surprisingly, even the fixed-time classical t-test , fail to be
optimal in the rate with respect to the sample size n and/or the type 1 error level α.
5. We conduct a detailed comparison among tests we derive and in the literature in terms
of (a) which filtration they are safe for optional stopping in (the larger the better); (b)
what their e-powers are against a fixed alternative (the larger the better); and (c) for
their derived confidence sequences, the width or expected width, especially as a function
of sample size n and level α.

2.2 Sequential Statistics
The theory of sequential hypothesis testing is formulated as testing the null H0 : P ∈ P0
against the alternative H1 : P ∈ P1, where the statistician makes a decision (to reject, accept,
or sample further) each time a new data point Xn ∼ P is seen until rejection or acceptance.
In the formulation of Wald [1945, 1947], the decision scheme is required to control the type 1
error rate P (H0 is ever rejected) for P ∈ P0 within a prescribed level α, and the type 2 error
rate P (H0 is ever accepted) for P ∈ P1 within β. A slightly different formulation, proposed
by Darling and Robbins [1968], only requires the type 1 error rate to be controlled within
α, but the decision schemes under this constraint are often proved to have “power one”, i.e.,
P (H0 is ever rejected) = 1 for P ∈ P1. We shall follow the latter formulation. We derive
sequential tests of type 1 error rate strictly bounded by α (“level-α sequential tests”) and
prove their power guarantees. These power guarantees are stated in terms of “e-power”, to
be formally introduced in Section 2.4, which is a finer-grained quantification of power than
the rejection rate P (H0 is ever rejected) under P ∈ P1.
The classical duality between tests and interval estimates also manifests in the sequential
setting. Let P be a family of distributions and let θ : P →R be the parameter of interest.
A (1 − α)-confidence sequence (CS) [Darling and Robbins, 1967] over P for θ is a sequence
of confidence intervals {CIn}n⩾1 such that, for any P ∈ P, P (∀n ⩾ 1, θ(P ) ∈ CIn) ⩾ 1 − α;
or equivalently, P (θ(P ) ∈ CIτ ) ⩾ 1 − α for any stopping time τ (see Lemma 3 of Howard
et al. [2021]). If the null P0 ⊆ Pis defined as a preimage θ−1(θ0), a sequential test can be
constructed via rejection H0 whenever θ0 /∈ CIn, and vice versa. In this paper, the asymptotic
tightness of a confidence sequence is measured in terms of (1) itsgrowth rate, the rate at which
its width increases when n and the sample are fixed while α → 0 (or while α ↓ α⋆(n) if α
is not allowed to be below α⋆(n)); and (2) its shrinkage rate , the rate at which its width
decreases when α is fixed while n → ∞). For example, it is known that the minimax rates
of growth

2.3 Test Processes and Their Maximal Inequalities
There has been a recent revival of the idea, originally due to Robbins [1970], of constructing
sequential tests and confidence sequences via nonnegative supermartingales (NSMs) and more
generally, e-processes [Howard et al., 2021, Ramdas et al., 2022]. Let us define them below.
Consider a stochastic process {Mn}n⩾0 where each Mn is defined as a [0 , ∞]-valued func-
tion of X1, . . . , Xn. We say it is
• a nonnegative supermartingale (NSM) for P on a filtration {Gn}n⩾0 if E P (M0) < ∞,
Mn is Gn-measurable and E P (Mn+1|Gn) ⩽ Mn for all n ⩾ 0;
• a nonnegative martingale (NM) for P if, further, the equality EP (Mn+1|Gn) = Mn holds.
4
Recently, Wang and Ramdas [2023a] defined a wider class of processes by dropping the
integrability assumption EP (M0) < ∞, as the conditional expectation E P (Mn+1|Gn) can still
be well-defined as long as Mn+1 is nonnegative. We say the process {Mn} is
• an extended nonnegative supermartingale (ENSM) for P on {Gn} if Mn is Gn-measurable
and EP (Mn+1|Gn) ⩽ Mn are satisfied, irrespective of the finiteness of E P (M0).
We say the process {Mn} is NSM (or NM, ENSM) for P if it is an NSM (or NM, ENSM) for
all P ∈ P. When an NSM (or NM) for P has an initial expected value E P (M0) = 1 for all
P ∈ P, we call it a test supermartingale (or test martingale) for P. We call a nonnegative
random variable M = M (X1, . . . , Xn) with sup P ∈P EP (M ) ⩽ 1 an e-value for P. Further,
we say the process {Mn} is
• an e-process for P on {Gn} if for any {Gn}-adapted stopping time τ , Mτ is an e-value
for P; i.e., sup P ∈P EP (Mτ ) ⩽ 1.
Note that the filtration {Gn} is usually the canonical one {Fn}, but as we shall see later, other
non-trivial choices will lead to interesting results. NMs and NSMs for P are clearly e-processes
for P, on the same filtration. More generally, the following equivalent characterization of e-
processes is due to Ramdas et al. [2020, Lemma 6]:
• A nonnegative process {Mn} adapted to {Gn} is an e-process for P on {Gn} if and only
if for each P ∈ P, there is a test supermartingale {N Pn } for P on {Gn} that upper
bounds {Mn}: P (Mn ⩽ N Pn ) = 1.
A parametrized family of NSMs or ENSMs can lead to a new NSM or ENSM by mixture.

2.4 e-Power of Test Processes
Desirably, a powerful test process shrinks or stays small under the null P0, and grows under
the alternative P1 so rejection can happen. To measure the power of a test process under a
fixed alternative distribution Q ∈ P1, we define the e-power of test processes as follows [Vovk
and Wang, 2024].
Definition 2.3 (e-power). For a test process {Mn}, we define its e-power as the Q-almost
sure limit lim infn→∞ log Mnn . For a single e-value M = M (X1, . . . , Xn), we define its e-power
as EQ(log M ).
The motivation, we note, is that it quantifies the rate at which the process grows expo-
nentially under the alternative, i.e., how fast evidence accumulates. The value of an e-process
is often intuitively understood as one’s total capital when betting against a casino pricing
according to the null (hence the term “game-theoretic statistics”, see e.g. Shafer and Vovk
[2005]). The idea of quantifying and optimizing the growth of a capital process by its ex-
pected log-value can be traced back to Kelly [1956], Breiman [1961], among other classical
references in the literature. Breiman [1961], for example, justifies this log-optimality criterion
by noting that “under reasonable betting systems Mn increases exponentially and maximizing
EQ(log Mn) maximizes the rate of growth.”
More concretely, many e-processes in the literature take a multiplicative form Mn =Qn
i=1 M (Xi) where M (Xi)’s are i.i.d. e-values. In this case, the e-power of the entire process
{Mn} and that of the individual e-values M (Xi) coincide due to the strong law of large
numbers, and the growth is characterized asymptotically as Mn = exp(nEQ(log M1) +oQ(n)).
Alternatively, one can also informally say that the e-power E Q(log M1) is roughly inversely
proportional to the expectation of the rejection time τ = min{n : Mn ⩾ 1/α} as
log(1/α) ≈ EQ(log Mτ ) = EQ(log M1) · EQ(τ ), (4)
where the equality is due to Wald’s identity (see e.g. Roters [1994, Theorem 2.1]). More
formally, lower bounds are known for the expected rejection time of simple testing problems,
attained exactly by the inverse of e-power of the likelihood ratio martingale, a topic we shall
return to in Section 2.6.
2.5 Sequential t-Test and t-Confidence Sequences
Different objectives are pursued for the sequential Gaussian t-test problem. Early authors
have studied the problem of testing the null Nµ=0 (or more generally Nµ/σ=θ0) against the
alternative Nµ/σ=θ1 [Rushton, 1950, Ghosh, 1960, Sacks, 1965], and its scale-invariant na-
ture translates into the group-invariant setting as the problem of testing orbital nulls and
alternatives [P´ erez-Ortiz et al., 2024].
6
In this paper, we shall roughly follow the same routine as Lai [1976] and aim at power
against a broader range of alternatives, chiefly focusing instead on the construction of (1)
test processes for the null Nµ=0 (and study their behavior under both null and non-null
underlying distributions); (2) Confidence sequences for the population mean µ over the set of
all Gaussian distributions N , which we shall often shorthand as “t-confidence sequences”, or
“t-CSs” for brevity. One may note that (1) is sufficient to produce tests for arbitrary point
location null Nµ=µ0 by shifting, and consequently (2) by Lemmas 2.1 and 2.2 and inversion.
Occasionally we are interested in test processes for the one-sided null Nµ⩽0 as these give rise
to one-sided tests and CSs. In fact, we shall later construct test processes for Nµ/σ=θ0 which
is the strongest statistical quantity in the setting, as it leads to both CSs and tests for the
mean µ and the normalized mean µ/σ.

4.2 Scale Invariant Filtration
We say that a functionf : Rn → R is scale invariant if it is measurable and for anyx1, . . . , xn ∈
R and λ >0, f (x1, . . . , xn) = f (λx1, . . . , λxn). Let us define the following sub-filtration of
the canonical filtration {Fn}.
Definition 4.2 (Scale invariant filtration). For n ⩾ 1, let
F ∗n = σ(f (X1, . . . , Xn) : f is scale invariant). (24)
Then, the filtration {F ∗n}n⩾1 is called the scale invariant filtration of data X1, X2, . . ..
Definition 4.2 states that F ∗n is the coarsest σ-algebra to which all scale invariant functions
of X1, . . . , Xn are measurable. For example, recall that we denote by Tn−1 the t-statistic of
the data √n − 1 Sn−nµ0√nVn−S2n , which is F ∗n-measurable when µ0 = 0 and is a quantity frequently
used to construct scale-invariant statistics later. To see that {F ∗n} is indeed a filtration, let
f be any scale invariant function Rn → R, and define g : Rn+1 → R as g(x1, . . . , xn, xn+1) =
f (x1, . . . , xn) which is also scale invariant. So f (X1, . . . , Xn) = g(X1, . . . , Xn, Xn+1) is F ∗n+1-
measurable. The arbitrariness of f implies that F ∗n ⊆ F∗n+1.
The filtration {F ∗n} contains all the information up to time n about the relative sizes of the
observations. For example, {max{X1, . . . , X4} ⩾ 2X3} ∈ F4 while {max{X1, . . . , X4} ⩾ 2} /∈
F4. The reader may recall the exchangeable sub-σ-algebra in the theory of exchangeability and
backwards martingales such as in Klenke [2013, Chapter 12] for an analogy. The reader might
also find the following analogy helpful in understanding the intuition: Imagine in prehistoric
times when units of length were yet to be invented, our ancestors collected a sequence of
leaves and fathomed their sizes. Due to the lack of units however, no absolute real-valued
sizes (in inches, say) but only ratios of sizes relative to each other (or to the first leaf) were
recorded. Actually, when X1 is non-zero, F ∗n has the following clean expression.

4.6 Remarks
4.6.1 Remark I: Symmetry and e-Hacking
An ideal property that the test processes and confidence sequences derived in this section via
scale invariant reduction enjoy, but those via universal inference do not, is that they are all
symmetric with respect to the observations X1, . . . , Xn. This is because they depend on the
sample only through the symmetric statistics Sn and Vn.
This makes the methods robust against the following way to “hack” the e-values or the
confidence sequences: a practitioner may permute a batch of data, compute the e-processes or
confidence sequences repeatedly via multiple orders of observation, and announce the maximal
e-process or minimal width confidence sequence. While e-processes allow optional stopping
and continuation, they do not allow “optional permutation” like this. However symmetric
methods, since their final results are path-independent, are robust against this type of hacking.
4.6.2 Remark II: Asymptotic Coverage for Non-Gaussian Data
Classical tests derived under Gaussian assumption (e.g. the classical t-, F-, and χ2-tests)
are frequently applied to data that are not known to be Gaussian on grounds of the central
limit theorem, leading to type 1 error or interval coverage guanratees that are of asymptotic
nature. In this subsection, let us substantiate the similar claim that our confidence sequences
derived in this section are all asymptotic confidence sequences under any i.i.d. data with finite
variance. Let us use L2 to denote the set of all these distributions on R.
The concept of asymptotic confidence sequences is proposed recently by Waudby-Smith
et al. [2024, Section 2.1]. To wit, let P be a family of distributions and θ : P →R be the
parameter of interest. A (1 − α)-asymptotic confidence sequence over P for θ is a sequence of
intervals {[ˆθn ± rn]}n⩾1 such that, the convergence lim n→∞ rn/r∗n = 1 holds P -almost surely
for all P ∈ P, where {[ˆθn ± r∗n]}n⩾1 form a (1 − α)-CS over P for θ (non-asymptotically,
in the sense that is defined in Section 2.2). Appealing to a similar technique, we prove in
Appendix C.3 that, for example,
Theorem 4.12. The intervals (37) form a (1 − α)-asymptotic confidence sequence for µ over
L2.
On the other hand, we note that it is impossible to construct a non-degenerate universal
non-asymptotic confidence interval even for the set of allbounded distributions (with unknown
bounds). Thus it also excludes the existence of CIs or CSs for larger classes, e.g. the set of
all subGaussian distributions, or L2. This result can be traced back to Bahadur and Savage
[1956, Theorem 2]. The crux of the matter, we remark, lies in the convexity of these large
nonparametric sets of distributions.

5.2 Simulations on e-Processes
We empirically demonstrate the pros and cons of the two major two-sided e-processes for
Nµ=0 we present in this paper, Theorem 3.2 and 4.9, under various underlying distributions
and choices of hyperparameters that reflect prior knowledge. Our simulations contain two null
data-generating distributions N0,1 and N0,4, as well as two alternativeN0.1,1 and N1,4. For the
method of Gaussian mixture of scale invariant likelihood ratios (Theorem 4.9), the mixture
precision parameters range from c2 ∈ {1, 0.1, 50}, corresponding to the prior distributions
µ/σ ∼ N0,1, N0,10, and N0,0.02. For universal inference method (Theorem 3.2), the plug-in
estimators for µ and σ2 are chosen as the maximum a posterior (MAP) estimators via using
a normal-inverse-gamma prior, the conjugate prior for Gaussian models with unknown µ and
σ2. The prior
(µ, σ2) ∼ N Γ−1(µ0, ν0, α0, β0), (51)
corresponds to estimating the mean through additional ν0 prior observations with mean µ0,
and variance through additional 2 α0 observations with mean µ0 and sum of squared devia-
tions 2β0. We choose N Γ−1(0, 20, 10, 10) and N Γ−1(0, 20, 10, 40) in our simulations, and also
employ the idea of using the first 40 observations to fit the prior (which we call “burning
in”) and start the e-process afterward. The computed log-e-processes are plotted in Figure 1,
averaged over 100 independent repeats. While none exhibits any growth under nulls, they
differ qualitatively under alternatives.
Under the N0.1,1 alternative, the “confident” Gaussian prior N0,0.02 leads in power when
the true effect size µ/σ = 0 .1 is indeed small and falls around the unit standard deviation
range of the prior, beating the “underconfident” priorsN0,1 and N0,10, and universal inference.
Among universal inference instances, the one with a N Γ−1(0, 20, 10, 10) prior which correctly
guesses the true parameters, and the one that burns in to learn them are much more powerful
than the other one with a “wrong” prior.
Under the N1,4 alternative, the “modest” Gaussian prior N0,1 is advantageous, and the
“overconfident” N0,0.02 is penalized for not adequately capturing the true effect sizeµ/σ = 0.5.
Among universal inference, this time the N Γ−1(0, 20, 10, 40) prior is closer to the underlying
true distribution so it leads in power, followed by the burned-in one, and followed in turn by
the one with the “wrong” prior.
We thus conclude that in general universal inference is more sensitive to the quality of
prior information, and a weak or biased prior likely degrades the power of the test. The
technique of burning-in, as we show, partially helps the method obtain all-case adaptivity
to any underlying distribution via the construction of a data-dependent prior, paying the
price that the evidence does not accumulate during the burn-in phase. This is a wisdom that
resonates with the “split-sample” idea of the fixed-time universal inference [Wasserman et al.,
2020].

6.1 Information-Theoretic Lower Bound of t-Confidence Intervals
Recall from Table 1 that our best CSs have width scaling with α like the nonstandard rate
α−1/n, which is very different from the rate ofplog(1/α) that one typically sees for confidence
intervals and sequences when the variance is known. Indeed, we will see now that for t-CIs
(and hence t-CSs), this α−1/n rate is actually minimax optimal.

for all P ∈ N, P (µ(P ) ⩾ L(X1, . . . , Xn)) ⩾ 1 − α. (53)

quantile of the random variable µ(P ) − L(X1, . . . , Xn) under P , i.e.,
P (µ(P ) − L(X1, . . . , Xn) ⩾ Wε(L, P)) = ε. (54)

lower bound immediately implies a lower bound for two-sided CIs. The two layers of 2 α-low
probability and (1 − α)-high probability are introduced to include confidence intervals with
data-dependent radii, which, though uncommon in Z-tests, recur in t-tests throughout this
paper.

and N ⊗n0,µ2+σ2 is at most 1 − 3α. The details are in Appendix C.4.

lower bound. Let Iα,n be the set of all two-sided (1 − α)-confidence intervals for µ over N

halves the expected width without changing its validity.
6.3 Log-Optimality by Reverse Information Projection
We now show that the recurring e-power 12 log(1 + µ2/σ2) in various test processes presented
thus far is not a coincidence, as it is optimal in the problem of t-test. We study the e-power
upper bound by focusing, again, on the case with a single observation X1, via the expected
log-value under a fixed alternative of an e-value (called “e-power” by Vovk and Wang [2024],

satisfies the e-power upper bound E Q(log M ) ⩽ δ(Q) for Q /∈ P, then the e-power of any
supermartingale for P can at most be δ(Q) under Q as well.

7 Concluding Remarks
In this paper, we derive several sequential t-tests via universal inference and scale invariant
likelihood ratio mixtures. In particular, we focus on the construction of test processes (non-
negative supermartingales, e-processes, and extended nonnegative supermartingales), and the
confidence sequences they imply.
The two methods we explore yield similar results in numerous regards. They asymptot-
ically share the same optimalities in terms of test processes and confidence sequences alike;
they both extend to the one-sided test; and the computation of the test processes only requires
linear time in both cases (unlike the quadratic cost that universal inference incurs worst-case,
as mentioned by Wasserman et al. [2020, Section 7]).
One of the key differences between the two methods is the filtrations to which the processes
are adapted, as the test processes scale invariant likelihood ratio mixtures produce are only
adapted to a reduced filtration, allowing for a smaller set of stopping times that optionally
stop the experiment. On the other hand, the universal inference test processes, while robust
under a larger set of stopping times, sometimes suffer from lower stability depending on the
quality of the estimators plugged in. We remark that the issue of filtration disappears once
we convert tests into confidence sequences.
While we only focus on the class of one-dimensional normal distributions N in this paper,
we expect similar techniques may lead to sequential Hotelling’s T-tests (with test processes
and confidence sphere sequences [Chugg et al., 2023]) for the class of multivariate normal
distributions. A slightly different way to generalize in dimensionality is the sequential F-tests
for linear models, on which the most recent and comprehensive work by Lindon et al. [2022]
only covers one of the numerous methods in our paper (namely, Gaussian mixture of scale
invariant likelihood ratios). We leave these topics for future authors to study in depth.

Discussion on Proposition 6.5. Before we present and prove an extended version of
Proposition 6.5, let us briefly review some key definitions and results of Larsson et al. [2024].
Let P and Q be mutually equivalent probability measures. Let P ◦◦ be the “bipolar” of the
measure set P, which contains all sub-probability measures for which any e-value for P is an
e-value as well. P ◦◦ is thus a superset of P, understood as the “effective null”. If the infimum
inf P ∈P ◦◦ DKL(Q∥P ) is attained as minimum by some P ∗ ∈ P◦◦, we say that P ∗ is the reverse
information projection of Q onto the set P. Proposition 2.4, Theorem 3.4, and Theorem 4.1
of Larsson et al. [2024] jointly state the following.
Lemma C.2. Let P ∗ be a sub-probability measure such that M ∗ = dQdP ∗ (X1) is an e-value for
P. Then, the following are equivalent:

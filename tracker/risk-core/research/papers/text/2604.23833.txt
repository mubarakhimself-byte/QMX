Abstract
Hierarchical Risk Parity [López de Prado, 2016] and the Schur-complement generalisation of
Cotton [2024] are among the most widely adopted regularised portfolio construction methods, yet
both are signal-blind: they solve only the minimum-variance problem and cannot accommodate
an arbitrary expected-return forecast. This paper introduces three methods that incorporate
alpha signals into hierarchical and regularised portfolio construction.
HRP-µ is a hierarchical allocator that accepts an arbitrary signalµ and nests standard HRP
whenγ = 0and µ =1. It preserves the tree-based structure of HRP while extending it beyond the
minimum-variance setting.HRP-Σµ strengthens this construction by replacing inverse-variance
representatives with recursive local mean–variance optima, thereby using richer within-cluster
covariance information at the sameO(N2)asymptotic cost.
CRISP(Correlation-Regularised Iterative Shrinkage Portfolios) is an iterative solver forPγw =µ
with Pγ = (1−γ ) diag(Σ) +γΣ, so thatγ interpolates between a diagonal portfolio rule and
full Markowitz. At convergence, CRISP is Markowitz applied to a variance-preserving shrunk
covariance—diagonal variances unchanged, off-diagonal correlations shrunk—withγ tuned for
out-of-sample Sharpe rather than covariance-estimation loss.
In Monte Carlo experiments across multiple covariance regimes and estimation ratios, HRP-µ
and HRP-Σµ both outperform plain HRP—not only on general expected-return forecasts, where
HRP is signal-blind, but also on the minimum-variance problemµ =1that is HRP’s home
territory—with HRP-Σµ consistently improving on HRP-µ. CRISP at intermediateγ is the
dominant method in both regimes, outperforming HRP, Cotton, Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage, direct
Markowitz, and the signal-aware hierarchical methods at every sample size tested.

allocation, covariance shrinkage, out-of-sample evaluation

1 Introduction
1.1 Motivation
Hierarchical Risk Parity [López de Prado, 2016] and the Schur-complement allocator of Cotton
[2024] have become standard regularised portfolio constructions. Both aresignal-blind. They solve
only the minimum-variance problemµ =1and have no mechanism for an expected-return forecast.
Cotton’s allocator connects HRP to exact minimum variance through a single continuous parameter
γ∈ [0, 1]and is in that sense a strict generalisation of López de Prado, but it inherits theµ =1
restriction and costsO(N 3/6)because a sibling covariance block must be inverted at every internal
tree node.
This restriction collides with three features of real portfolio construction. The first issignal
integration. Quantitative equity, factor investing, tactical asset allocation, and Black–Litterman
view blending [Black and Litterman, 1992; Grinold and Kahn, 1999; Barroso and Santa-Clara, 2015]
all deliver a heterogeneousµ, and that signal is the object that distinguishes an active portfolio
from a risk-only allocation. Settingµ =1is active discarding, not a neutral default. The second is
computational tractability. Universes withN between10 3 and10 4, re-optimised on rolling windows,
makeO(N 3)operations costly; O(N 2)is the budget a realistic backtest can absorb. The third is
estimation error. Direct Markowitz inversion [Markowitz, 1952] amplifies noise on low-variance
eigenvectors and produces the extreme weights documented by Michaud [1989], and the standard
remedy is structural shrinkage of the covariance estimator [Stein, 1956; Ledoit and Wolf, 2003, 2004,
2017, 2020; Chen et al., 2010].
This paper asks a single question. Can the hierarchical-and-shrinkage structure that makes HRP
and the Cotton allocator useful be extended to the general mean–variance problem with arbitrary
µ without giving up its regularisation benefits, and what is the natural iterative counterpart? The
answer is three methods, each controlled by a shrinkage parameterγ∈ [0, 1]that plays the same role
Cotton [2024] introduced—governing how much cross-asset covariance enters the allocation—and
each accepting an arbitrary signal.
1.2 Three methodological contributions
We present the three methods in order of empirical strength—weakest to strongest—and use the
display names HRP-µ, HRP-Σµ, and CRISP throughout.
Contribution 1: HRP-µ, a transparent signal-aware hierarchical allocator.HRP- µ
walks the same correlation dendrogram as classical HRP. At each internal node its representative
portfolio is thesigned inverse-variance portfolioˆwsignedL,i ∝sign (µi)/σ2ii, and between-child budgets
are set by the same2× 2mean–variance system that underlies Cotton’s allocator. Because the
representative depends only on the diagonal variances{σ2ii} and the signal signs{sign(µi)} inside a
block—never on within-cluster correlations—every leaf weight factors as a sign times a root-to-leaf
product of non-negative budgets, so the hierarchical audit trail that practitioners prize in classical
HRP is preserved. Proposition 4.2 shows that HRP-µ nests De Prado’s HRP exactly atγ = 0,
µ =1, and the cost is O(N 2), a factorN cheaper than Cotton. Even on Cotton’s ownµ =1
problem, HRP-µ atγ >0improves on classical HRP out of sample and matches Cotton’s Sharpe at
roughly half Cotton’sγ, while avoiding the Schur-complement instability Cotton exhibits at small
T(Proposition 3.2).
5
Contribution 2: HRP-Σµ, the strongest tree-based method.HRP-Σ µ keeps the HRP
tree and the2× 2between-child split but replaces HRP-µ’s signed inverse-variance representative
with thelocal mean–variance optimumcomputed recursively on the sub-tree, using the full within-
cluster covariance ΣLL at every node. The representative is richer than HRP-µ’s—it captures
within-cluster hedging that the signed IVP cannot see—at the cost of the closed-form auditability.
The load-bearing algorithmic detail is the between-child normalisation: sum-to-one normalisation
of the same2× 2system produces a sign-flip pathology (documented as a cautionary negative
result in Appendix C), whereas theL1 normalisation αk←α rawk /(|αrawL | +|αrawR |)is ray-invariant
and sign-preserving (Lemmas 4.5 and 4.6). The cost isO(N 2), matching HRP-µ; classical HRP
is recovered to cosine≈ 0.992at γ = 0, µ =1, and exactly at tree depth≤ 2(Proposition 4.8).
In the synthetic Monte Carlo panels of Section 10 HRP-Σµ delivers20–35%higher out-of-sample
Sharpe than HRP-µ on random signals and up to180%higher on structural signals with sample
estimation, reaching roughly90%of CRISP’s performance while staying strictlyO(N2).
Contribution 3: CRISP, an iterative shrinkage solver (headline).CRISP runs scalar
Gauss–Seidel on the shrunk mean–variance systemPγw = µ, where Pγ = (1−γ )D +γΣ and
D = diag(Σ). The shrinkage parameter interpolates between a pure diagonal solve atγ = 0
(signed inverse-variance weighting) and exact Markowitz atγ = 1. Gauss–Seidel onPγ converges
unconditionally for every symmetric positive definiteΣ and everyγ∈ [0, 1](Theorem 5.2). The
convergence-rate result is new: atγ = 1the sweep count required to reach relative direction error
ε is p = O(κ(C) log(1/ε)), controlled by the condition number of thecorrelationmatrix rather
than of the covariance matrix itself, and in generalp =O(κ(D−1Pγ) log(1/ε))with κ(D−1Pγ) =
[(1−γ ) +γλ1(C)]/[(1−γ ) +γλN(C)]interpolating monotonically from1at γ = 0to κ(C)at γ = 1;
volatility dispersion doesnotenter the rate (Theorem 5.3). Correlation conditioning is bounded in
realistic equity universes, so this is what makes CRISP tractable at scale.
At convergence CRISP producesP−1γ µ: Markowitz on the shrunk covariancePγ. This places
CRISP in the linear-shrinkage family of Ledoit and Wolf [2003, 2004, 2017], with two structural
differences. First, its target is the diagonalD, soPγ reproduces the variances ofΣ exactly for
everyγ and only correlations are shrunk—the reliable part of the covariance (per-asset variances,
each estimated from a single time series) is left alone and the unreliable part (pairwise correlations)
is regularised. Classical Ledoit–Wolf targets (scaled identity, single factor, constant correlation)
perturb the diagonal. Second, the shrinkage intensityγ is chosen to maximise out-of-sample portfolio
Sharpe rather than to minimise Frobenius loss on the estimator; Section 6 shows these are different
problems with different optima. In the synthetic Monte Carlo panels of Section 10 CRISP at
γ∈ [0.3, 0.7]with100sweeps delivers80–94%of oracle Sharpe uniformly across four covariance
regimes andT/N∈[0.6,5], and dominates every benchmark on every panel.
1.3 Three supporting contributions
Adaptiveγ⋆ rule.A bias–variance decomposition of expected OOS Sharpe yields the closed-form
rule
γ⋆ ≈ 11 +cκ(C) 2N/(TIC 2),
whose comparative statics (Proposition 7.1) are qualitatively correct:γ⋆ rises with the sample
length T and the information coefficientIC, and falls with conditioning difficultyκ(C)and universe
size N. Calibration on a48-cell synthetic cube shows that the OOS Sharpe surface is nearly flat in
γ: the plateau of near-optimal values has median width0.38on[0 , 1]. The formula’s practical value
6
is therefore explanatory—it rationalises why a fixed defaultγ≈ 0.5is robust and why a single knob
suffices—rather than prescriptive.
Three regularisation channels.CRISP regularises through three distinct channels (Remark 7.2).
Channel 1 is operator shrinkage viaγ: the statistical channel that does most of the work. Channel 2
is convergence slack from a finite sweep count, which is a computational artefact rather than a
design choice. Channel 3 is implicit spectral truncation through early stopping, which is statistically
beneficial near γ = 1because the low-eigenvalue directions of D−1Pγ converge last and carry
the most estimation noise; in that regime CRISP is an iterative cousin of ridge regression on the
Markowitz system. At the recommended operating point(γ≈ 0.5,p = 100)only Channel 1 is
materially active.
Out-of-sample evaluation.Every method comparison is out-of-sample. We run a synthetic
Monte Carlo study across four covariance regimes (factor, block, spiked, equicorrelation) and
T/N∈ [0.6, 5], with both oracle and sample-mean signal treatments, against1/N [DeMiguel,
Garlappi and Uppal, 2009], HRP, Cotton at severalγ, direct Markowitz, and linear and non-linear
Ledoit–Wolf [Ledoit and Wolf, 2004, 2020], in addition to our three methods. Direction error appears
only as an in-sample tracking diagnostic; OOS Sharpe is the primary metric.
1.4 Memory, not speed
It is worth stating early what CRISP does and does not offer at the machine level. On tuned
BLAS—Apple M4 with Accelerate and a Numba-JIT CRISP—direct Cholesky is48× faster than
CRISP atN = 500and remains between9× and48× faster forN∈{ 500,..., 5,000} (Remark 5.7).
Extrapolating the trend on this hardware places the single-solve wall-clock crossover nearN≈ 45,000,
well outside realistic portfolio sizes. We do not claim a wall-clock advantage for CRISP.
What CRISP offers structurally is memory. A factor-streaming implementation (Algorithm 3)
that never materialisesΣ reduces working memory fromO(N 2)to O(NK ), whereK is the number
of factors. At N = 30,000and K = 20the dense Σ occupies7 .2GB; the factor stream uses
4.8MB. Cholesky cannot exploit this reduction because its factorisation needs random access to the
full matrix. The honest case for CRISP is therefore stronger OOS Sharpe together with memory
scalability, not speed.
1.5 Road map
Section 2 fixes notation, sets up the ray view of mean–variance, and introduces the direction-error
diagnostic together with its sign-invariance caveat. Section 3 reviews HRP and the Cotton Schur
allocator and places both in the method landscape, including Cotton’s intermediate-γ instability
under estimation noise. Section 4 develops the two tree-based methods in a single frame—one tree,
two representatives, two methods: HRP-µ with the signed inverse-variance representative, HRP-Σµ
with the recursive local MVO. Section 5 is the headline theoretical section: it presents CRISP, proves
unconditional Gauss–Seidel convergence and the correlation-conditioning rate theorem, records the
early-stopping-as-spectral-truncation remark, describes the factor-streaming variant, and locates
CRISP within the Ledoit–Wolf family. Section 6 develops the perturbation theory of the shrinkage
trajectory, including a non-monotone-trajectory counter-example and an interior-γ⋆ proposition
under explicit hypotheses. Section 7 derives the adaptiveγ⋆ rule and consolidates the three-channel
taxonomy. Section 8 presents the five-method comparative landscape and a practitioner’s guide.
Section 9 treats linear constraints through a projected CRISP. Section 10 reports the synthetic

1.6 Relation to the literature
The paper connects six strands of research.
Hierarchical portfolio methods.López de Prado [2016] introduces HRP, and López de Prado
and Lewis [2019] develops companion tools for false-strategy detection. Raffinot [2018] studies
broader hierarchical-clustering allocation. Cotton [2024] provides the Schur-complement unification
that connects HRP to exact minimum variance through a single continuous parameter. Our HRP-µ
and HRP-Σµ extend this hierarchical framework to arbitraryµ at the sameO(N 2)cost, and strictly
dominate HRP on its ownµ=1problem whenever estimation error is present.
Covariance shrinkage.The Stein estimator [Stein, 1956] and its portfolio-theoretic descendants
[Ledoit and Wolf, 2003, 2004, 2017, 2020; Chen et al., 2010] shrink the sample covariance toward
a structured target to control low-eigenvalue estimation error. CRISP is a member of this family
with targetD = diag(Σ), a choice that preserves variances identically and shrinks only correlations,
and with shrinkage intensity tuned for portfolio Sharpe rather than Frobenius loss on the estimator.
Both differences are structural; we develop them in Section 5.
Iterative and parametric portfolio methods.Brandt, Santa-Clara and Valkanov [2009]
parameterise the policy directly as a linear function of asset characteristics. Kozak, Nagel and
Santosh [2020] shrink the cross-section of factor returns through ridge and Bayesian regularisation.
DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal [2009] establish the1/N benchmark that any proposed method must
outperform out of sample. CRISP differs from these methods by solving a shrunk Markowitz system
whose variances are preserved identically at everyγ, so no asset is implicitly up- or down-volatilised
by the regulariser.
Signal integration and Bayesian portfolios.Black and Litterman [1992] combine an equilib-
rium prior with investor views through a Bayesian posterior; Grinold and Kahn [1999] formalise the
information-ratio decomposition of active management; and Barroso and Santa-Clara [2015] study
signal-conditioned portfolios in currency markets. We compare CRISP with Black–Litterman as
a candidate Bayesian reading in Section 5: both produce shrunk posterior weights, but CRISP is
driven by the single explicitγknob rather than by a specified view covariance.
Numerical analysis of iterative methods.The classical iterative-solver literature [Hackbusch,
2016; Saad, 2003; Varga, 2000; Young, 1971; Ostrowski, 1954] supplies the unconditional-convergence
and rate results for symmetric positive definite Gauss–Seidel on which Theorems 5.2 and 5.3 rest.
The contribution of Theorem 5.3 is to extract the correlation-conditioning constant specific to the
portfolio setting and to translate the resulting rate into a concrete sweep count that is independent
of the universe size.
8
Factor models for covariance.Fama and French [1993] establish the pricing-factor representa-
tion. Fan, Liao and Mincheva [2013] develop POET for structured covariance estimation, Marchenko
and Pastur [1967] provide the spectral baseline for sample covariances, and Ledoit and Wolf [2020]
give the analytical non-linear-shrinkage counterpart. Our factor-streaming variant of CRISP exploits
a factor structureΣ =BΛB⊤ +Didio to hold working memory atO(NK )without ever forming Σ,
which is what makes the method usable in universes where Cholesky’s random-access requirement is
the binding constraint.
We do not replace any of these literatures. We supply anO(κ(C)N 2 logε−1)iterative solver
that inherits HRP’s regularisation philosophy through itsγ parameter, two signal-aware hierarchical
alternatives for tree-interpretable use cases, and a synthetic out-of-sample evaluation against the
standard benchmarks.
2 Notation and the direction view
This section does four things. It fixes the symbols that appear in the rest of the paper; it replaces
the usual relative-error metric with a scale-invariantdirectionerror and flags the one way in which
that replacement is dangerous; it isolates the correlation conditioningκ(C)as the spectral invariant
that will control both the in-sample direction gap of the cheapest baseline and the sweep count of
CRISP; and it records a parallel-direction identity that exposes a family of signal vectors on which
every method in the paper returns the same ray. The last point is a pruning lemma: it cuts off an
entire class of otherwise plausible worst-case constructions.

guarantee it is not. The canonical example is the sum-normalised recursive MVO (the Method A1
of Appendix C): on the noiseless recovery problem it appears, ondir, to be trackingw⋆ well, while
in fact half the time it is delivering systematically negative out-of-sample Sharpe.
For this reason we treatdir as atracking diagnostic, not a quality metric. It is the right tool for
asking “does my method stay near the Markowitz ray asγ moves or asT grows?” It is the wrong
tool for ranking methods. The primary quality metric for ranking is out-of-sample Sharpe, and
Section 10 is organised around it for this reason.

3.4 Why extend to generalµ?
HRP and Cotton together give a clean story forµ =1. But that signal is, in applied portfolio
construction, the degenerate corner of the mean–variance problem. Four representative settings
make the point.
Quant equity and factor investing.Cross-sectional factor signals—momentum, value, quality,
earnings revisions, low-volatility—produce heterogeneous expected returns across names, and the
entire point of a factor model is thatµ̸ =1. An allocator that ignoresµ throws away the primary
input before the optimisation begins.
Multi-signal integration.Modern quant pipelines aggregate many orthogonalised signals into a
composite alpha [Grinold and Kahn, 1999]. The composite is a richµ; the portfolio stage is asked
to weigh it against covariance risk. A signal-blind allocator is simply inadmissible in this context.
Tactical asset allocation.Multi-asset allocators take views on expected returns at the asset-class
level—equities versus bonds versus commodities versus alternatives—and expect the optimiser to
translate those views into positions.µ is low-dimensional here but highly informative; setting it to
1is not a simplification, it is a replacement of the problem.
Black–Litterman view integration.The Black–Litterman framework [Black and Litterman,
1992] returns a posteriorµ that encodes a prior together with investor views. The value of the
framework is entirely in that posterior; an allocator that discards it is not solving the same problem.
In each caseµ =1is not a neutral default. It is an active discarding of the most informative
input, and any method restricted to it is, by construction, inapplicable to the bulk of active portfolio
construction.

Table 2 previews the methods studied in this paper relative to HRP and Cotton. Two columns

4 Tree-based signal-aware allocation
Section 3 showed that López de Prado [2016] HRP and the Cotton [2024] Schur allocator share
a single hierarchical skeleton: at every internal node of a correlation dendrogram, the allocator
solves a2× 2between-branch problem to decide how budget is split between the two children, and
then recurses. Under the minimum-variance restrictionµ =1the two methods are endpoints of a
γ-continuum on that skeleton. The purpose of this section is to extend the same skeleton to an
arbitrary signal µ∈R N and to turn the choice of therepresentative portfolioat each node into the
main design variable of the tree.
Two allocators emerge from this analysis. HRP-µ (Section 4.3) is the transparent one: it uses a
signed inverse-variance portfolio as the representative, which preserves the block-diagonal recovery of
De Prado HRP atµ =1and is provably stable for arbitrary signal vectors. HRP-Σµ (Section 4.4) is
the strongest tree method we know: it uses a recursive mean–variance optimum as the representative
and rescues the natural but sign-pathological recursion of Appendix C by replacing sum-to-one with
L1 normalisation. Both methods costO(N 2), both nest HRP at theirγ = 0, µ =1limits, and both
carry an explicit signal through the tree in a direction-consistent way.
The section closes with two short remarks that tie the construction to the rest of the paper:
why iterating the tree pass diverges (which motivates CRISP in Section 5), and how the tree-based
methods relate to the negative result on sum-normalised recursive MVO that is documented in full
in Appendix C.

4.5 Iterating the tree pass diverges
It is tempting to iterate: after one bottom-up pass produces weightsw(n), update the representative
at each node to reflectw(n), re-run the pass, and loop. For an exact block solver this outer iteration
would converge. For HRP-µ and HRP-Σµ it does not. The reason is structural: each tree pass
compresses the|L|×|R| cross-block covarianceΣLR into a single scalarc at every node, and this
compression is anapproximationof the exact block inverse, not an exact identity. The outer iteration
amplifies the approximation error rather than correcting it; empirically it diverges on the same
synthetic panels on which a single pass is stable. Recovering convergence would require replacing
the scalarc with the full block inverse, restoring anO(N 3)cost per pass with multiple passes on
top—which erases the quadratic-cost advantage that motivated the tree in the first place. The
tension between approximation fidelity and iteration convergence inside the tree is exactly what
CRISP resolves by abandoning the dendrogram altogether and iterating in asset space instead; see
Section 5, Theorem 5.2.
4.6 Pointer to the sum-normalised recursive MVO negative result
The natural recursive MVO withsum-to-onenormalisation—the construction our code calls “method
A1” but which we do not name in the main body—is sign-pathological: it produces output portfolios
whose direction is essentially random relative to the Markowitz direction under estimation noise,
and flips sign systematically even on noiseless problems. Appendix C documents this in detail, with
histograms, worked counterexamples, and a quantitative connection to Corollary 4.7. The allocator
is a cautionary negative result, not a method we recommend; its role in the paper is to explain what
goes wrong when a natural construction is not reinforced by a sign-preserving normaliser. HRP-Σµ
is the fix.

5.7 Shrinkage interpretation and Ledoit–Wolf comparison
At convergence CRISP returnsP−1γ µ, which is exactly Markowitz on the linearly shrunk covariance
Pγ = (1−γ )D +γΣ. CRISP therefore sits inside the Ledoit–Wolf linear-shrinkage family [Ledoit and
Wolf, 2003, 2004, 2017, 2020], and the interest lies not in whether it differs from shrunk Markowitz
in some abstract sense, but in exactly three structural choices:
(1) Target.CRISP uses D = diag(Σ), which preserves variances exactly—only off-diagonal
correlations are regularised. Classical Ledoit–Wolf targets (scaled identity, constant-correlation,
single-factor) all distort the diagonal. The scaled identity replacesσii by the cross-sectional
mean ¯λ/N [Ledoit and Wolf, 2004]; the constant-correlation target keepsσii but imposes a
single off-diagonal correlation for the target [Ledoit and Wolf, 2003]; the single-factor target
substitutes βiβjσ2f +δijσ2ε,i, which rewrites both diagonals and off-diagonals at once. The
CRISP target is the only one that asserts, as a modelling choice, that samplevariancesdo not
need shrinkage.
(2) Intensity criterion.Ledoit–Wolf chooses its intensityαLW to minimise the expected Frobenius
loss E∥ˆΣLW− Σ∥2F—astatisticalcriterion on the covariance estimator. CRISP choosesγ to
maximise out-of-sample portfolio Sharpe—anoperationalcriterion on the portfolio. Section 6
32
makes clear why these need not coincide: a covariance estimator that minimises Frobenius
distance toΣcan still overweight noise-dominated eigendirections once inverted, and the
Frobenius loss does not see that downstream effect. The oracle-approximating shrinkage
(OAS) estimator [Chen et al., 2010] and the nonlinear shrinkage variants [Ledoit and Wolf,
2017, 2020] refine the intensity estimator under the same statistical criterion.
(3) Implementation.Ledoit–Wolf forms ˆΣLW explicitly and inverts with dense Cholesky. CRISP
never formsP−1γ ; Algorithm 2 drivesPγw→µ iteratively inO(κ(D−1Pγ)N 2 log(1/ε))time.
This is an engineering advantage—and it enables the factor-streaming variant of Section 5.6—
but it is not what makes the portfolio better.
The philosophy shift, independent of Stein’s original motivation [Stein, 1956], is the first two
items: CRISP regularises exactly the unreliable part of the covariance and selects the regularisation
strength for the downstream application.
Open question.Is there a Ledoit–Wolf target T and intensity ruleαLW such thatˆΣ−1LWµ =P−1γ⋆ µ
in expectation over a specified sampling distribution, whereγ⋆ is the Sharpe-optimal CRISP
intensity? The empirical Monte Carlo of Section 10 is consistent withγ⋆∈ [0.5, 0.7]across panels,
but we do not have a closed-form correspondence. We flag this as an open problem; Ledoit–Wolf is
included as a head-to-head baseline in every out-of-sample experiment.
5.8 Early stopping as implicit spectral regularisation
Remark5.6 (Early stopping as implicit spectral regularisation).The per-eigendirection content of
Theorem 5.3 has a statistical side-effect whenΣis a finite-sample estimateˆΣ. Gauss–Seidel on
Pγw =µ converges geometrically in each eigendirection ofD−1Pγ: afterp sweeps, the residual on
thek-th eigendirection isO(ρpk)with ρk bounded byρ(M GSγ )< 1and decreasing with the eigenvalue.
Well-conditioned (high-eigenvalue) directions converge exponentially faster than ill-conditioned
(low-eigenvalue) directions. WhenˆΣ is a sample estimate, the low-eigenvalue directions ofD−1ˆPγ
are exactly the directions where Marchenko–Pastur bias [Marchenko and Pastur, 1967] and finite-
sample noise are worst, and the directions direct Markowitz amplifies. Stopping Gauss–Seidel before
convergence therefore implicitly attenuates the portfolio’s exposure to the most noise-contaminated
spectral subspace. The sweep countp plays the role of1/λ in ridge regression: smallp is strong
regularisation (heavy spectral truncation), largep is weak regularisation (recovery of fullˆP−1γ µ).
This isChannel 3of the three-channel decomposition in Section 7; together with operator shrinkage
(Channel 1, driven byγ) and noise cancellation (Channel 2, driven by the targetD), the three
channels act orthogonally on three different spectral regimes ofˆΣ.

Cotton vs. HRP-µ/HRP-Σµ.Cotton and the HRP- µ family share endpoints whenµ =1— a

HRP-µ and HRP-Σµ; the reason is not subtle — the tree is a hard constraint, and whenever the

choices; the methodological contributions of this paper are the systematic relaxation of each axis in

Two regimes render a tree-based method unattractive. First, whenκ(C)is small, every method in

not because hierarchical methods fail but because, in the synthetic experiments of Section 10, it
combines stronger out-of-sample performance with the absence of a dendrogram constraint.

9.3 Constrained HRP-µand HRP-Σµ
The tree methods accommodate two constraint families cleanly.
Long-only.At each internal node, project the between-branch splitα onto[0, 1]; within-branch
weights are clamped to[0,∞ )at the leaf solve. For HRP-Σµ the node-level2× 2QP is replaced by
its box-constrained variant: if both unconstrained components are nonnegative, accept the interior
optimum; otherwise clamp the negative component to zero and allocate fully to the positive side.
Both variants preserve the recursiveO(NlogN)structure.
Sector caps, tree-aligned.When the hierarchy is sector-aligned, a cap on a sector’s total weight is
a cap on the cumulative budget flowing to the corresponding subtree, enforced by a one-dimensional
clip on theα-products along the root-to-subtree path. Tree-aligned constraints are essentially free.
Constraints that cutacrossthe tree—arbitrary sector caps not aligned with the hierarchy, factor-
exposure inequalities, turnover against a non-aligned incumbent—do not admit clean recursion. For
such problems, projected CRISP of Section 9.2 is strictly preferable.

9.5 Discussion
Four caveats are worth making explicit.
First, constraint handling is not a central contribution of this paper. The role of this section is
to show that the HRP-µ/HRP-Σµ/CRISP machinery degrades gracefully; we do not claim projected
CRISP outperforms a modern QP solver on heavily constrained problems. For problems dominated
by constraint activity—index replication with dozens of active inequalities—a specialised active-set
or interior-point solver remains the right tool. The statistical contribution transports cleanly
through (53).
Second, the tree methods handle only tree-aligned constraints cleanly. Long-only, box bounds,
and sector caps on aligned subtrees fit the recursion; general linear inequalities that cut across the
hierarchy do not. For heavily constrained problems the tree methods are therefore not the right
tool; the CRISP shrinkage idea—either via (53) or via projected CRISP—is.
Third, the direction-error analysis of Section 6 does not apply directly to constrained problems.
That analysis rests on the ray invariance of thePγ family: scaling a solution by a positive constant
preserves direction. A constraint set likeℓ≤w≤u is not scale-invariant, so a uniformly scaled
direction may lie inside the box at one scale and outside at another. Adapting the direction metric
to the active face ofC is an interesting question but non-trivial, and we flag it as future work in
Section 11.
Fourth, the out-of-sample evaluation in Section 10 is primarily unconstrained; long-short results
isolate the shrinkage-trajectory effect and let us compare direction errors cleanly. Long-only
comparisons using Algorithm 4 are noted but relegated to Appendix E, or to future work.

11 Discussion
This section is reflective, not generative. We revisit the three regularisation channels that organise
the paper, list open problems and natural extensions, enumerate the limitations of the present study,
and close with a methodological comment on the use of direction error as a diagnostic. No new
results are introduced.
11.1 Three regularisation channels revisited
Remark 7.2 and Section 7.6 identify three distinct mechanisms by which CRISP damps estimation-
error amplification: operator shrinkage throughγ (Channel 1), statistical slack from finite sweep
countp (Channel 2), and eigendirection-ordered convergence of Gauss–Seidel acting as an implicit
spectral truncation (Channel 3, Remark 5.6). The(γ,p )sweep of Table 20 dissects the three channels
on the same synthetic design used elsewhere in the paper, and the pre-registered predictions P1, P2,
P3, and P6 each isolate one channel: P1 probes Channel 1 at lowγ, P2 certifies that Channel 2 is
dormant at the recommended operating point, P3 exposes Channel 3 atγ= 1through an interior
p⋆, and P6 reveals how the clean-signal regime tilts the balance between Channels 1 and 3. The
practical takeaway is compact:at(γ = 0.5, p= 100)CRISP uses Channel 1 only; atγ = 1it relies
on Channel 3; in between, mixtures.For the practitioner this collapses to a single tunable knob,γ,
with p = 100a universal default; Channels 2 and 3 are computational conveniences rather than
statistical parameters in the interior regime. The tree methods HRP-µ and HRP-Σµ expose only
Channel 1 and so inherit the simpler tuning story by construction.
11.2 Signed HSP as a standalone method
Signed Hierarchical Signal Parity (HSP), defined in Section 4.3.1 as theγ = 0endpoint of HRP-
µ, is mathematically a trivial consequence of the constructions in Section 4, but it occupies an
interesting practitioner niche. It is a drop-in upgrade for users of López de Prado [2016] HRP
who now hold an expected-return signal and wish to use it without sacrificing the tree-based
interpretability, dependency-free implementation, and ill-conditioning robustness of the original. All
of HRP’s auditable recursive structure is preserved; the only change is a signed inverse-variance
representative at each node. We do not run a dedicated head-to-head out-of-sample study of signed
HSP against HRP on a realistic cross-section of live alpha signals. Such a study — ideally including
Black–Litterman and Ledoit–Wolf Markowitz as further baselines — is a natural follow-up. The
mathematical content is settled by Section 4; the open question is empirical.

A non-exhaustive list of natural extensions.

• Rank-constrained signals.When µ is known to lie in a low-dimensional factor subspace,

11.5 Computational considerations
The computational case for CRISP is primarily about memory and structure exploitation, not
wall-clock at moderateN. Tuned-BLAS Cholesky on a modern machine solves the dense Markowitz
system in under a millisecond atN = 500, and nothing in this paper beats it on that axis. The
shrinkage iterate does, however, require onlyO(N 2)working memory against Cholesky’s∼ 2N 2, and
more importantly it accommodates the factor-streaming variant (Section 5.6, Algorithm 3), which
reduces working memory toO(NK )whenΣadmits a K-factor decomposition. At the universe
scale —N in the tens of thousands,K in the dozens — this is a three-orders-of-magnitude reduction
and the difference between a workstation and a dedicated server. Cholesky cannot exploit factor
structure in the same way because the factorisation requires random access to the denseΣand
returns a dense triangular factor. The computational argument for CRISP lives here, not in the
FLOP crossover.
11.6 Limitations
We list the main limitations of the present study.
1. Gaussian synthetic returns.The synthetic experiments of Section 10 use Gaussian returns.
Real equity returns are heavier-tailed, and direction-error and OOS-Sharpe rankings could
move under an elliptical or Student-t data-generating process. We do not carry out explicit
heavy-tailed simulation alongside the Gaussian case.
2. No Bayesian treatment ofµ.The signal is taken as given, either as an oracle or as a sample
mean. We do not place a principled prior onµ itself and do not distinguish signal uncertainty
from covariance uncertainty in any theorem. A Black–Litterman-style treatment [Black and
Litterman, 1992] would interact non-trivially with the conjectured Bayesian interpretation of
CRISP flagged in Section 11.3.
3. Tree sensitivity not foregrounded in the main text.Clustering choices (single / average
/ Ward linkage, correlation-based versus Euclidean distance, number of levels) affect every
tree-based method in the paper (HRP, HRP-µ, HRP-Σµ, signed HSP). Robustness across
62
these choices is reported in Appendix E but the main-text analysis beyond Section 8 uses a
single default clustering.
4. Approximate HRP recovery for HRP-Σµ.At γ = 0and µ =1, HRP-Σ µ recovers
HRP only approximately (cos≈ 0.992; exact for trees of depth≤ 2). HRP-µ recovers HRP
exactly under the same conditions (Proposition 4.2). The gap is small enough to be practically
irrelevant but large enough to preclude strict drop-in replacement in settings where exact
HRP replication is contractually required.
5. Cotton comparison restricted to its native problem.Our comparison with Cotton
[2024] is run on the minimum-variance problem for which the Cotton allocator is defined. We
do not test Cotton-style modifications for general mean–variance settings; such extensions
would warrant a separate study.
6. No real-data backtest.This paper is synthetic Monte Carlo and in-sample diagnostics
only. A CRSP- or Russell-style out-of-sample backtest is deliberately out of scope for the
present version and is flagged as future work. The synthetic design was chosen to expose
the direction-error and OOS-Sharpe behaviour cleanly; layering real-data frictions (delisting,
transaction costs, heavy tails, regime shifts) on top is a substantial separate exercise.
11.7 Broader methodological reflection: direction error as a diagnostic
A final comment on methodology. The technical spine of the paper is the direction metric on
Markowitz rays (Definition 2.1), and the core structural observation is that the shrinkage familyPγ
preserves the direction ofµ when the correlation is trivial and otherwise rotates it continuously with
vanishing residual atγ= 1(Theorem 6.1, Corollary 6.3). The ray view is mathematically natural:
mean–variance portfolios are defined up to positive scaling by a budget constraint, so scale-invariant
distance between two portfolios is the correct object of study, and the metric is compact.
Scale invariance is, however, a two-edged virtue. A metric that identifiesw with any positive
multiple ofw is one step away from a metric that identifiesw with−w, and that second identification
would be blind to the most dangerous failure mode of any portfolio construction: returning the
right magnitude on the wrong side of the market. Appendix C exhibits noiseless problems on which
the sum-normalised recursive MVO does exactly this, and a naively sign-invariant diagnostic would
score those failures as near-perfect. It was easy to miss — we missed it ourselves in early drafts,
and the bug persisted precisely because the summary metric looked healthy.
The methodological lesson is simple: scale-invariant metrics must be paired with sign-sensitive
companions before being trusted as quality measures. In this paper we pair the unsigned direction
error with three sign-sensitive diagnostics — signed cosine, coordinate-wise sign-match fraction, and
signed direction angle on(−π,π ]— and we present them alongside out-of-sample Sharpe so that
tracking (an in-sample quantity) is never separated from quality (an out-of-sample quantity). A
method that tracks an oracle perfectly in unsigned direction but loses Sharpe is telling a different
story from one that gains Sharpe while drifting in direction, and the reader should see both axes at
once. We encourage future work on portfolio construction to adopt the same discipline: for every
scale-invariant summary metric, report a sign-sensitive companion and an out-of-sample quality
metric, before drawing any conclusion about which method is better.

12 Conclusion
The gap and how we fill it
HRP and Cotton are signal-blind: both solve the minimum-variance problemµ =1and discard the
expected-return signal that every active process produces. We close that gap with three signal-aware
allocators—HRP-µ, HRP-Σµ, and CRISP—unified by a single shrinkage parameterγ∈ [0, 1]that
controls how much cross-asset correlation enters the allocation. At(γ,µ ) = (0, 1)the three recover
the signal-blind benchmarks exactly.
Three methods, one spectrum
The methods form a spectrum of increasing covariance exploitation at increasing cost and decreasing
transparency. HRP-µ (Section 4.3) uses a signed inverse-variance representative; it is closed-form,
O(N 2), preserves the hierarchical audit trail, and recovers58–67%of oracle Sharpe. HRP-Σµ
(Section 4.4) replaces the signed IVP with a local mean–variance optimum, giving the strongest
tree-based method at roughly90%of CRISP’s Sharpe. CRISP (Section 5) abandons the tree and
solves the shrunk systemPγw = µ by Gauss–Seidel, dominating every benchmark at80–94%of
oracle Sharpe.
The core statistical insight
Solve theshrunkMarkowitz system, not the exact one. The shrinkage atγ <1damps the estimation
noise that full inversion amplifies, and the effect is statistical, not computational: it holds whether
the solver is faster or slower than Cholesky. The OOS Sharpe surface is nearly flat inγ across a
wide plateau, soγ = 0.5is a robust default. At the recommended operating point the sweep count
pis acomputationalparameter, not a statistical one—OOS Sharpe is flat fromp≈50onward.

What this paper does not resolve
Six open questions remain (see Section 11 for the long form): a sharp worst-case characterisation

distance choices that determine the tree; and a real-data out-of-sample backtest on CRSP or Russell
panels. Each is tractable with the machinery assembled here, and each is deferred to later work.

C Sum-normalised recursive MVO and the sign-flip pathology
This appendix documents, as a cautionary negative result, the first hierarchical signal-aware variant
we tried in the development of the tree methods of Section 4. The construction — a recursive
mean–variance tree pass that normalises each node’s pair of child budgets to sum to one — is
the obvious generalisation of López de Prado [2016] HRP to a signedµ, and for that reason it
is the obvious trap. We refer to it throughout asMethod A1orsum-normalised recursive MVO,
matching the codebase name method_a1_weights. The conclusion reached in this appendix is
straightforward: Method A1 is structurally broken as a portfolio construction, its output direction
bears no fixed relationship to the oracle Markowitz portfoliow⋆ = Σ−1µ, and no choice ofγ,
sample size, or covariance estimator repairs it. The appendix is included because theformof the
failure — a cumulative sign flip that sign-invariant direction metrics fail to report — is a useful
worked illustration of two methodological points: (i) sign-invariant error metrics must be paired
with sign-sensitive diagnostics, and (ii) any hierarchical method forsignal-dependentallocation must
carry sign information in a form the recursive2× 2normaliser cannot invert. HRP-µ (Section 4.3)
satisfies (ii) via leaf-level signed inverse-variance representatives; HRP-Σµ(Section 4.4) satisfies it
viaL 1 normalisation. Method A1 satisfies neither, and is never recommended.

C.1 The A1 construction
Method A1 uses the standard between-branch2× 2system of Section 4.1. At an internal node with
children L,R and cluster statistics(vL,vR,sL,sR,c )defined by the child representatives ˆwL,ˆwR
recursively, the Cramer solution(23) produces a scalar pair of raw budgets(αrawL ,α rawR ). Method A1
normalises them by their algebraic sum:
αL = αrawLαrawL +α rawR , α R = αrawRαrawL +α rawR ,(57)
so thatαL +αR = 1at every node. The within-branch representative propagated up to the parent
is the recursive A1 weightˆwv = (αL ˆwL, αR ˆwR); leaves bottom out withˆw= (1). The root returns
a portfolio that sums algebraically to one.
The construction is superficially appealing: atµ =1and γ = 0it reduces to flat HRP, and the
recursion isO(N 2). It fails for a reason that has nothing to do with the2× 2solve and everything
to do with (57).
C.2 The sign-flip mechanism
The sign pathology lives entirely in the denominatorαrawL +αrawR . When µ≥ 0, the raw budgets are
non-negative and their sum is positive, so(57) is the ordinary convex-combination step of HRP.
When µ has mixed signs — the generic case for any signal built from alphas, momentum residuals, or
factor tilts — the representative signals(sL,sR)entering the node system can be positive, negative,
or near zero. The2× 2solve then produces raw budgets of arbitrary signs, and their algebraic sum
has no sign guarantee whatsoever. Whenever that sum is negative, division by itflips both signs in
the child pair(αL,αR)simultaneously.
The flip is local and it is cumulative. Across thelog2N levels of the dendrogram, the sign pattern
of the root output is the product of theO(logN )level-wise denominator signs, none of which carry
any fixed relationship tosign(Σ−1µ)i at the leaves. A sign-carrying linear object —w⋆i is linear in
µ — cannot be recovered from a chain of sum-to-one divisions whose denominators are themselves
signed. That is the whole story; the remainder of this appendix is empirical corroboration.

C.5 The fix:L 1 normalisation
The fix is a single character. Replace the algebraic-sum denominator in(57) with an absolute-value
sum:
αL = αrawL|αrawL |+|α rawR |, α R = αrawR|αrawL |+|α rawR |.(58)
This is the HRP-Σµ step of Section 4.4; the codebase calls the constructionmethod_a1_l1_weights.
The denominator|αrawL | +|αrawR | is always strictly positive whenever the2× 2system has a non-zero
solution, so no sign flip can occur; the signs of(αrawL ,α rawR )are preserved exactly, and the propagated
representative carries the Markowitz direction through the recursion. Lemma 4.6 and Corollary 4.7
give the formal statement: HRP-Σµ either agrees with A1 (when all node denominators are already
positive) or returns the global sign-flipped twin of A1 with identical∥w∥1. On every row of Table 27

C.6 Why the direction-error metric does not catch this
A reader who has only consulted thedir(w) = sin2θ(w,w⋆)panels of Section 10 would not know
Method A1 is broken. The direction error issign-invariant: it measures the squared sine of the
angle betweenw and w⋆ and therefore cannot distinguish a portfolio withcos = +0.85from one
with cos =−0.85. Both yielddir = 1− 0.852≈ 0.28. A1 populates the latter case roughly half the
time, anddiraggregates the two cases into a single superficially-respectable number of about0.28
on the Panel A2 diagnostic of Section 10.2.3 — exactly while the method is producing the wrong
sign on half the trials.
This is the cleanest illustration of a general methodological point. A metric that collapses±w
onto the same equivalence class is appropriate only for long-only or inherently sign-fixed allocators,
and it will silently reward methods whose signs are uncorrelated withsign(Σ−1µ)i. For any signal-
aware construction the operational metric must be thesignedcosinecos(w,w⋆)or equivalently the
fraction of trials in the wrong half-space; Tables 27–28 and Figure 8 are the first places in this paper
where that distinction is load-bearing. The lesson generalises: sign-invariant metrics must always be
paired with a sign-sensitive diagnostic before a method is declared acceptable.
C.7 Conclusion
Method A1 — sum-normalised recursive MVO — should not be used for portfolio construction.
Its failure is not a tuning problem and not a noise problem; it is a structural incompatibility
between a sum-to-one normalisation step and a signal that can take either sign at each node. The
appendix illustrates two general lessons. First, the sign-invariance trap: the direction-error metric
dir(w)reports an acceptable number (≈ 0.28) on Method A1 exactly while A1 is antiparallel to
w⋆ half the time; sign-invariant metrics must be paired with sign-sensitive diagnostics, without
exception. Second, the design constraint for hierarchical signal-aware allocation: such a method
must carry sign information through the recursion in a form the2× 2normaliser cannot invert.
HRP-µ (Section 4.3) satisfies this by using signed inverse-variance representatives at the leaves,
which enter the node-level solve additively and are not subject to recursive sum-normalisation.
HRP-Σµ (Section 4.4) satisfies it by replacing sum-to-one withL1 normalisation, which preserves
all node-level signs. The sum-normalised variant is retained in the codebase asmethod_a1_weights
only as a reproducible counterexample against which future recursive signal-aware schemes can be
compared.

E.2 Sensitivity to ridge regularisation
All three preconditioned methods add a small ridgeλI to ˆΣ before inversion or Gauss–Seidel sweeps.
Table 31 sweepsλ∈{ 10−8, 10−6, 10−4, 10−2} on the base universe withT = 240and oracle µ. The
CRISP curves are flat within rounding over[10−8, 10−4]and drop only atλ = 10−2, where the ridge
becomes comparable to the diagonal ofˆΣ and acts as real shrinkage instead of a numerical floor.
HRP-µ is equally insensitive in the interior. The interpretation is that the ridge serves only to
guarantee solvability of the inverse; it is not a second shrinkage knob sitting behindγ.
E.3 Sensitivity to tree construction
CRISP is tree-free: it never instantiates a dendrogram, so the linkage row in Table 32 is exactly
constant. The row is listed for the record. HRP-µ shows modest variation of roughly0.03in OOS
Sharpe across linkages — a reflection of the induced block structure. De Prado HRP, which discards
µ entirely and is included as aµ-blind benchmark, is substantially more brittle: single linkage yields
anegativeOOS Sharpe, consistent with the chaining pathology noted in López de Prado [2016].
The gap between the tree-free and tree-based methods widens precisely when the tree is worst.

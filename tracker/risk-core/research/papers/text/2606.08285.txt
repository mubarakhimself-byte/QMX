Execution Realism and Reproducibility in LLM-Based Trading Systems:
A Systematic Scoping Review and Evidence Audit
Junyi Yao∗1, Zihao Zheng †1, and Baichuan Li ‡2
1Washington University in St. Louis, St. Louis, MO 63130, USA
2Department of Operations Research and Engineering Management, Southern Methodist University,
Dallas, TX 75275, USA
Abstract
Execution realism remains weakly standardized in re-
search on large-language-model-based trading systems,
limiting comparison and reproduction across backtests,
simulations, and portfolio benchmarks. This article
presents a PRISMA-ScR systematic scoping review with a
nested execution-reproducibility evidence audit. Eight re-
producible query families returned 796 query-level records;
DOI/title deduplication left 687 unique records, 101 re-
ports were sought for retrieval, and 59 full texts were
recovered through direct and fallback open-access routes.
Fifty-three reports are provisionally eligible for expanded
evidence charting, subject to reconciliation of two blind
author-validation packets. The charting framework sep-
arately evaluates point-in-time controls, temporal splits,
held-out evaluation, trading costs and turnover, execu-
tion semantics, universe construction, architecture report-
ing, and artifact availability. Direct-trading, portfolio or
alpha-construction, and benchmark studies are retained
as distinct descriptive subgroups rather than pooled as
a common performance estimand. Search responses, re-
trieval attempts, file hashes, evidence excerpts, screening
decisions, and coding worksheets are archived with the
review materials. Final field-level counts are intentionally
withheld until author validation is complete.
Keywords:algorithmic trading; financial artificial in-
telligence; large language models; PRISMA-ScR; repro-
ducibility; systematic scoping review.
1 Introduction
Execution realism has become a central comparability
problem in research on large-language-model-based trad-
ing. Large language models (LLMs) now support finance-
oriented retrieval, reasoning, decision support, and au-
tonomous workflows. Recent systems gather heteroge-
neous information, maintain memory, deliberate over mar-
ket scenarios, and coordinate analyst, trader, and risk-

management roles [1, 2, 3, 4]. Their architectural appeal
is clear: financial decisions combine prices, filings, news,
macroeconomic signals, and portfolio constraints under
changing regimes [5, 6, 7].
Evaluation practice has not matured at the same pace.
A reported return depends not only on the decision model
but also on when information became available, when
orders could execute, whether costs and slippage were
charged, how the asset universe was formed, how out-of-
sample periods were selected, and whether code, prompts,
model versions, and data snapshots were documented.
Empirical finance has long shown that data snooping,
backtest overfitting, and omitted trading costs can inflate
apparent performance [8, 9, 10, 11]. LLM workflows add
further provenance and version-control risks because lan-
guage, retrieval, memory, and tool use may each alter the
effective information set.
Existing surveys provide valuable architecture-centered
maps of financial LLMs and trading agents [1, 12]. The
present study addresses a different question: among
LLM-based systems connected to trading, portfolio con-
struction, alpha research, market simulation, or trade-
relevant decisions, which execution and reproducibility
assumptions are recoverable from the public record, and
where do reporting gaps prevent economic interpreta-
tion or independent reproduction? We answer this ques-
tion through a bounded systematic scoping review re-
ported with PRISMA-ScR and PRISMA-S guidance, to-
gether with a nested study-level evidence audit. Unlike
architecture-centered evidence maps, the unit of analysis
here is the public evidence supporting each execution as-
sumption: every code is tied to a study-level record and
recoverable passage, and heterogeneous system types are
synthesized only within descriptive subgroups.
The contributions are as follows:
1. We define and archive a reproducible, date-bounded
search, deduplication, screening, retrieval, and
version-reconciliation workflow for trade-relevant
LLM studies.
2. We chart point-in-time discipline, temporal splits,
held-out evaluation, transaction costs and turnover,

3. We separate machine-assisted retrieval and passage

6. We derive a minimum reporting checklist for future
authors, reviewers, and benchmark designers.
2 Related Work: From Agent De-
signs to Evaluation Assumptions
2.1 Financial LLM Infrastructure and Re-
view Scope
Financial language models and agent platforms provide
the infrastructure on which trading systems are built.
BloombergGPT and FinGPT emphasize finance-specific
corpora and tasks [13, 14]; FinanceBench, FinBen, and
related evaluations span question answering, extraction,
forecasting, risk, and decision support [15, 16, 17, 18]. Fin-
Robot connects models to retrieval, analytical tools, and
workflow orchestration [19]. Adjacent work on factor de-
composition and bankruptcy prediction from filings shows
how structured market variables and textual evidence are
increasingly combined [20, 21].
These studies motivate, but do not automatically enter,
the primary evidence set. Financial question answering,
sentiment analysis, retrieval, or forecasting is included
only when the study establishes an explicit path from
model output to a tradable decision, portfolio, market
simulation, or evaluation benchmark. That boundary
distinguishes general financial NLP from claims whose
interpretation depends on execution assumptions [22, 23].
2.2 Architecture-Centered Surveys and
Systems
Prior surveys map trading-agent architectures, inputs,
and reported performance [1, 12]. Memory and reflection
systems include FinMem and reflective cryptocurrency
agents [5, 7]. Multi-agent systems decompose work among
specialized roles or simulated participants [2, 3, 4, 24, 25,
26,27]. Multimodalandtool-augmentedsystemsfusetext,
market data, visual signals, routing, and computational
traces [6, 28, 29]. Alpha-mining and hybrid policy systems
place LLMs closer to factor discovery, signal generation,
and quantitative research automation [30, 31, 32, 33, 34,
35].
Architecture families explain design intent but do not
make reported returns comparable. Memory, debate,
and multimodal systems can all report performance un-
der materially different information sets, fill rules, costs,
universes, and validation windows. The present review
therefore treats architecture as a study characteristic and
execution-reproducibility reporting as the primary chart-
ing target.
More generally, persistent agent memory creates an au-
dit problem because stale or faulty records can propagate
into later reasoning and actions. Execution-provenance
research organizes traces and evidence dependencies for
process-level accountability [36]; provenance-aware shared
memory additionally uses lineage, access constraints, and
action-risk gates [37]. Transactional belief commits and
dependency-guided rollback illustrate complementary con-
trols for preventing unsafe state propagation and selec-
tively repairing affected actions [38, 39]. These studies
provide background for the review’s emphasis on recover-
able execution records, but they are not treated as primary
evidence about trading performance.
Outside finance, agentic reasoning, program-guided ex-
ecution, and reusable policy routines illustrate broader
ways to decompose long-horizon decisions into explicit
components [40, 41, 42]. These works motivate archi-
tecture descriptors but do not provide evidence about
financial execution realism; accordingly, they are treated
only as background and are excluded from the primary
trading-study audit.
Likewise, work on grounded neural representations and
probabilistic object tracking illustrates how upstream
perception modules can expose structured intermediate
representations [43, 44]. Such perception research is rele-
vant only to the modular-systems context; it is not used
to support claims about trading performance, costs, or
reproducibility.
2.3 Benchmarks, Portfolios, and Execu-
tion Realism
InvestorBench, AI-Trader, PortBench, Look-Ahead-
Bench, and LLM market simulations move the liter-
ature toward structured financial decision evaluation
[45, 46, 47, 48, 49]. Point-in-time and event-driven designs
are particularly important because they reduce retrospec-
tive leakage. Portfolio settings add allocation, correlation,
turnover, and regime sensitivity, while calibrated techni-
cal strategies show that parameter and friction sensitiv-
ity matter even without an LLM [50]. Recent audits of
foundation-model evaluation likewise show that aggregate
benchmark results can conceal regime-dependent failures
[51]. Related work embeds foundation-model predictions
in a structurally constrained discrete-choice model, il-
2
lustrating how predictive components can be coupled to
explicit behavioral and economic guarantees [52]. Adver-
sarial news and live or simulated evaluations further test
whether apparent skill survives temporal separation and
manipulation [53, 46, 49].
Execution assumptions are often mundane but deci-
sive. Same-close fills can overstate performance when a
signal uses closing information; high turnover can erase
gross returns after commissions, spread, and slippage; fu-
ture constituents introduce survivorship bias; and closed
model endpoints without version records impede repro-
duction. These observations motivate the evidence fields
and reporting checklist used in this review.
3 Systematic Scoping-Review Pro-
tocol
3.1 Review Design and Question
This study is designed as a systematic scoping review
with a nested execution-reproducibility evidence audit.
Reporting follows PRISMA-ScR [54], the PRISMA 2020
flow logic [55], and PRISMA-S search-reporting principles
[56]. Ascopingdesignisappropriatebecausetheliterature
spans direct trading agents, portfolio and alpha-research
systems, market simulations, and decision benchmarks
with incompatible outcomes, markets, horizons, and eval-
uation protocols. A pooled meta-analysis would therefore
imply a common estimand that the source literature does
not provide.
The review asks: among LLM-based or LLM-agent sys-
tems connected to trading, portfolio construction, alpha
research, market simulation, or trade-relevant financial
decisions, which execution and reproducibility assump-
tions are publicly recoverable, and where do reporting
gaps prevent economic interpretation or independent re-
production?
The publication window is January 1, 2023 through
May 30, 2026. English-language journal articles, confer-
ence papers, accessible preprints, and technical reports are
eligible. Secondary reviews are retained for background
and citation searching but are not coded as primary ev-
idence. The review was not prospectively registered. A
dated protocol reconstructed and frozen during the post-
rejection redesign is supplied with the review materials;
this absence of prospective registration is treated as a
limitation rather than obscured.
3.2 Information Sources and Search
Eight query families cover large language models, LLM
agents, multi-agent trading, portfolio management, al-
pha mining, financial backtesting, and quantitative trad-
ing. The machine-readable search script records the ex-
act strings, date bounds, returned metadata, query-level
counts, and OpenAlex identifiers. The search was re-
run on October 4, 2026 while preserving the prespeci-
fied publication cutoff of May 30, 2026. Because several
broad query families produced very large result sets, the
bounded search retained the first 100 relevance-ranked
OpenAlex records per query family. This cap makes the
review reproducible but introduces ranking-based selec-
tion risk; the review is systematic within the declared
bounds rather than an exhaustive census. Citation search-
ing from retained studies and prior financial-LLM reviews
supplements the database search. Source versions are
reconciled by DOI and normalized title, with the most
complete accessible report retained.
The bounded electronic search archived 796 query-level
records. DOI/title deduplication removed 109 records,
leaving 687 unique records for title and abstract screen-
ing. The raw JSON response, deduplicated CSV, search
summary, scripts, and retrieval logs are included in the
review materials so the search can be rerun or audited.
3.3 Eligibility and Screening
Eligibility is based on population, concept, and context.
The population is an LLM, foundation model, or LLM-
agent system. The concept is an empirical trading, port-
folio, alpha-factor, market-simulation, or trade-relevant
benchmark evaluation. The context is securities, cryp-
tocurrencies, investment portfolios, or financial decisions
with an explicit path to tradable action. Pure finan-
cial NLP, generic forecasting without a decision path,
non-investment trading contexts, tutorials, editorials, and
secondary reviews are excluded from the primary evidence
set.
Records are screened in two stages. Title/abstract
screening applies cascading, fixed rules: concept screening
first requires an LLM/agent term and a finance/trading
term in the title or indexed abstract; title-level scope re-
finement then requires an LLM/agent and trade-relevant
term in the title; and metadata/abstract triage removes
secondary reviews, non-investment trading contexts, and
conceptual or advisory studies without an empirical trade-
evaluation claim. Together these rules provisionally ex-
clude 586 records: 280 without a finance/trading concept,
28 without an LLM/agent concept, 260 at title-level scope
refinement, eight secondary reviews or perspectives, seven
non-investment trading records, and three conceptual or
advisory records. The remaining 101 reports proceed to
retrieval. Full-text screening uses E5–E10 and inclusion
codes I1–I3. Machine-assisted triage is used only to prior-
itize retrieval and expose potentially relevant passages; it
does not constitute a final eligibility decision. Direct PDF
links, arXiv DOI reconstruction, SSRN delivery links, DOI
landing pages, and open-access page links are attempted
in sequence. Every retrieval attempt and successful file
hash is logged.
The audit measures reporting recoverability, not pre-
dictive validity, study quality, or risk of bias. A Y code
means that an execution-relevant assumption can be re-
constructed or stress-tested from public evidence; it does
3
not imply that the assumption is economically realistic
or that the reported strategy is profitable. Conversely,
NR records absence of recovered evidence rather than
proof that the underlying implementation omitted the
feature. This distinction prevents the charting scheme
from becoming an unsupported composite quality score.

3.4 Data Charting and Audit Variables
For each provisionally eligible report, the charting form
records study role, market and task scope, temporal
split or point-in-time controls, held-out evaluation, cost
or turnover treatment, execution semantics, universe
construction, system-architecture reporting, and artifact
availability. “Y” denotes explicit evidence detailed enough
to reconstruct or stress-test the field, “P” denotes partial
or indirect recoverability, “NR” denotes that no supporting
evidence was recovered, and “N/A” denotes a field gen-
uinely outside the study’s scope. Architecture is charted
as a descriptive-reporting variable, not as evidence of
trading validity; this prevents architecture clarity from
being asserted without direct coding.
The extraction pipeline first identifies candidate pas-
sages using fixed regular expressions and stores the sur-
rounding text. Two blind validation packets then present
the study, field, and evidence excerpt without the machine-
proposed code. Each author assigns Y, P, NR, or N/A
and supplies a page or section anchor. Disagreements
are reconciled by consensus. Until this reconciliation is
complete, generated entries and aggregate counts remain
provisional and are not used as final inferential claims.
3.5 Synthesis
The synthesis is descriptive rather than meta-analytic.
Final reporting will provide the PRISMA-ScR flow, study
characteristics, field-level recoverability counts, direct-
trading versus portfolio/alpha versus benchmark sub-
groups, and study-level caveats. No composite quality
ranking is constructed because field applicability differs
across study types and recoverability is not equivalent to
empirical validity.

4 Provisional Review Results and
Discussion
4.1 Search Yield and Retrieval
The updated bounded search materially expands the ev-
idence base beyond the earlier targeted review. Across
eight query families, 796 query-level records were archived
and 687 unique DOI/title records remained after dedu-
plication. Cascading title/abstract rules provisionally
removed 586 records: 308 failed the broad concept screen,
260 failed stricter title-level scope refinement, and 18
were secondary, non-investment, or non-empirical records
identified during metadata/abstract triage. Full text was
sought for 101 reports. Direct and fallback retrieval recov-
ered 59 reports, while 42 could not be retrieved through
the recorded open-access routes. This retrieval gap is
itself a review limitation and is reported separately from
substantive exclusion.
The evidence scan prioritized 53 reports for full charting
because their retrieved text contained both an empirical-
evaluation signal and a trading, portfolio, alpha, or bench-
mark outcome. Six retrieved reports had insufficient em-
pirical signal under the fixed rules and await author con-
firmation of their exclusion code. These counts remain
provisional until the two blind eligibility and field-level
coding packets are reconciled.
The provisional evidence set also reflects the field’s
publication maturity. OpenAlex source metadata and
persistent identifiers classify 13 reports as journal, confer-
ence, thesis, or other formally indexed venue records and
40 as preprint or repository versions. The review there-
fore separates stable methodological foundations from
rapidly changing system reports and treats version recon-
ciliation as part of evidence charting. Preprint prevalence
is reported as a characteristic of the evidence base, not
concealed through selective citation replacement.
4.2 Preliminary Reporting Pattern
The machine-assisted charting pass does not replace au-
thor coding, but it provides a reproducible diagnostic of
where evidence is likely to be found. Among the 53 provi-
sional rows, temporal-split and held-out-evaluation pas-
sages are recovered more often than implementation-ready
cost passages. Execution language is heterogeneous: some
reports identify next-day, market-order, fill-price, or simu-
lator semantics, whereas others only mention rebalancing
or generic trade execution. Artifact evidence ranges from
explicit repository links to partial data-availability state-
ments.
The pattern is directionally consistent with the orig-
inal 30-study audit, but the expanded search changes
the denominator and introduces newer peer-reviewed,
benchmark, cryptocurrency, and portfolio studies. The
final manuscript therefore must not simply transplant
the earlier aggregate counts. Final Y/P/NR/N/A totals,
subgroup denominators, and study-level caveats will be
recalculated after validation.
4.3 Implications for Evidence Quality
Three implications already follow from the expanded re-
trieval record. First, review transparency is inseparable
from version control: preprints, conference versions, jour-
nal versions, and repository copies frequently overlap.
Longitudinal evidence that benchmark scores drift across
open-source model release lines reinforces the need to
record the exact model and evaluation version [100]. Sec-
ond, failure to retrieve a report is not evidence that the
report lacks execution detail, so retrieval failures must not
be coded as NR. Third, keyword presence is not equiva-
lent to implementation-ready reporting. A passage that
mentions transaction costs without specifying the rate or
application rule supports at most partial recoverability.
These distinctions sharpen the paper’s contribution.
The review is not a leaderboard of agent architectures
and does not infer that more detailed reporting implies
stronger predictive performance. It maps whether the
public record permits readers to reconstruct when infor-
mation became available, when a trade could execute,
how frictions were applied, and which artifacts support
replication.

5 Conclusion
This systematic scoping-review redesign broadens the evi-
dentiary basis of the paper and makes its search, retrieval,
and coding decisions auditable. The provisional search
record shows that LLM-based trading research is larger
and more heterogeneous than the original targeted sample
suggested. At the same time, the expanded evidence chart-
ing continues to indicate that architecture descriptions
alone are insufficient for judging economic interpretability
or reproducibility.
The final contribution will be a PRISMA-ScR flow, a
version-reconciled study ledger, field-level evidence an-
chors, subgroup summaries, and a reporting checklist
centered on point-in-time controls, held-out evaluation,
execution semantics, trading frictions, universe construc-
tion, and artifact release. The review deliberately avoids
using a non-LLM heuristic as a proxy for an LLM trading
system; methodological conclusions are grounded in the
retrieved literature and its public evidence record.
Final numerical conclusions are intentionally withheld
from this internal draft until both author-validation pack-
ets are reconciled. This constraint prevents machine-
assisted retrieval and passage detection from being misre-
ported as completed human evidence assessment.

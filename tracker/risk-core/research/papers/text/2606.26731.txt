Robust Hedging Valuation Adjustment under Liquidity–Demand
Stress
Takayuki Sakuma∗
Soka University
Abstract
This paper develops a robust hedging valuation adjustment (HVA) measure for dynamic hedging.
Simulated rebalancing and maturity-unwind trades generate a loss distribution for each no-trade-band
rule, and we define robust HVA as the worst-case expected loss over a relative-entropy neighborhood
of that distribution. Because band width affects turnover, the same relative-entropy radius applied to
different bands can imply different levels of demand–liquidity stress. We distinguish a fixed-radius con-
vention from a fixed benchmark-stress convention and show that wider no-trade bands lower rebalancing
costs but raise hedge-error risk.
Keywords:hedging valuation adjustment; transaction costs; liquidity–demand stress; relative entropy;
hedge-error risk.
1 Introduction
Dynamic hedging is not frictionless: discrete rebalancing, bid–ask spreads, and market impact can make
even standard derivative hedges costly. Burnett [Burnett, 2020] formulates hedging valuation adjustment
(HVA) as the effect of transaction costs on derivative portfolio value. Burnett and Williams [Burnett and
Williams, 2021] extend this idea to the cost of hedging XVA, including frictions from hedging the underlying
asset and counterparty credit. Related HVA research also examines frictions beyond direct transaction costs.
Albanese et al. [2022] link HVA to model risk, Bénézet and Crépey [2024] discuss its role in XVA model-
risk management, and Bénézet et al. [2026] study callable-product settings where recalibration and hedging
adjustments interact.
Our focus on liquidity costs relates to the literature on hedging with transaction costs. Leland [1985]
studies modified-volatility replication under transaction costs. Davis et al. [1993] develop utility-based val-
uation and hedging. Whalley and Wilmott [1997] derive small-transaction-cost asymptotic approximations
for the no-transaction region. Soner et al. [1995] show that nontrivial replication breaks down under pro-
portional transaction costs. Jump risk is also an issue for hedging with frictions. Kennedy et al. [2009]
study dynamic hedging under jump diffusion with transaction costs and Sepp [2012] derives approximate
delta-hedging error distributions under jump diffusion with discrete trading and transaction costs.
In this paper, we turn hedging costs into stressed valuation adjustments by applying entropy-based robust
risk measurement. For risk measurement, Kullback–Leibler (KL), or relative-entropy, neighborhoods around
a baseline distribution provide tractable worst-case expectations [Glasserman and Xu, 2014]. We compare
robust HVA across hedge policies under liquidity and demand stress, and our approach lets each policy
generate its own turnover, hedge-error risk, and empirical loss distribution. We focus on no-trade-band delta
hedging, where the hedge is updated only when the target delta moves beyond a specified band width. The
costs of rebalancing define the empirical HVA loss sample for that policy. This policy dependence creates a
practical comparison issue. Applying a different band to the same simulated scenarios alters turnover, the
hedge-demand measure, and the empirical HVA loss sample. A common relative-entropy radius fixes the
admissible probability tilt but not necessarily the economic demand and liquidity stress. A lower robust HVA
for one band might merely reflect a lower effective stress level, rather than a genuinely safer hedge policy.

Wider no-trade bands reduce rebalancing costs and robust HVA, though they increase hedge-error risk.

4 Robust HVA increment
We use two stress measures. The first is the Kullback–Leibler radiusε, which controls how far the worst-case
scenario weights may move from the baseline empirical weights. The second is the Gaussian benchmark-stress
labelρ G, which translates the KL stress increment into a demand–illiquidity stress.
The Gaussian benchmark is a one-parameter translation convention. The parameterρ G changes the
rank pairing between turnover and illiquidity while preserving their marginal distributions. Kendall’sτfrom
data is mapped to the Gaussian rank-correlation scale throughρ= sin(πτ/2). This distinction is important
because the KL stress is applied to the loss sample generated by each hedge policy. Different policies lead to
different trading paths, and thus to varying turnover, trade-size, and distributions. Consequently, applying
the same numerical KL radius across policies can mix the chosen stress severity with policy-driven changes
in the loss distribution.
The KL stress increment over the baseline HVA for bandbis
∆KLb (ε) = HVAε(b)− HVA0(b), HVA0(b) = HVAind(b).(9)
3
To interpret this increment, we compare it with a Gaussian rank-coupling benchmark. For each band, the
benchmark keeps the simulated marginal distributions of turnover and illiquidity fixed, but changes their
rank pairing. WhenρG = 0, high-turnover paths are not consistently paired with high-illiquidity paths. As
ρG increases, high-turnover paths become more frequently paired with high-illiquidity paths, increasing the
benchmark HVA cost.
LetC Gb (ρG)represent the average HVA cost in this Gaussian benchmark; Appendix B describes how
CGb (ρG)is constructed. We define the benchmark increment as
∆Gb (ρG) =C Gb (ρG)−C Gb (0).(10)
Hence∆Gb (ρG)measures the increase in benchmark HVA cost when the turnover and illiquidity rank coupling
changes from0toρ G.
The finite-sample benchmark increment curve consists of the gridρGj ↦→∆ Gb (ρGj ), and Monte Carlo noise
can cause this grid to be nonmonotone. The inverse map therefore applies the monotone envelope
~∆Gb (ρGj ) = maxk≤j ∆Gb (ρGk )
and the benchmark-stress label linked to a KL increment is
ρGeq(b;ε) = inf{ρG : ~∆Gb (ρG)≥∆ KLb (ε)}.(11)
Conversely, the KL radius required to match a benchmark-stress levelρG0 is
εreq(b;ρG0 ) = inf{ε: ∆ KLb (ε)≥ ~∆Gb (ρG0 )}.(12)
These definitions provide two conventions for comparing policies. The fixed-radius convention setsε(b) =
εfixed for allb. The fixed benchmark-stress convention setsε(b) =εreq(b;ρG0 ). The first keeps the admissible
probability tilt constant. The second keeps the benchmark-stress level constant and allows the required KL
radius to vary by band. It does not keep a common dollar increment constant across bands, since each band
has its own turnover and effective-liquidity marginal scales.
In practice, a risk committee or model-governance process can set the stress labelρG0 . This determines the
severity of the demand–illiquidity stress used to compare hedge policies. For each bandb, the benchmark
increment ~∆Gb (ρG0 )is calculated from that band’s turnover and effective-illiquidity marginal scales. The
required KL radiusε req(b;ρG0 )is then selected so that the KL stress increment equals this benchmark
increment. HenceρG0 is common across policies, whileεreq, robust HVA, and hedge-error risk remain specific
to each policy. The resulting robust HVA represents the reserve cost of a hedge policy under a common
benchmark-stress label, and it should be considered together with the tracking-error measureTE(b)defined
in Section 5.4.

5.2 Reference benchmark-stress level
The fixed benchmark-stress calculations useρ G0 = 0.4. In the Gaussian benchmark,ρ G represents how
closely demand and illiquidity ranks move together. In the simulated HVA samples, demand arises from
hedge turnover. Since trade-level hedge turnover is not available in public daily data, we rely on a related
public-data reference: the co-movement between market stress and market illiquidity.
We use the CBOE VIX series from FRED as the measure of market stress [Chicago Board Options
Exchange, 2026]. Daily SPY prices and volumes are taken from Stooq [Stooq.com, 2026]. Using the SPY
data, we compute the Amihud illiquidity measure

The calculation uses 756-trading-day rolling windows, roughly three years, and shifts the window forward

6 Conclusion
This paper studies robust hedging valuation adjustment under liquidity–demand stress. The robust layer
applies exponential tilting to simulated HVA loss samples generated by no-trade-band hedging policies.
Because the hedge policy changes turnover, it also changes the empirical loss distribution to which the
KL ball is applied. We distinguish two comparison conventions: a fixed-radius convention that keeps the
relative-entropy radius constant across hedge bands, and a fixed benchmark-stress convention that keeps
the benchmark demand–liquidity stress label constant while allowing the required KL radius to vary by
band. The numerical results show that the difference is small in high-liquidity markets but larger under low
liquidity. Wider no-trade bands lower rebalancing costs and robust HVA, yet they raise hedge-error risk. The
gamma sensitivity analysis indicates that high-gamma delta movement accounts for a disproportionate share
of rebalancing demand, so state-dependent bands can shift the balance between robust HVA and hedge-error
risk. Future work could apply the same policy-comparison logic to other HVA settings, such as XVA hedging
costs, model-risk HVA, and callable products, path-dependent derivatives, or other structured instruments.

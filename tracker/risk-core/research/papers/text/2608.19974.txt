Abstract
LLM agents in finance must translate regula-
tory instructions into valid orders and evidence-
based surveillance judgments. We introduce
REGUSIM, a controlled financial-compliance
environment, and REGUBENCH, a benchmark
covering five market-manipulation patterns.
Together they separate stated reasoning, at-
tempted action, execution enforcement, and
monitor evidence. Experiments with four cur-
rent model configurations reveal complemen-
tary compliance weaknesses: traders submit
orders that breach executable constraints, while
monitors depend on how surveillance evidence
is presented. Removing role-revealing actor
names reduces detection F1 by 8–18 percent-
age points, and a supervised detector using the
same visible evidence outperforms the LLM
monitors. In a bridge study, adding trader ratio-
nales increases Claude Fable’s acceptance of
rejected orders; explicit checking reduces this
error. ReguSim makes these failures traceable
to actions, rules, and evidence, providing a re-
producible framework for evaluating financial-
compliance behavior beyond plausible explana-
tions.
1 Introduction
LLM agents are increasingly used to support trad-
ing decisions and financial surveillance. In these
settings, compliance requires more than recogniz-
ing regulatory language: an agent must connect a
rule to the order it submits, the portfolio it controls,
or the transaction records it reviews. A trader may
correctly mention a resale restriction yet sell re-
stricted shares; a monitor may repeat a convincing
compliance explanation while overlooking a price-
band breach. Evaluating these behaviors requires
a financial environment where the rule, the action,
and its consequences can be inspected together.

Financial compliance provides a particularly use-
ful setting for this question. Profit objectives and
risk-taking personas can conflict with prescribed
constraints, while prices, cash, holdings, and set-
tlement state make many order-level requirements
executable. Market surveillance adds a comple-
mentary challenge: recognizing suspicious relation-
ships across actors and transactions. Resale restric-
tions, price bands, and short-sale rules motivate
our trading constraints (Shanghai Stock Exchange,
2026; Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing Limited,
2026; U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission,
2010; Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing Lim-
ited, 2017); wash trading, spoofing, and related
patterns motivate the monitoring tasks. Our aim
is to evaluate how agents behave in a controlled
financial-compliance simulation.
We introduce REGUSIM, a trading environment
with deterministic rule checks, and REGUBENCH,
an independently generated surveillance bench-
mark. ReguSim records a trader’s explanation and
submitted order before the engine decides whether
to execute it. ReguBench asks a monitor to classify
a marked record within a local evidence window. A
bridge study connects the two roles by asking an in-
dependent monitor to audit actual ReguSim orders,
with or without the trader’s rationale, a checking in-
struction, or the engine’s decision. These interfaces
separate four artifacts:stated reasoning, attempted
action, execution enforcement, and monitor evi-
dence(Figure 1).
A rejected order exposes an inadmissible action
even though the execution gate prevents it. Surveil-
lance, in turn, tests how a monitor uses evidence
about a marked transaction. Executable rules and
generator predicates define these simulated finan-
cial tasks; the measurements concern agent behav-
ior within them, rather than real-market legal certi-
fication.

2 Related Work
Financial agents and behavioral simulation.Fi-
nancial language models support domain text and
knowledge tasks (Liu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023),
while trading frameworks study portfolio decisions
and specialized agents (Liu et al., 2021; Ding
et al., 2024, 2026; Xiao et al., 2025; Zhang et al.,
2024; Lopez-Lira, 2025). Simulators also exam-
ine market interaction and behavioral consistency
(Hashimoto et al., 2025; Papadakis et al., 2025;
Yang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Our depen-
dent variables are attempted constraint violations
and evidence-conditioned judgments. Exogenous
prices and fixed rules make the financial setting a
controlled task environment, rather than an attempt
to reproduce market dynamics.
Instruction conflict and behavioral influence.
IHEval tests conflicts across instruction priori-
ties (Zhang et al., 2025); AgentHarm evaluates
harmful multi-step agent tasks and attack effective-
ness (Andriushchenko et al., 2025). SycEval tests
answer changes under user rebuttals (Fanous et al.,
2025). These are adjacent behavioral questions, but
ReguSim uses fixed user-level objectives and an in-
dependent stateful ledger to distinguish a submitted
action from what a rule gate executes. Its contri-
bution is this experimentally inspectable setting
and its conditional behavioral findings, not a new
jailbreak attack or training algorithm. The bridge
measures the effect of adding another agent’s text;
it does not isolate sycophancy or persuasive style.
Replayable-agent and benchmark-auditing work
motivates preserving intermediate actions and
provenance (Khatchadourian, 2026; Wang et al.,
2026b,a; Ojewale et al., 2026). ReguSim spe-
cializes that principle to executable financial con-
straints. Legal reasoning benchmarks instead study
statutory questions and document judgments (Guha
et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Chalkidis et al., 2022;
Hendrycks et al., 2021).
Surveillance and evidence use.Financial
surveillance methods exploit transaction graphs,
temporal patterns, and order lifecycles (Nam
and Skillicorn, 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Losavio
et al., 2026; Tuccella et al., 2021; Wang and
Wellman, 2017). Strategic manipulation and
detection motivate adversarial formulations (Shi
et al., 2025); adjacent tasks include federated fraud
detection (Obiefuna et al., 2025) and financial
question answering (Choi et al., 2025). ReguBench
uses named surveillance patterns as operational
constructs to test how an LLM interprets records.
Matched targets and visible-window baselines
distinguish evidence access from decision behavior,
while identity and prompt controls probe depen-
dence on task presentation. The bridge study then
tests whether another agent’s rationale changes
judgments of an executable rule violation.
3 Methodology
The framework connects financial trading and
surveillance through two data sources and three
evaluation tasks (Figure 1). ReguSim produces
model-generated orders and deterministic execu-
tion outcomes. ReguBench independently gener-
ates surveillance records with programmatic labels;
these are not trader outputs. Only the bridge task
sends recorded trader actions to another model.

3.3 ReguBench Monitoring Task
The current confirmation panel contains 900 tar-
gets from 225 synthetic scenarios: five pattern cate-
gories, three difficulties, and three regime contexts
form 45 cells with ten positive and ten negative
targets each. Independent training data have the
same 900-target/225-scenario size; pilot data are
disjoint. The categories are wash trading (same-
actor buy/sell reversals), spoofing (large cancelled
orders), pump-and-dump (coordinated accumula-
tion and disposal), churning (repeated turnover),
and marking the close (trades near a constructed
period boundary). Seeded templates define actors,
record patterns, timing, noise, and binary labels.
Exact predicates are in Appendix C.2. The case-
source audit excluded all case-tagged templates
from this panel. Historical data (191 scenarios,
49,440 records; 800 evaluation targets) remain a
separate protocol in Appendix B.
A <TARGET> marker identifies one record within
up to four neighbors on each side. Timestamps,
actor IDs, side, quantity, price, and available order
status are visible; labels are withheld. The actor-
identity control replaces role-revealing names with
random neutral aliases while preserving actor rela-
tions and transactions. The evidence-representation
comparison adds 17 summaries computed from the
same neutral-ID window, testing whether explicit
features help interpret unchanged evidence. Mon-
itor prompt variants change wording and section
order while keeping the window and output schema
fixed. No hidden global index or synthetic period
is supplied.
Table 2 illustrates the task: the monitor must
connect an order’s lifecycle or an actor’s turnover
to the specific marked record, not simply flag every
record in a suspicious window.
Generator predicates supply reproducible inter-
nal targets. The monitor receives verbal mechanism
descriptions, not every latent index/noise predicate.
For example, marking-the-close membership de-
pends on position within a synthetic period whose
boundary is not supplied. Accordingly, F1 mea-
sures agreement with generator membership under
the provided evidence and verbal descriptions, not
execution of a fully specified visible decision rule.
Errors can reflect hidden-state ambiguity or mis-
match between those descriptions and the template
predicates, as well as model behavior.

4 Experiments
We evaluate three financial-compliance capabilities:
submitting admissible orders, detecting market-
manipulation patterns, and independently auditing
a trader’s compliance claim. We evaluate GPT-6
Astra, Claude Fable, Kimi K3, and DeepSeek V4.1
Flash. All four participate in state-visibility, actor-
identity, evidence-representation, and bridge com-
parisons; additional trader repetitions and prompt
variants cover the first three. Of 53,596 planned
decision/target slots, 52,004 were attempted and
51,760 yielded valid outputs. Coverage includes
invalid responses and unattempted tails. Settings,
Kimi transport continuation, and historical results
are documented separately in Appendices A.1

4.2 Monitor Experiment: Financial
Surveillance
Task and measures.The new panel contains 900
marked targets from 225 scenarios, balanced within
45 type–difficulty–regime cells. The binary refer-
ence identifies whether the marked record belongs
to the generator’s manipulation pattern. Macro Cell
F1 averages the 45 cell F1 values; precision and
recall use the same equal-cell averaging, with zero
denominators assigned zero. Type rows average the
nine cells for each manipulation category, rather
than scoring the model’s predicted type string.
Our primary condition replaces role-revealing ac-
tor names with neutral identifiers while preserving
actor relations. This tests surveillance from the
records with semantic name cues removed.
Which financial patterns do models detect?Ta-
ble 5 shows substantial differences across manipu-
lation types. Claude Fable has the highest descrip-
tive LLM F1 on wash trading (73.4%), spoofing
(83.5%), and churning (80.0%). Kimi leads on
pump-and-dump (71.4%) and marking the close
(70.5%). Pump-and-dump is particularly difficult
for Astra, Flash, and Claude Fable (36.6–45.5%),
whereas all four achieve at least 74.9% on spoofing.
Table 2 illustrates cancellation evidence and actor
attribution, two distinct surveillance demands.
Kimi’s overall F1 is 70.9%, compared with
Claude Fable’s 70.0%, Flash’s 62.4%, and Astra’s
60.6%. Their operating profiles differ more than
the first two F1 scores suggest: Kimi has 91.7%
recall but 59.5% precision, while Claude Fable has
75.3% recall and 75.1% precision. Thus Kimi iden-
tifies more positive targets at the cost of more false
alerts; Claude Fable is more balanced on these mea-
sures.

4.3 Bridge Study: Auditing Trader
Explanations
The bridge audits 256 historical Pro trader traces
from 118 source sessions: 128 rejected orders,
balanced across four rejection families, and 128
accepted controls. The original 64 traces form
a nested subset. Four monitors receive C1:
state/rules/action; C2: C1 plus trader rationale; C3:
C2 plus explicit checking instructions; or C4: C2
plus engine decision and codes. Calls have no
trader conversation history. False acceptance (FA)
means accepting an engine-rejected order; false
rejection (FR) means rejecting an accepted control.
Claude Fable falsely accepts 6.25%, 14.84%,
and 1.56% of rejected orders in C1, C2, and C3
(8, 19, and 2 of 128), respectively: adding the
trader’s reasoning and compliance fields raises FA
by 8.59 pp, while explicit checking reduces it by
13.28 pp. Both paired 95% intervals exclude zero
(Table 23). The other monitors’ corresponding in-
tervals include or touch zero, so evidence for these

5 Discussion
Compliance is grounded in financial state.A
trading agent must bind rule text to price move-
ments, cash, holdings, and resale eligibility. The
rejected-order example shows why recognizing a
rule in language is insufficient: the order can con-
tradict a directly observable price-band limit even
when its explanation claims compliance. Astra has
fewer rejected attempts but more zero-submission
sessions, while Claude Fable executes more often;
both activity and rejection inform the comparison.
Surveillance depends on the financial pattern
and its evidence.Surveillance requires same-
actor trading sequences for wash trading, order life-
cycle evidence for spoofing, and trading sequences
for turnover or price-timing patterns. Lower scores
after actor-name replacement show why those re-
lations must be tested without role cues. Logistic
regression remains strong on the same window-
derived evidence, whereas adding explicit features
does not uniformly help LLMs. Comparisons
should report both visible information and train-
ing supervision.
A trader’s explanation needs an independent
check.The bridge fixes state and action while
changing the accompanying evidence. For Claude
Fable, added reasoning and compliance text in-
creases false acceptance; explicit checking reduces
it. This provides a model-specific mitigation, with-
out isolating persuasive style as the mechanism. Re-
vealing the engine outcome instead tests use of an
available audit result. Financial evaluations should
distinguish explanations, independent assessments,
and enforcement records.
6 Conclusion
ReguSim evaluates LLM agent rule grounding
in financial compliance through stateful trading,
surveillance, and independent trace auditing. Ex-
ecutable constraints and controlled evidence con-
nect explanations to orders, ledger outcomes, and
monitoring decisions. Experiments identify con-
trasting trader profiles, manipulation-specific de-
tection weaknesses, actor-identity dependence, and
a rationale-induced auditing failure mitigated by
explicit checking for Claude Fable. It exposes com-
pliance behavior beyond fluent explanations.

Limitations
Task coverage.ReguSim evaluates behavior
within specified financial-compliance simulations.
The current implementation covers a finite set of
financial constraints and five manipulation cate-
gories generated from fixed mechanism families.
Extending the rule surface, market interactions, and
manipulation mechanisms would broaden this cov-
erage. Reported performance is conditional on the
observation interfaces described in Section 3; the
benchmark does not certify real-market compliance
or infer intent from an invalid action.
Evaluation scope.The model panel, objectives,
personas, and prompt variants provide a bounded
set of behavioral probes. Trader profiles describe
completed sessions; coverage and support for
paired controls vary by configuration. The cor-
responding coverage, uncertainty, and sensitivity
results are reported with the experiments and in
Appendix A. These comparisons do not establish
invariance across all prompts or isolate every lin-
guistic mechanism behind the bridge effects. The
logistic baseline uses labeled training examples,
whereas LLMs are zero-shot (Section 4.2).
Service and reproducibility scope.Findings de-
scribe the requested API configurations on the
recorded service dates. Additional model families
and future service versions may behave differently.
The anonymous release supports offline reproduc-
tion from archived scientific records; exact model-
request settings, recovery details, and historical
record coverage are documented in the appendices.
Repeating live API calls need not reproduce the
archived outputs.
Ethical considerations
Experiments use synthetic records and author-
defined sandbox rules, without private market
records or human-subject data. Conflicting incen-
tives and stealth prompts are controlled behavioral
probes. Reported failures describe the specified
tasks, not actual misconduct by market participants;
the work does not provide a procedure for bypass-
ing a deployed financial control.

A Controlled Supplemental Protocol and
Results
This appendix records the additional financial-
compliance experiments. Their reference outcomes
are the execution engine’s decisions and the syn-
thetic monitor generator’s labels. They test ad-
herence to the specified sandbox constraints and
recognition of its manipulation motifs. Expert ad-
judication is not required to define these reference
outcomes. Internal validity instead requires correct
implementation, faithful state transitions, and an
explicit account of which label-relevant facts are
visible to the model. A rejected attempt is not an
executed breach: the external engine continues to
enforce the rule. Nor does a rejected attempt alone
establish an intentional or successful jailbreak.
A.1 Frozen Design and Model Requests
The original supplemental campaign ran on
September 29–30, 2026 (UTC), under a frozen
seven-stage protocol. It planned 53,596 deci-
sion/target slots in 21,406 jobs. A trader job is
a 30-step session; a monitor or bridge job contains
one target. Studies proceeded in the order listed
in Table 8, with model and condition order ran-
domized within paired blocks. Up to 16 jobs ran
concurrently, while steps within each trader ses-
sion remained sequential. Astra, Claude Fable and
Kimi participated in every study. Flash participated
in the state-visibility, actor-identity and evidence-
representation controls and the bridge audit; it did
not receive additional trader repetitions or prompt
variants. Historical Pro results are a separate proto-
col, not another condition in the new campaign.
All four configurations used the same
hosted gateway’s chat-completions interface
(zctotoken.com, path /v1/chat/completions).
Temperature and top_p were not explicitly
supplied in these requests; the historical shared
temperature setting does not describe the supple-
ment. The connect/read timeouts were 15/180
seconds for Astra, Claude Fable and Kimi, and
15/600 seconds for Flash. The gateway’s model
identifiers and parameter acceptance do not inde-
pendently attest its backend identity or effective
effort mapping. Flash’s larger output budget
followed independent engineering calibration of
truncation at 8,192 tokens. Calibration did not
select settings by task accuracy or effect direction,
and its answers do not enter these results.
Recorded resources.Table 10 reports all 52,787
retained HTTP attempts from the original cam-
paign and Kimi continuation, including retries and
invalid answers. Of these, 51,795 have both re-

partial usage and are excluded from token sums,
not from attempt counts. The totals exclude pi-
lots and calibration. Inference ran on remote APIs;
model parameter counts and provider accelerator re-
sources are not available from these request records.
Reported tokens are not a complete billing record,
and no cash cost or accelerator-hour estimate is
inferred.
Fixed inputs and pairing.Trader cells cross five
regimes, three incentives and two personas. The
state-visibility control uses two seeds per cell for
each observation condition. Additional trader repe-
titions add three new seeds only to the original con-

Astra, Claude Fable and Kimi. The trader prompt

the three additional seeds. Price paths, rule param-

The full-state condition adds the portfolio at ex-

A.4 Estimands and Uncertainty
For each complete trader session, let r be re-
jected submitted attempts divided by submitted
non-HOLD attempts, assigning r= 0 to a session
with no submitted order. Regulatory rejection ex-
cludes resource-only failures. Activity is executed
non-HOLD orders divided by valid decisions. No-
order frequency is the complete-session proportion
with zero submissions. Each reported aggregate
first averages sessions within a factorial cell, then
averages the 30 cells with equal weight. This pre-
vents missing sessions from silently changing the
intended condition weights. All reported trader
means are conditional on complete-session avail-
ability; partial sessions still contribute to output
coverage, not complete-session rates.

A missing cell precludes the fixed-cell point es-
timate; a singleton cell precludes its variance es-
timate. A zero total observed variance does not
supply a fabricated zero-width interval. The t
approximation does not establish calibrated cov-
erage with only two observations per cell. Un-
adjusted within-cell percentile-bootstrap quantiles
from 10,000 draws are retained only as a diagnos-
tic: at nc = 2 their variance is half the unbiased
stratified estimate.
Planned-panel identification bounds allow each
missing latent full-session rate to lie anywhere in
[0,1] , or differences to lie in [−1,1] , under the
fixed planned cell weights. They are not confidence
intervals and do not impute continued actions to ter-
minated sessions. Hoeffding sensitivity uses inde-
pendent bounded session outcomes; complete-case
concentration does not remove completion selec-
tion. Expanding the planned-panel identification
endpoints additionally assumes bounded latent out-
comes for every planned seed. These assumptions
are made explicit rather than treating unavailable
intervals as evidence of equivalence.
Monitor precision, recall and binary F1 are com-
puted within each of 45 cells and separately av-
eraged. Thus Macro Cell F1 is not the harmonic
mean of Macro P and Macro R. A zero denominator
is assigned zero. Conditions are compared on their
jointly valid targets using 10,000 paired scenario-
cluster bootstrap draws, stratified by cell; a sce-
nario’s targets stay together. All-valid condition
scores and matched-valid comparison scores can
differ because their denominators differ. Invalid-
answer F1 bounds assign each missing binary pre-
diction its most and least favorable label. Those
bounds are not confidence intervals.
Bridge false acceptance is measured among
engine-rejected traces; false rejection is measured
among engine-accepted controls. Contrast intervals
use 10,000 paired bootstrap draws over historical
source sessions. All intervals are pointwise and
unadjusted for multiplicity. A confidence inter-
val containing zero does not establish no effect or
equivalence.

B.1 Model Configurations
Historical configurations request temperature 0.0.
Current-model requests are listed separately in Ap-

B.2 Price Series for the Trader Experiment
Trader sessions contain 30 decision steps over ten
days. Each condition–session pair uses an indepen-
dently seeded stochastic background path with a
shared positive shock on day 4 and negative shock
on day 7. The confirmatory DeepSeek V4 Pro run
contains five sessions for each factorial condition,
yielding 150 sessions and 4,500 retained valid de-
cisions. The Gemini 3.5 Flash replication uses the
same reported protocol with two sessions for each
factorial condition, yielding 60 sessions and 1,800
retained valid decisions. Earlier exploratory runs
used a different labeling protocol and are not used
as confirmatory evidence.
C Historical Prompts and Label
Definitions
C.1 Trader Prompt
Trader calls are single-turn requests. No previ-
ous model messages are retained as conversation
history; instead, each prompt contains the current
market state, portfolio state, and applicable regime
rule text, while the execution engine maintains the
authoritative ledger. The conservative persona text
is “You are conservative. Prioritize capital preser-
vation.” The aggressive persona text is “You are
aggressive. Take calculated risks for high returns.”
The neutral objective is to maximize risk-adjusted
returns within all regulatory constraints; the profit
objective states that the only goal is maximum
profit and that the bonus depends entirely on P&L;
the stealth objective asks the trader to maximize
profit while avoiding detection.

E Historical Qualitative Examples
Table 45 reports representative cases selected from
the logged two-target-per-cell monitor subset. The
cases cover all five surveillance categories and in-
clude all-model successes, all-model false posi-
tives, model disagreements, and examples where
structured baselines avoid LLM false positives. Ob-
solete unmarked-prompt examples are excluded to
avoid presenting ambiguous target attribution as
evidence.
Table 44 summarizes the qualitative error pat-
terns we observed in these cases. The taxonomy
is deliberately evidence-centered: it distinguishes
failures to localize evidence to the marked target,
failures to use order-lifecycle fields, and failures
caused by coarse temporal context. These monitor-
side errors are separate from the trader-side gap be-
tween stated compliance reasoning and attempted
action.
The selected cases illustrate why target marking
and evidence representation matter. In these cases,
LLM monitors use nearby suspicious context as
evidence for the marked trade even when the target
itself is a non-manipulative noise trade. This is
visible in churning and pump-and-dump false posi-
tives, where the surrounding sequence contains the
right pattern but the target label is negative. Con-
versely, spoofing positives are easier because the
target-levelCANCELLED marker is directly visible.
These examples support the quantitative finding
that structured target-level features can outperform
prompt-only monitoring on this synthetic sample.

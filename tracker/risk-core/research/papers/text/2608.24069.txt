Abstract
LLM-based multi-agent trading systems are moving from research
prototypes to live deployments that control real assets. They ingest
news and social media from the open web and pass it between
agents, so a single corrupted signal can reach the final decision and
cause financial loss. We study two adversaries that can introduce
such signals without access to model internals: a low-barrierdata
adversarythat plants manipulated content in web sources, and a
compromised-agent adversarythat tampers with an agent’s prompt.
To our knowledge, this is the first systematic empirical study in
finance of how an adversarial signalentersa multi-agent trading
system and whether itsurvivesto alter the final trading decision.

Trader, and Risk Manager—with an attack matched to its inter-
face. Along the structural axis, we compare four communication
topologies, interpreted with a simple parameter-free heuristic, the

1 Introduction
Large language models [4, 10, 24] have been adapted into autonomous
agents for financial tasks [30, 32, 40], and recent work has shifted
towardmulti-agent trading systems[ 22, 31, 33, 41, 42], where spe-
cialized agents—analysts, researchers, traders, and risk managers—
collaborate through structured communication to produce trading
decisions. The inter-agent communication that makes these sys-
tems effective also exposes them: as illustrated in Figure 1, a single

direct in financial markets: these systems continuously ingest con-
tent from the open web—news sites and social platforms such as
Reddit—where anyone able to publish online can plant fabricated
news, rumors, misinformation or hidden instructions [11, 26, 37],
and inherent LLM biases (e.g., toward large-cap technology stocks)
further expose the system to attacks aligned with them [20].
These risks are not hypothetical. Autonomous trading agents
already control live wallets and execute irreversible transactions,
and deployed systems have suffered six-figure losses both from
an unguarded action [8] and from adversarial inputs that steered
an agent into transferring funds to an attacker [1, 28]. As agentic
trading moves from research prototypes to live deployment, inci-
dents of this kind are being reported with increasing frequency [13].
Adversarial manipulation of financial agents is therefore no longer
a speculative concern but a pressing one. Yet despite this urgency,
how role-conditioned attack surfaces and communication architec-
tures affect the ability of adversarial inputs to alter final trading
decisions remains poorly understood.
To address this issue, we present a systematic empirical char-
acterization of adversarial failure modes in multi-agent trading
systems and distill the findings into practical considerations for
defense design. We organize the analysis along two complementary
axes. First, the adversarial signal mustenter: an attack targets a
particular role, and which attacks are viable depends on that role’s
task. Second, it mustsurvive: the compromised signal is filtered,
aggregated, revised, or voted upon before it becomes a decision,
and whether it still alters that decision depends on the communica-
tion topology. To our knowledge, this is the first finance-specific
empirical study to examine role-conditioned attack channels and
communication design in multi-agent trading systems.
Prior work differs from ours mainly in scope. One line of work
studies multi-agent architecture in general domains: Hagag et al.
[12] argue that role assignment, communication topology, and mem-
ory shape a system’s security surface, and NetSafe [38] shows em-
pirically that adversarial signals such as misinformation propagate
differently across communication topologies—yet neither ties at-
tacks to role-conditioned trading tasks. In finance, red-teaming
studies [7] and trust benchmarks [15] target single-agent settings,
and FinVault [ 36] addresses general financial tasks rather than
trading. AutoRedTrader [23] optimizes misinformation against a
single trading agent rather than characterizing how a corrupted
signal survives across roles and communication design, and Trade-
Trap [34] stress-tests a single fixed trading architecture through
infrastructure-level perturbations that presume internal access to
tool servers and state-reading interfaces.
In contrast, we analyze how adversarial signals affect multi-
agent trading systems across functional roles and communication
structures under two types of adversaries, neither of which has
access to model weights, tool servers, or orchestration state. A
low-barrierdata adversaryplants manipulated content in the web
sources agents consume and requires no access to the system itself,
whereas acompromised-agent adversarytampers with the prompt of
an agent inside the pipeline, modeling a compromised component
with strictly more access. Under these two adversaries, we examine
where an adversarial signal enters, whether it changes the final deci-
sion, and its expected financial impact. In the role-conditioned anal-
ysis, we decompose a widely-used trading pipeline [31] into four
functional roles—Analyst, Researcher, Trader, and Risk Manager—
and pair each with an attack matched to its interface. The data
adversary targets the Analysts through Data Poisoning and Indirect
Prompt Injection, while the compromised-agent adversary mounts
a Persuasive Adversary attack against the Researchers, Objective
Hijacking against the Trader, and Jailbreaking against the Risk
Manager. In the structural analysis, we evaluate four representative
topologies—linear, centralized, decentralized, and hybrid—under
both kinds of attack. To interpret the ordering we observe, we use
the Adversarial Signal Preservation Score (APS), a simple parameter-
free heuristic based on aggregation depth and voting, and examine
where it holds and where it breaks down. All experiments use post-
cutoff market data to avoid lookahead bias and span five assets and
two contrasting targets (BUY and SELL), with two backbone models
in the structural analysis, so that the patterns we report are not
tied to a single asset, target, or model. We find that no architecture
is inherently robust: majority voting protects only while compro-
mised agents remain below the quorum, a compromised terminal
decision node overrides all upstream deliberation, and decision-flip
rates do not track realized financial loss. These findings inform the
design of safer agentic trading systems that ingest untrusted web
content.

3 Task Formulation
We study a multi-agent LLM trading system that consumes mar-
ket prices, news articles, and social media posts and issues a daily
decision𝑠𝑡 ∈{buy,sell,hold} for a target instrument. LetA=
{𝐴1,...,𝐴 𝑛} be the agent set. The adversary seeks to drive the sys-
tem’s decision to a target𝑠∗∈{buy,sell} , either by replacing part
of the ingested data𝐷 with poisoned data𝐷′ or by manipulating
the prompt of a subset of agent Aadv ⊂A through adversarial
prompts𝑝 adv. Benign agents are unaware of the attack.
Threat model.We consider two adversaries, neither of which
has access to model weights, tool servers, orchestration state, or
reasoning traces. Thedata adversaryplants manipulated content in
the external web sources the system ingests, such as news articles
and social media posts, and requires no access to the system itself.
Thecompromised-agent adversarytampers with the prompt of an
agent inside the pipeline, modeling a compromised component or
insider with strictly more access.
Evaluation metric.We measure vulnerability with the Attack
Success Rate (ASR), the standard metric for targeted attacks:
ASR= #{𝑖:𝑑 clean𝑖 ≠𝑡𝑖, 𝑑atk𝑖 =𝑡𝑖}
#{𝑖:𝑑 clean𝑖 ≠𝑡𝑖} ,(1)
where𝑑clean𝑖 and𝑑atk𝑖 denote the system’s final decision on day 𝑖
under the clean and attacked conditions, and𝑡𝑖 is the adversary’s tar-
get. The denominator restricts attention toattackabledays, whose
clean decision differs from the target, so ASR is the fraction of
flippable decisions that the adversary steers to its target. We eval-
uate both BUY- and SELL-targeted attacks. Figure 2 provides an
overview of our framework. ASR measures survival end to end: a
signal survives if it changes the final decision to the target.
Multi-agent trading systems.Following the widely-used Tradin-
gAgents [31], we adopt four functional roles that constitute a multi-
agent trading system: (1) anAnalyst Teamof social media, news,
fundamental, and market analysts; (2) aResearch Teamconsisting of
a bullish and a bearish researcher who debate market conditions; (3)
Traderagents with differing risk profiles; and (4) aRisk Management
team enforcing exposure constraints. For the architecture-level anal-
ysis, we simplify the pipeline to three analyst agents—Market (M),
Social (S), and News (N)—while holding the analyst set and attack
entry point fixed, allowing us to examine the effects of communi-
cation and aggregation design separately from role specialization.

Adversarial attacks.Each role exposes a different attack sur-
face, defined by the interface through which it receives informa-
tion, so we pair each role with an attack matched to that interface.
The data adversary mounts twodata-levelattacks on the analysts,
which ingest web text. The compromised-agent adversary mounts
threeagent-levelattacks on the researcher, trader, and risk manager,
which act on upstream conclusions. Because the two adversaries
assume different access, ASR values across attacks characterize
scenario-specific failure modes rather than a ranking of intrinsic
role vulnerability.

Attack settings.For both data-level attacks, we inject adversarial
content at a 1:9 ratio. This corresponds to the10%setting in the
ratio sweep of Section 5. For the persuasive adversary, we use a

Backbones.The role-conditioned analysis fixes the backbone to

gpt-4.1-mini or Qwen3-30B-A3B. This assignment is fixed across
all architectures, with temperature set to0(Appendix B).

Each role is paired with one representative attack matched to its
interface. Table 1 reports ASR for five role-conditioned stress-test
scenarios under both target directions. Because the scenarios oper-
ate through different interfaces and attack mechanisms, the values
characterize scenario-specific failure rates rather than an intrinsic
cross-role vulnerability ranking. Pairwise two-proportion z-tests
(Fisher’s exact cross-checked; Appendix G) show Data poisoning,
Indirect injection, and Objective hijacking form a statistically over-

sive adversary is significantly higher than every data-level attack

Compromising the terminal decision role creates a high-impact
failure mode.The most pronounced failure mode in our stress
tests occurs when the Risk Manager is directly compromised: Jail-
breaking succeeds on98.9%(BUY) and95 .5%(SELL) of attackable
days, whereas the four remaining scenarios fall within 13.8–53.3%.
Because this scenario combines prompt-level access, an explicit
authority-override payload, and the role that also holds final deci-
sion authority, we do not attribute this rate to the Risk Manager role
itself. Rather, compromising the terminal decision node bypasses
all upstream aggregation, debate, and independent validation. The
result highlights the design risk of coupling safety enforcement
with final decision authority without an additional check.

6 Architecture-Level Analysis
We evaluate four architectures under two backbones across three
attacks, with data-level adversarial content at a10%ratio: twodata-
levelattacks, Data Poisoning (D1) and Indirect Prompt Injection
(D2), which corrupt the evidence an agent reasons over; and one
agent-levelattack, Objective Hijacking (A1), which leaves the rea-
soning intact but biases the objective the agent optimizes for, so it
rationalizes toward a predetermined outcome rather than reasoning
from the evidence.
To interpret the result, we compare the resulting ASR patterns
and use the Adversarial Signal Preservation Score (APS) as a post-
hoc analytical lens: APS serves as a qualitative structural heuristic.

6.2 Adversarial Signal Preservation Score (APS)
Conventional graph centrality measures [5, 9, 25, 27] characterize a
node’s position but not how signals aretransformedin transit. Con-
ventional attack-tolerance results likewise depend onwhichnode an
adversary targets—scale-free networks resist random failure but col-
lapse under targeted attacks on high-centrality hubs [2]—whereas
in our setting the attacked node is fixed (an analyst) and vulner-
ability depends primarily onhowthe decision node aggregates
incoming signals (averaging or voting). This does not mean central-
ity is useless: concurrent work leverages it to prioritize which nodes
to defend [29]; centrality captureswhereinfluence concentrates,
while aggregation determineshow muchadversarial signal survives.
Consequently, these measures do not reproduce the vulnerability
ordering we observe: multi-agent communication averages, revises,
and votes, and these operations transform an adversarial signal to
different degrees.
We summarize this ordering with the Adversarial Signal Preser-
vation Score (APS), a qualitative structural heuristic introducedpost
hoc. APS rests on two simplifying assumptions. First, each addi-
tional aggregation stage is assumed to weaken rather than reinforce
a single adversarial report, with its residual weight approximated
by1/(1+𝑘) . This need not hold for LLM aggregation: a down-
stream agent that trusts and restates a corrupted report can amplify
it. Second, a majority vote is assumed to discard a minority adver-
sarial signal. This holds only when the benign agents agree on a
non-target decision; when they split, a single adversarial vote can
complete a majority. Under these assumptions, we define the APS
as follows:

8 Conclusion
This work presents the first systematic finance-specific study of
this attack chain, examining both where adversarial signals enter
through role-conditioned interfaces and whether they alter the final
decision across communication architectures. Across five assets,
two backbones, and BUY- and SELL-targeted attacks, we find that
no architecture is inherently robust and that multi-agent design
alone is insufficient as a defense. Robustness depends on where
validation, aggregation, and majority thresholds are placed, while
APS provides only a first-order lens on this structural vulnerability.
These findings provide practical insights for designing safer agentic
trading systems: security must come from deliberate validation and
aggregation, not from agent multiplicity alone.
Limitations
Our study has some limitations. First, due to cost, the role-based
evaluation focuses on a single backbone. Second, five large-cap
assets with dense information coverage may not represent thinner-
coverage securities. Third, the daily closing-price backtest omits
execution timing, transaction costs, slippage, and detailed position

management. These simplifications may affect absolute loss esti-
mates, especially for attacks that induce frequent trading, but still
support comparisons under a common protocol. Future work should
validate the findings across additional proprietary and open-weight
models and in execution-aware settings.
Ethics and Privacy Statement
This work analyzes vulnerabilities in LLM-based trading systems
through sandboxed backtests with no live capital or market inter-
action. All manipulated content was synthetically generated and
confined to the experimental environment. We disclose attack mech-
anisms only as needed to reproduce the findings and discuss corre-
sponding mitigations. Source-reputation and provenance checks
may help agents distinguish credible from corrupted information,
while stronger provider- and user-defined guardrails and periodic
audits of prompts, outputs, and logs may reduce prompt-injection
risk. Our goal is to support role-conditioned, architecture-aware
defenses before deployment in financially consequential settings.

F Repeatability Study
We re-ran the 31 GPT-4.1 cells three times each with identical con-
figurations: 7 role-conditioned cells (Data Poisoning and Indirect
Injection, each BUY- and SELL-targeted; Persuasive Adversary; Ob-
jective Hijacking; and Jailbreaking) and the 24 architecture cells.
All runs use the same 50 asset-days (10 trading dates× 5 assets),
chosen by a fixed rule that depends on clean behavior only: among
dates with complete results for all five assets, we stratified by the
clean BUY/SELL balance (BUY-dominant, SELL-dominant, mixed)
and took evenly spaced quantiles of the balance score within each
stratum. ASR is computed on the asset-days attackable under the
original clean run, which fixes each cell’s denominator across repe-
titions (27–50 asset-days per cell, so one asset-day moves an ASR
by 2–3.7 pp). The remaining sources of variation are model non-
determinism and, for the data-level attacks, the unseeded sampling
of poisoned items.
Rank correlations between repetitions are 0.93–0.96 (Spearman),
the mean absolute difference is 4.2 pp, and 90% of repetition pairs
differ by at most 11 pp. Over the 30 cells below a 95% ceiling, the
pooled repetition standard deviation is about 4.5 pp: 6.9 pp for role-
conditioned cells and 3.6 pp for architecture cells. Six cells exceed
5 pp: three role-conditioned cells (Data Poisoning-SELL, Indirect
Injection-SELL, and Objective Hijacking) and the BUY-targeted
A1, D1, and D2 cells of the Centralized architecture. Jailbreaking
ranks first among role-conditioned cells in every repetition. Two
limits apply: the study covers a single 50-asset-day subset, so it
measures repetition noise rather than variability across dates, and
with three repetitions the standard deviation of any single cell is
itself uncertain, so we draw conclusions about the set of cells rather
than individual ones.

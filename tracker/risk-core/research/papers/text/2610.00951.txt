Negative Oil & Nickel Squeeze: A Feedback Model for Extreme
Commodity Futures Prices

Abstract
On April 20, 2020, the May front-month WTI oil futures contract, one day before its expira-
tion date, opened near $17/barrel and dropped far below zero in a single trading day, reaching
an intraday low of−$40.32 and settling at−$37.63. Such market behavior was unforeseen at
the time. This event, and the March 2022 nickel squeeze, illustrate how constraints on physical
delivery create pressure to close futures positions and distort futures prices. We develop a feed-
back model that connects the resulting price distortion to a roll option, and to the imbalance
between delivery-constrained long and short positions. Under lognormal benchmark dynamics,
the roll-option value is shown to satisfy a pricing PDE which is nonlinear because its payoff
depends on the observed futures price, which itself includes the feedback correction. Remark-
ably, in both cases, there is an explicit solution in terms of classical Black-Scholes-Margrabe
exchange option formulas, up to solving a scalar equation. We show this allows that prices may
go negative under distortion of a positive-price lognormal model. An analogous construction
applies when the lognormal base is switched to Bachelier normal dynamics. Illustrations based
on WTI and nickel show the imbalance required to reproduce the extreme event prices under
the benchmark assumptions.
1 Introduction
Commodity futures link financial trading to physical delivery. Storage, convenience yield and the
term structure of supply and demand are central to their valuation; see, for example, Bouchouev
(2023). Participation by financial investors and the interaction between trading and fundamentals
add further channels through which prices can move (Tang and Xiong, 2012; Cheng and Xiong,
2014). During periods of market stress, however, the ability to hold a contract through delivery can
differ sharply across participants. Traders who must close their positions may then face a limited
set of counterparties able and willing to absorb them.
Two episodes motivate this paper. On April 20, 2020, the May WTI futures contract settled at
−$37.63/bbl on its penultimate trading day, amid a collapse in oil demand and severe constraints
on storage and delivery (Commodity Futures Trading Commission, 2020). On March 8, 2022,
the LME three-month nickel price reached $101,365/ton during a short squeeze, before trading
was suspended and that day’s trades were canceled (Oliver Wyman, 2023). Both raise a related
question: how can pressure to exit positions amplify a futures-price movement when access to
physical delivery is limited?

We address this question through a model that separates traders by their license (or not) to take
delivery. An imbalance among unlicensed positions creates one-sided demand to close-out: excess
longs must sell, and excess shorts must buy. The cost of this constraint is represented by a roll op-
tion. Since the option payoff depends on the observed futures price, and the observed price includes
the option-value correction, the pricing equation contains feedback, and is thereby nonlinear.
Our model is explanatory. It takes a benchmark price as an input, and builds an additional mecha-
nism through which constrained positions can move the observed price away from that benchmark.
Its main advantage is analytical tractability: despite the nonlinear pricing PDE, the specifications
studied here admit exact solutions in terms of standard option-pricing formulas. These represen-
tations make the direction and magnitude of feedback transparent, and allow the implications of
benchmark and imbalance assumptions to be quantified.
1.1 Contract structure and motivating events
The two episodes differ in their contract structure and delivery constraints, but both feature a class
of market participants that cannot remain in the futures contract through physical settlement. We
first describe the institutional features of the WTI and LME nickel markets and the circumstances
surrounding the April 2020 oil’s price collapse and the March 2022 nickel squeeze. We then show how
these distinct settings give rise to the common mechanism studied in the paper: forced liquidation
by delivery constrained traders and the resulting pressure on observed futures prices. Additional
contract specifications and delivery details for the two markets are summarized in Appendix A.
1.1.1 NYMEX WTI crude oil and the April 2020 collapse
The NYMEX West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil futures contract is physically settled by
delivering 1,000 barrels of WTI-grade crude at Cushing, Oklahoma. Trading ends shortly before the
delivery month according to the schedule specified by the exchange (CME Group, n.d.). Physical
settlement requires access to storage, pipeline capacity, and the operational arrangements necessary
to receive or deliver crude at Cushing.
Many financial participants do not possess this infrastructure and therefore close or roll their front-
month positions before the delivery process begins. Producers, refiners, merchants, and other
commercial participants may have the arrangements needed to remain in the contract through
settlement, although commercial status alone does not guarantee delivery capacity. Throughout
the paper, we refer to these groups as unlicensed and licensed traders, respectively, where the
terminology describes the delivery capability within the model rather than an actual regulatory
classification.
The May 2020 WTI contract provides a striking example of a forced long liquidation, which we
call Case 1. On April 20, 2020, its penultimate trading day, the contract initially traded at around
$17/bbl and ended the day at−$37.63/bbl. A series of events over the previous weeks had driven
down the price of physical barrels and amplified the downward pressure on the futures contract.
The COVID-19 pandemic sharply reduced oil demand, while the breakdown of production negoti-
ations between Russia and Saudi Arabia added to supply pressures (Commodity Futures Trading
Commission, 2020). Although a replacement agreement was announced on April 12, it did not take
effect until May 1.
Meanwhile, Cushing inventories reached 59.7 million barrels on April 17, up from 55.0 million barrels
one week earlier (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2020). Open interest in the expiring
2
contract was 108,593 lots, corresponding to 108.6 million barrels (Commodity Futures Trading
Commission, 2020). These conditions made physical delivery particularly unattractive and, for
many financial participants, operationally infeasible. The concentration of the price collapse in the
final hours is consistent with liquidation pressure, alongside the deterioration in physical-market
conditions.
The open-interest data provide additional context, but do not identify which positions were delivery-
constrained. Open interest fell from 108,593 to 13,044 contracts during April 20, implying that
approximately 88% of outstanding positions were closed during the day. Relative to its April 2
peak of 634,727 contracts, residual open interest on the penultimate trading day was only 2.06%,
the smallest among the 198 WTI front-month expiration cycles from 2010 to 2024, compared with a
median of 5.55%. We calculate these statistics using contract-level open-interest data obtained from
Bloomberg. These figures document extensive position closure. They are consistent with a large
price concession during liquidation, but aggregate open interest alone cannot establish the size or
ownership of the constrained imbalance. This distinction motivates the mixing weightεintroduced
in Section 2.1: it measures the intensity of the delivery-constrained positional imbalance relative
to total open interest, rather than the fraction of positions remaining after liquidation.
A particularly important source of forced selling is documented by Bouchouev (2023,§3.5). The
Bank of China had offered an OTC oil-linked product, YuanYouBao, to domestic retail investors
and hedged its exposure through long NYMEX WTI futures positions. Those positions had to be
liquidated on April 20 through the Trading-at-Settlement (TAS) mechanism. As selling pressure
intensified, TAS liquidity became insufficient even at the minimum permitted value, and the re-
maining orders had to be executed in the regular order book during the final part of the session
(Bouchouev, 2020). In the terminology of our model, this is a Case 1 event, in which unlicensed
traders are net long and must sell into a limited pool of licensed traders.
1.1.2 LME three-month nickel and the March 2022 squeeze
The March 2022 LME nickel squeeze provides the mirror-image Case 2 event. The LME nickel
contract differs from WTI in several important respects. The LME offers daily prompt dates out to
three months and quotes a rolling three-month tenor; an individual position nevertheless has a fixed
delivery date. Physical delivery takes place through LME-approved warehouses and requires eligible
Class I refined nickel. Much of global nickel production, however, consists of Class II material that
is not deliverable against the LME contract. Consequently, even a physical nickel producer may be
unable to satisfy an LME short position using its own production (Oliver Wyman, 2023).
This distinction became critical during March 7–8, 2022. Concerns about the availability of de-
liverable nickel intensified following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, contributing to reduced liquidity
ahead of the squeeze (Oliver Wyman, 2023). Warranted nickel stocks in LME warehouses had
declined from 173,000 tonnes on September 15, 2021, to approximately 80,000 tonnes by February
24, 2022 (Oliver Wyman, 2023, p. 15). Against this backdrop, the nickel futures price rose sharply,
reaching $101,365/ton on March 8 before the LME suspended trading and subsequently canceled
that day’s transactions. The episode led to an FCA enforcement investigation (Financial Conduct
Authority, 2023) and litigation concerning the cancellation of trades (High Court of Justice, 2023).
Large short positions and rising margin requirements contributed to the squeeze. Some producers
used LME contracts to hedge Class II nickel, which was not deliverable against those contracts. As
prices rose, participants reduced their short exposures through purchases, adding further upward
pressure in a market with limited liquidity (Oliver Wyman, 2023). This mechanism illustrates
3
Case 2: delivery constrained shorts face pressure to buy back their positions.
1.1.3 A common feedback mechanism
The episodes motivate two directions of the same feedback mechanism. In Case 1, constrained
traders are net long and closing their positions puts downward pressure on the observed price. In
Case 2, they are net short and covering puts upward pressure on the price. For WTI, the relevant
constraint is access to storage and delivery arrangements at Cushing. For nickel, it includes the
mismatch between a producer’s output and the exchange’s deliverable grade. Section 2.1 formalizes
the position imbalance.
Our approach is related to models in which trading changes the price dynamics faced by the traders
themselves. Frey and Stremme (1997) examine how the implementation of dynamic hedging strate-
gies changes the underlying asset’s price dynamics and volatility. Sircar and Papanicolaou (1998)
account for the effect of dynamic hedging on the underlying asset price and, in turn, on derivative
values. Their construction leads to a nonlinear pricing PDE and explains how hedging demand
can increase volatility. In commodity markets, Chan, Sircar, and Stein (2015) introduce portfolio-
optimizing investors alongside traditional commodity demand. Their feedback model studies how
investors’ positions affect commodity prices and their correlation with financial markets. Basak
and Pavlova (2016) develop an equilibrium model with institutional investors whose performance
is measured relative to a commodity index. They show how financialization affects futures prices,
volatilities and correlations, and transmits shocks to spot prices and inventories when storage is
included. Sockin and Xiong (2015) examine an informational feedback channel in which commodity
prices influence producers’ decisions by conveying information about economic conditions, thereby
affecting commodity demand. More directly related to the WTI episode, Ma and Wang (2021)
develop a three-player game-theoretic model in which strategic interactions can generate negative
oil prices.
The common feature is that financial trading enters price formation. Our model focuses on a
specific source of this interaction: the need to close positions when physical delivery is unavailable.
Feedback acts through a roll-option value, which depends on the same observed price that it helps
determine. The benchmark is supplied externally, and the imbalance is held fixed within each
valuation. The model therefore complements the broader equilibrium and trading mechanisms
above. Its main analytical contribution is an exact reduction of this particular nonlinear pricing
problem to standard option formulas, coupled with a scalar equation.
1.2 Contributions
The paper makes three related contributions. First, it links market segmentation to a pricing
distortion. A normalized imbalance of unlicensed long and short positions determines the weight
ε, while a roll option measures the cost associated with closing the excess position. The resulting
feedback relation accommodates both downward and upward distortions.
Second, we derive and solve the associated nonlinear pricing problem. Under lognormal benchmark
dynamics, homogeneity reduces the PDE to one state variable, and a change of variables converts
it to the classical Margrabe equation. The resulting exact representation expresses the solution
through a standard put or call Black-Scholes pricing formula. Under normal dynamics, an analogous
reduction gives Bachelier representations.
Third, we use the representation to characterize the implications of the feedback mechanism and
4
to construct event illustrations. In particular, we show how the distortion can drive an observed
oil futures price below zero even when the underlying licensed price remains positive, and we
identify the role of the position imbalance and calendar-spread dynamics in determining when such
a crossing can occur. We then use the WTI and nickel episodes to illustrate the magnitude and
direction of the resulting price distortions.
2 Feedback Model
As discussed earlier, commodity futures markets differ fundamentally from equity markets because
they include distinct classes of participants with different objectives and delivery capabilities. Pro-
ducers, merchants, and commodity trading firms primarily use futures contracts to hedge the price
risk of physical inventories or future production. By contrast, financial participants trade futures
for speculation, portfolio diversification, or financial hedging.
To capture this segmentation, we distinguish between two types of traders, based on the delivery
rules of major commodity exchanges such as the CME.
1.Licensed traders. These are producers, merchants, and commodity trading firms that are
authorized to make or take physical delivery at contract maturity.
2.Unlicensed traders. These include hedge funds and other financial participants who trade
commodity futures for speculative or hedging purposes but are not authorized to participate
in physical delivery.
The key distinction is that an unlicensed trader must close its position before contract maturity,
whereas a licensed trader may hold the contract until expiration and either make or take physical
delivery. This reflects the market structure observed in both canonical events, where speculative
participants were required to liquidate their positions before maturity. Maintaining an open position
beyond that point without delivery eligibility is generally prohibited by exchange rules and may
result in financial penalties or trading restrictions.
We introduce this market segmentation to describe the observed futures price, defined asF obst,T .
LetF lict,T denote the licensed price, namely the price that would prevail if all market participants
were licensed and no forced liquidation occurred. The difference between the two arises from the
presence of the unlicensed traders and is captured by a third quantity, the distorted futures price
Ht,T,T 2. This is the price at which the unlicensed component of the market clears.
Throughout the paper,F t,T2 denotes the observed price of a later-month futures contract, where
T2 > T. Since this contract matures well after the front-month contract, it is not subject to the
same liquidation pressure and is therefore treated as undistorted. In the WTI oil application, this
is the next-month contract.
The following sections derive the relationship betweenF obst,T ,F lict,T ,H t,T,T 2, andF t,T2 from a market-
clearing argument based on the open-interest composition of the futures market.

2.2 Fundamental Futures Pricing
The framework we develop in this paper is built on top of any standard commodity-pricing model
for the licensed futures priceF lict,T in equation (6). The rest of the equation provides a structural
mechanism that maps this fundamental price into the observed futures price.
Several standard frameworks can supplyF lic to our model. These include classical cost-of-carry
models with storage costs and convenience yields, lognormal dynamics commonly used for futures
prices, and the models of Schwartz (1997), which incorporate mean reversion in commodity prices
and, in their multi-factor specifications, stochastic convenience yields and interest rates. These
models, together with a broader overview of the commodity-pricing literature, are presented in
Bouchouev (2023).
These classical frameworks produce a fundamental futures price that is, by construction, positive
and consistent with the absence of arbitrage. The mechanism introduced in the following subsection
adds a structural correction that generates the extreme price distortions observed in practice due
to exit pressure. The main structural component of our framework is therefore the mappingF lic 7→
F obs through the recursive feedback equation derived below in Section 2.3.
2.3 Speculative Futures Pricing
The distorted futures price follows from a market-clearing argument. We consider separately the
two possible imbalances in unlicensed positions.
In Case 1, unlicensed traders hold an excess long position that must be closed before maturity.
These traders are therefore forced to sell their contracts, while only licensed traders can take the
opposite side of the transaction. This creates one-sided selling pressure and can push the observed
futures price below its fundamental value. The cost of this restriction can be represented through
a roll option. A licensed trader can hold the front-month futures contract through maturity and
take delivery of the commodity. An unlicensed trader cannot. To maintain the same exposure
beyond maturity, the unlicensed trader must instead close the front-month position and replace it
with a position in the next-month futures contract. We model this transaction as the long-side
roll option,R (+). The value of this option measures the cost associated with being unable to
take delivery and being forced to close the front-month position. We therefore interpretR (+) as
the close-out premium borne by an unlicensed long trader. The corresponding distorted price is
Ht,T,T 2 =F lict,T −R (+)t .
In Case 2, the direction of the imbalance is reversed. Unlicensed traders hold an excess short
position that must be closed before maturity. They are therefore forced to buy the front-month
7
contract to cover their positions. This one-sided buying pressure can push the observed futures
price above its fundamental value. The corresponding restriction is represented by the short-side
roll optionR (−). Its value measures the additional cost faced by an unlicensed short trader who
must buy back the front-month contract rather than settle through delivery. We interpretR (−) as
the squeeze premium generated by forced short covering:H t,T,T 2 =F lict,T +R (−)t .
We now define these two roll options more precisely. Both are non-negative option payoffs written
on the spread between the front-month and a later-month contract. Which option is relevant is
determined by the open-interest imbalance:R (+) applies when unlicensed traders are net long,
whileR (−) applies when they are net short.

represent its value at timeTusing the discounted value of the next-month futures contract. Let

5 Conclusion
This paper develops an explanatory model of how delivery constraints can amplify commodity
futures price movements. Separating participants by their ability to participate in physical deliv-
ery links the direction of the price distortion to the imbalance of constrained positions. Excess
constrained longs generate downward pressure, while excess constrained shorts generate upward
pressure. A roll option connects this pressure to the observed price, producing a nonlinear feed-
back relation.
The main analytical contribution is an exact representation of the resulting pricing solution. Under
lognormal benchmark dynamics, dimension reduction and a change of variables connect the non-
linear PDE to the Margrabe exchange option formula. At a given observed price state, valuation
requires one scalar root. The normal dynamics extension provides an analogous Bachelier represen-
tation. These results make the feedback mechanism accessible without numerically solving the PDE
and allow its effects on option values, admissible prices and effective variance to be studied directly.
In particular, the lognormal benchmark can remain positive while the observed price crosses zero.
The WTI and nickel illustrations show how the mechanism can generate large distortions in either
direction. Their role is to relate the model to recognizable market events and to quantify the imbal-
ance required under stated assumptions. They do not establish that feedback alone caused either
event, or that the implied imbalance equals the actual historical position imbalance. Benchmark
prices, the later-maturity futures price and the relevant horizon all matter. The model comple-

Several extensions follow naturally. The first is a nonconstant imbalance. A deterministic time-
dependentε(t) could represent a changing concentration of positions as a closure date approaches. A
stochasticε t could respond to prices, margin calls, liquidity and changes in participants’ positions.
Such extensions require a new dynamic pricing derivation: differentiating the feedback relation
introduces terms involving changes inε t and, in the stochastic case, covariation terms. Substituting
a time-varying estimate into the constant-εformula is therefore not, by itself, a solution of the
extended model. It would be useful to determine which reductions remain available and how
stochastic imbalance changes the probability and persistence of extreme prices.
A second direction is empirical. Applying the framework to a broader set of historical squeezes,
liquidation episodes and less stressed contract expiries would help assess when this mechanism is
informative and when it is insufficient. This would require synchronized prices across maturities,
measures of liquidity and delivery conditions, and sensitivity analysis for the fundamental bench-
mark. Comparisons across events could then examine whether the model’s implications extend
beyond the two motivating examples.
The most important data requirement is contract-specific information on positions and delivery
capability. Aggregate open interest or broad commercial and noncommercial classifications do not
identify the licensed and unlicensed groups used here. Positions would need to be linked, where
possible, to access to eligible inventory, storage and delivery arrangements. Such data would allow
the imbalance to be measured independently of the price inversion, turning the present explanatory

illustrations into tests of the proposed feedback mechanism.

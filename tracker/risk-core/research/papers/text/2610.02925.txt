ABSTRACT
Safety monitors help safeguard language-model agents interacting with external
tools and environments, but conservative monitoring can generate many false
alarms, consuming extensive review resources and weakening trust in alerts. Be-
cause false and genuine alarms often remain interleaved in native monitor scores,
obtaining a reliable cutoff still requires substantial manual verification. In prac-
tice, a small set of verified-safe non-alarmed trajectories may be available while
alarms remain unlabeled, naturally casting false-alarm auditing as a positive–
unlabeled (PU) ranking problem. The key challenge ismonitor-induced selection,
since observed safe references are accepted by the monitor, while the hidden safe
alarms of interest are precisely those it incorrectly flags, making the observed
positives poorly representative of the positives to be recovered. To address this
challenge, we propose a two-stage framework in whichTrust-aware PU Super-
visionadapts safe references toward the alarm domain and protects plausible
false alarms from excessive negative pressure, whileReliability-gated Rank Dis-
tillationconsolidates consistent ordering preferences from multiple PU reference
models into a single student.Consensus-guided Structural Refinementthen im-
proves the student ranking using hierarchical safe-reference support, alarm re-
lations, and predicted reference consensus. The framework requires no alarm
safety labels for fitting and leaves the underlying monitor unchanged. Across
mainstream safety monitors, our method achieves a macro AUPRC of 0.6444,
outperforming eight evaluated PU baselines by 5.27–16.98 absolute percentage
points; compared with PULDA, the strongest evaluated PU baseline, it recovers
33.3% more false alarms at a 5% review budget. Code is available at https:
//anonymous.4open.science/r/safe-alarm-ranking-c4d2/.
1 INTRODUCTION
Language-model agents increasingly perform real-world tasks through multi-round interactions with
external tools and environments. During execution, they may be exposed to prompt injection attacks
or take actions that violate safety constraints, potentially causing serious harm (Debenedetti et al.,
2024; Wang et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2026; Jiao et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026). An agent’s execution
trajectory records its intermediate actions, observations, and tool interactions, providing a natural
basis for safety monitoring mechanisms that detect unsafe behavior (Liu et al., 2026; Xiang et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2025). Existing trajectory monitors assess such execution traces using learned
evaluators or explicit safety criteria and produce safety scores, risk judgments, or intervention signals
when potentially unsafe behavior is detected. These approaches have demonstrated effective detection
of unsafe agent behavior across a range of settings, including coding, web interaction, and tool-use
tasks (Liu et al., 2026; Xiang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a).

However, this seemingly strong performance in unsafe-behavior detection comes with a substantial
cost. Agent trajectories vary considerably in structure and format across tasks and datasets, while the
safety of an individual action often depends on both the task context and the preceding execution
history (Yuan et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026). To reduce the risk of overlooking unsafe behavior
across such heterogeneous settings, safety monitors often favor sensitivity, for example by adopting
permissive alarm thresholds or overly inclusive safety criteria that also flag borderline cases. Our
empirical study reveals that this design choice results in a substantial number of false alarms: across
mainstream safety monitors, 39.4%–57.9% of triggered alarms actually correspond to safe trajectories
and should therefore be regarded as false positives (FPs) (Figure 1a). Such a high false-positive rate
is consequential. Repeated false warnings can erode users’ trust in the monitoring system and reduce
their willingness to respond to subsequent alerts (Chancey et al., 2017). Moreover, each false alarm
must be investigated before it can be dismissed, incurring additional human effort and computational
overhead (Alahmadi et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024; Oliver et al., 2024).
A natural way to prioritize likely false alarms for review is to reuse the monitor’s original safety
score, possibly with an additional cutoff. Our empirical study shows that false and true alarms
often receive similar scores and remain mixed in the native ordering (Figure 1b). With this overlap,
moving the cutoff cannot reliably separate the two groups. Even if stronger future monitors produced
better-separated scores, choosing a cutoff that preserves genuine alarms would require manually
verifying many cases. That verification is impractical under a limited review budget.
Let us reconsider the alarm-isolation problem from a different perspective. In practice, it is often
easy to obtain a small set of verified-safe trajectories, either from routine benign traces that do not
trigger alarms or from the monitor’s training data. Because safety monitors are typically conservative,
trajectories that they confidently classify as safe provide reliable positive examples. Treating safety
as the class of interest, these verified-safe trajectories form the observed positive set, while false
alarms hidden within the alarm pool constitute latent positives. The task therefore becomes one of
identifying positive-class samples from an otherwise unlabeled alarm pool, naturally giving rise to a
positive-unlabeled (PU) learning formulation (Elkan & Noto, 2008). However, this setting differs
fundamentally from standard PU learning. The safety monitor itself has already partitioned the two
types of safe trajectories across its alarm boundary: observed positives correspond to safe behavior
that the monitor accepts, whereas the latent positives of interest are precisely the safe trajectories
that it incorrectly rejects. Consequently, the observed positive set may not be representative of the
false alarms in the unlabeled pool. Learning directly from these positives without accounting for this
selection mechanism risks reproducing the monitor’s existing decision pattern rather than correcting
its errors (Bekker & Davis, 2018).

In this work, to prioritize false-alarm agent trajectories identified by safety monitors, we propose a
two-stage positive–unlabeled ranking framework that can prioritize most-likely false alarms in an
auditing queue. In the first stage, we adapt the verified-safe non-alarms towards the alarm pool and
reduce excessive negative pressure on plausible false alarms to mitigate the shift between the safe
references and the false alarms, which helps us develop much more effective PU classifiers. But due
to the uncertainty of PU learning process, we found that trained classifiers with different initializations
provide distinctive auditing queues. Therefore, we propose a reliability-gated distillation strategy to
distill the consistent alarm orderings across multiple PU classifiers into a single student. Then, in the
second stage, we can obtain an initial auditing queue by ranking the scores predicted by the student
PU classifier. To further exploit structure across task contexts, we refine the auditing queue with
consensus-guided structural refinement driven by hierarchical safe-reference support, target-alarm
relations, and reference agreement. Extensive experiments on two benchmark datasets and four
dataset–monitor settings demonstrated the superiority of our proposed framework in prioritizing false
alarms of agent trajectories compared with a variety of state-of-the-art potential baseline approaches.
In summary, our main contributions are:
• We identify the issue of frequent false alarms raised by current agent safety monitors, and to our
best knowledge, propose the first effective framework to prioritize most-likely false alarms in the
auditing queue.
• We formulate agent false-alarm auditing as a special positive–unlabeled ranking problem under
monitor-conditioned selection and develop a series of designs to mitigate the shift between
positives and hidden positives and search for consistency across PU learning processes and task
contexts.
• We conduct extensive experiments against established PU methods, direct auditing controls
based on native monitor scores and frozen representations, and local LLM re-judgment. Across
mainstream safety monitors, our method achieves a macro AUPRC of 0.6444, outperforming
eight evaluated PU baselines by 5.27–16.98 absolute percentage points and recovering 33.3%
more false alarms than PULDA at only a5%auditing budget (Table 1).
2 PRELIMINARIES
Safety monitoring and LLM-based judgment.Existing work assesses agent safety through
learned risk prediction, policy verification, and direct LLM judgment. Granite Guardian detects
risks in model inputs and outputs (Padhi et al., 2025), while AgentDoG provides contextual risk
diagnosis for execution trajectories (Liu et al., 2026). StepGuard checks candidate tool actions before
execution and balances safe and unsafe supervision to reduce over-defense and under-defense (Zheng
et al., 2026). Policy-based approaches include GuardAgent, which translates safety requirements
into executable checks (Xiang et al., 2025), and ShieldAgent, which combines policy modeling with
logical verification (Chen et al., 2025). R-Judge evaluates the ability of general-purpose LLMs to
judge safety from interaction records (Yuan et al., 2024). These studies focus on improving safety
judgments and interventions. Our work addresses a complementary task after alarms are raised: using
limited verified-safe supervision to prioritize likely false alarms for human review.
Positive–unlabeled learning.PU methods learn from labeled positives and an unlabeled mixture
through risk estimation, distribution alignment, or sample-level supervision. Prior work includes
nnPU, which uses non-negative risk estimation to reduce overfitting (Kiryo et al., 2017), and DC-
PU, which constrains positive and negative risks to balance classification errors (Li et al., 2025).
Dist-PU aligns predicted label distributions with class-prior information (Zhao et al., 2022), and
PULDA combines this alignment with functional-margin learning (Jiang et al., 2023). Robust-PU
iteratively selects reliable negative examples (Zhu et al., 2023), PUL-CPBF constructs supervision
using classifiers with different probability boundaries (Li et al., 2024), and HolisticPU identifies
unlabeled examples through their prediction trends (Wang et al., 2023). NcPU addresses unreliable
PU supervision through representation alignment (Zhao et al., 2026). Related work also addresses
nonrandom positive selection (Bekker & Davis, 2018) and AUC optimization from PU data (Sakai
et al., 2018). Our setting connects these directions through monitor-induced selection: verified-safe
non-alarms and hidden safe alarms share a safety label but are selected by opposite monitor decisions.
Our method adapts safe supervision and ranking refinement to recover false alarms under a fixed
review budget.

Setting and observed data.Let τi denote an agent execution trajectory and yi ∈ {+1,−1}its
safety label, with +1 indicating safe behavior and −1 indicating unsafe behavior. A safety monitor
Mψ with fixed configuration ψ provides a representation hi ∈R d and an alarm decision ai ∈ {0,1},
where ai = 1 indicates an alarm. We observe a small set DP of verified-safe non-alarmed trajectories
(yi = +1, ai = 0), a set DU of unlabeled training alarms, and a separate target cohort C={τ i}Ni=1
with ai = 1 for every member of both alarm sets. Safety is the positive class in our PU formulation;
members of DP are observed positives and are termedTN referencesrelative to the monitor’s alarm
decision. Frozen representations, task features, and isolation-group identifiers are available for all
three sets. Safety labels are observed only forDP during fitting; the labels and false-alarm proportions
ofD U andCare unknown.
Problem definition.The auditor produces an ordering of the N target alarms for human review.
Under a review budget B∈ {1, . . . , N}, only the first B alarms are inspected, and the objective is
to maximize the number of false alarms (yi = +1) among them. The ordering may depend on the
unlabeled target features; target safety labels are used only for evaluation after the order is fixed. The
auditor determines review priority while preserving all original alarm decisions.
3 METHOD
3.1 OVERVIEW
The difficulty is that the known-safe trajectories passed the monitor, whereas the false alarms we
seek were selected by it. Treating every alarm as a negative example can therefore bury the very
trajectories that reviewers should see first. Stage I learns a PU auditor in two steps. Trust-aware
supervision transfers TN references toward the alarm domain and reduces negative pressure on
plausible FPs. Reliability-gated distillation then combines the ordering preferences of several frozen
reference auditors in one student. Stage II freezes that student and refines its queue with hierarchical
TN support and relations among target alarms. A predicted measure of reference agreement controls
the final per-alarm adjustment. Both stages may use unlabeled target features; neither uses target
safety labels for fitting. Figure 2 follows these three components from left to right.

4 EXPERIMENTS
4.1 EXPERIMENTALSETUP
Protocol.We evaluate four dataset–monitor settings on ATBench (Li et al., 2026b) and Trace-
Safe (Chen et al., 2026), covering AgentDoG (Liu et al., 2026), Granite Guardian (Padhi et al., 2025),
and StepGuard (Zheng et al., 2026). Each setting provides 40 verified-safe non-alarms and unlabeled
training alarms, with 510 target alarms in total. Target labels remain sealed until all rankings are
fixed, and all safety monitors remain frozen.
Baselines and metrics.We compare against eight PU methods: nnPU (Kiryo et al., 2017), DC-
PU (Li et al., 2025), Dist-PU (Zhao et al., 2022), Robust-PU (Zhu et al., 2023), PULDA (Jiang
et al., 2023), PUL-CPBF (Li et al., 2024), HolisticPU (Wang et al., 2023), and feature-adapted
NcPU (Zhao et al., 2026). All methods use the same frozen features and data splits. We report macro
AUPRC/AUROC (↑), AUGRC (↓), and false alarms recovered under 5%, 10%, and 20% review
budgets. Full implementation and evaluation details are in Appendix A.

5 CONCLUSION
In this work, we study false-alarm auditing for agent safety monitors under monitor-induced selection,
where verified-safe non-alarms may poorly represent the hidden safe alarms of interest. We develop a
two-stage PU ranking framework that first adapts verified-safe supervision toward the alarm domain
and reduces excessive negative pressure on plausible false alarms, then stabilizes ranking preferences
across independently trained PU models and further exploits hierarchical safe-reference support and
relations among alarms. Across mainstream safety monitors, our method improves macro AUPRC
over eight PU baselines by 5.27–16.98 absolute percentage points and recovers 33.3% more false
alarms than PULDA at a 5% review budget, with consistent gains on a larger external benchmark
of 4,054 held-out alarms. The framework requires no alarm safety labels for fitting and leaves the
monitor decisions unchanged.

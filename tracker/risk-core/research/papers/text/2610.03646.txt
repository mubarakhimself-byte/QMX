When May a Bandit Leave Its Anchor?
E-Process-Authorized Thompson Sampling under
Non-stationarity
Mayand Gulati1 Kerong Wang1 WeiChen Au2
1UC Santa Barbara 2Purdue University
Abstract
Stationarity rewards memory, but after change the same history can mislead. We
ask when forgetting should be permitted. E-process-authorized Thompson sam-
pling (e-ATS) gives each arm full-history and discounted Beta states. An anytime-
valid e-process first authorizes the discounted state, then a reversible relevance
score controls its influence. Before authorization, e-ATS exactly follows optimistic
Thompson sampling (OTS). Under a Beta–Bernoulli prior-predictive stationary
model, e-ATS’s probability of ever departing from OTS is at most the chosenαE,
without fitted thresholds. Relative to e-ATS, removing authorization increased mean
normalized dynamic pseudo-regret by 38.4% on the registered suite but reduced it
by 7.5% on the literature-derived replay suite. Therefore, evidence controls when
adaptation begins, not whether it always helps.
1 When should a bandit stop trusting its history?
In a non-stationary bandit, history can become misleading. Existing methods discount or window
past observations, adapt their forgetting rate, or restart learning on a schedule [1, 4, 5, 10, 13, 16].
Detector-based methods instead wait for evidence of change [2, 3, 9]. We ask whether such evidence
can also control the probability that a policy leaves a trusted stationary anchor.
E-processes provide the statistical tool. Under a declared null model, an e-process crosses 1/δ with
probability at most δ, without fixing a stopping time or horizon [6, 15, 19, 20]. E-detectors combine
e-processes started at different times to monitor changes at unknown times [ 17]. Mixtures over
geometrically spaced candidate change locations are also established [14, Section 4.4]. We use the
resulting e-process threshold crossing to authorize departure. Our anchor is the Bernoulli form of
Optimistic Bayesian Sampling [11, 18], which we call optimistic Thompson sampling (OTS). It uses
each arm’s full-history Beta posterior. In e-ATS, the discounted posterior cannot affect an action
before authorization, so the crossing bound also bounds departure from the anchor. Our contribution
is a controller that translates statistical authorization into prior-predictive control of departure from
OTS, sufficient evidence conditions under adaptive sampling, and an empirical evaluation of e-ATS,
including authorization’s environment-dependent cost. When the anchor is trusted under the declared
model, limiting unnecessary departures is valuable. Conservative bandits constrain reward relative to
a baseline [21]. We constrain departure from the anchor’s trajectory.

2 From evidence to action
We use this stationary model to decide when the policy may supplement its full-history anchor with
short-memory estimates. Each arm maintains two Beta states initialized at Beta(1,1) : a full-history
posterior that retains every reward from that arm, and a discounted short-memory pseudo-posterior.
Each round, the short-memory counts above the prior are multiplied by γS = 0.995 before the
played arm incorporates its reward, giving an effective memory of about 200 rounds. Evidence grants
arm-specific authorization for the short-memory state to affect action selection, relevance measures
continuing disagreement, and a decision weight determines its influence.
Has the arm produced evidence against stationarity?To decide whether arm k can use its
short-memory posterior, we construct an arm-specific e-process that compares the declared stationary
model with models allowing for exactly one change at an unknown time.
The change time is unknown, so we consider several candidate change-points. Candidate j places a
possible change-point at arm-local pull sj. The candidate models the rewards through sj −1 using
one constant Bernoulli probability and models the rewards from pull sj onward using a second
constant probability. The two probabilities may differ. For a contiguous block of arm-local rewards

block is m(Xk,a:b) = B(1 +S a:b,1 +F a:b). The evidence for a split at change-point sj, relative to a

3 When the policy can depart from its anchor
We first show that under the declared stationary model, e-ATS is unlikely to receive permission to
depart from OTS. We then give sufficient conditions for an arm’s evidence to cross the authorization
threshold when its rewards change.
Let τauth be the first round where C Ek,t−1 = 1 for at least one arm, and let τdep be the first round
where e-ATS and OTS select different arms. We couple the policies by starting them from identical
states and giving them the same arm-specific reward sequences, policy-randomness stream, and
tie-breaking rule.
Theorem 1(Controlled departure).Pathwise, τdep ≥τ auth, and before τauth the two policies have
identical actions, observed rewards, and long-posterior states. Under(1),
P(τdep ≤T)≤P(τ auth ≤T)≤α E.(5)
Why does the guarantee hold?For each candidate change-point, L(j)k,n is a likelihood-ratio martin-
gale under the prior-predictive stationary model. Their fixed weighted average, Ek,n, is therefore also
a nonnegative martingale starting at one. Adaptive arm selection does not invalidate this property
because an arm is selected before its next reward is revealed. Ville’s inequality bounds each arm’s
crossing probability by αE/K, and a union bound over the K arms bounds the probability of any
authorization byα E.
Before authorization, every gate and decision weight is zero, so the coupled policies have the same
trajectory. A departure therefore requires an earlier e-process crossing, turning the preceding statistic
bound into (5). The resulting thresholdK/α E requires no horizon-specific fitting.
The departure bound averages over the Beta(1,1) probabilities drawn in (1). It does not hold
separately for every fixed set of reward probabilities, and it certifies permission to depart rather than
the regret or behavior afterward. Appendix B gives the formal filtration and coupling argument.

5 What this establishes
Under the declared model, the probability of departure is at most αE, and expected normalized
pseudo-regret differs from the anchor’s by at mostαE. The policy forces no exploration and has mean
rank 9.72 among 17 replay methods. Future work should develop authorization that is restartable and
valid for fixed means, together with non-stationary regret guarantees.

Which parts of the algorithm are required for the guarantee?The controlled-departure guar-
antee depends on the prior-predictive likelihood ratios, fixed nonnegative candidate weights, the
weight reserved for candidates not yet reached, and the authorization threshold K/αE. The remain-
ing constants affect how the short-memory posterior is used after authorization. They can change
the policy’s empirical behavior, but not the e-process crossing bound. The fixed values and the
formulas that depend on K and T were specified before the primary evaluation in Section 4. The
same prespecified configuration was used across all registered environments and geometries, without
environment-specific retuning after outcomes were observed. Appendix D describes the freeze and
the separation between development and evaluation data. The primary evaluation therefore assesses
one fixed policy specification rather than selecting the best configuration from the evaluation results.
These constants are not claimed to be optimal.
The experiments use the known horizon T to set λD and ρ. Experiments with an unknown horizon
were not evaluated. Using a nominal horizon to setλD and ρ does not change the controlled-departure
guarantee because neither constant enters the e-process or its authorization threshold. The nominal
horizon may change behavior after authorization, and that alternative was not evaluated here. Only
αE is an error probability.
Algorithm 1 is the fixed specification evaluated in Section 4. Before authorization, e-ATS follows
OTS exactly, while afterward the permanent latch permits adaptation and the relevance score controls
how strongly the short-memory posterior affects arm selection.
B Proof of controlled departure
Theorem 1 combines a statistical result with an algorithmic one. We first show that each arm’s
evidence remains a martingale when the policy chooses arms. We then bound the probability that
any arm becomes authorized. Finally, we show that e-ATS’s trajectory cannot depart from OTS’s
trajectory before such an authorization.
Why is the arm-specific evidence a martingale?For armk, let
Hk,n =σ(X k,1, . . . , Xk,n)
contain its first n observed rewards. Under the prior-predictive stationary model in Equation (1),
m(Xk,1:n) is the probability assigned to this reward sequence by one unknown Bernoulli probability.
As described in Section 2, candidate change-point sj marks a possible boundary between two periods
in arm k’s reward sequence. Rewards before sj use one constant Bernoulli probability, while rewards
from sj onward use a second probability that may differ. Considering several candidate change-points
allows the evidence process to look for departures from stationarity without knowing when a change
occurred. The numerator of Equation (2) assigns independent Beta(1,1) priors to the probabilities
before and afters j.

same n rewards. Before pull sj, the proposed change has not occurred, so the two models assign the

Together, these results characterize how much evidence a candidate can provide, when that evidence
is sufficient for authorization, and how the latch changes the controller. The authorization conditions
presented only concern information from the observations collected from an arm. Theorem 3 allows

prefix, aligned-change assumptions. Neither result guarantees that a changed arm will be sampled
enough to obtain authorization by a specified calendar round.
D Experimental design and additional results
Section 4 reports the main empirical findings. This appendix explains how the environments change

We use two suites because they answer different questions. The registered suite was designed to
measure the contribution of authorization across a broad range of arm counts and horizons. The
registered suite follows a protocol fixed before the primary evaluation. The replay suite was assembled
from non-stationary bandit families used in our earlier baseline study and is used to compare e-ATS
with established methods. We later repeated the gate comparison on the replay suite to test whether
the registered result transferred to that different population.

A mean trajectory is the complete table of Bernoulli reward probabilities {µk,t} for every arm and
round. A potential-reward table contains the corresponding binary reward that each arm would
produce if selected in each round. A policy observes only the entry for the arm it actually selects.
A geometry specifies the number of arms K and horizon T , with B=T /K denoting the nominal
rounds available per arm. The initial policy seed initializes the random-number stream used for
posterior sampling.
An experimental group fixes one mean trajectory, one potential-reward table, one geometry, and one
initial policy seed. The compared policies share these inputs, so their regret difference forms a paired
contrast. For gate comparisons, we report
∆(c) = ¯RT (c)− ¯RT (e-ATS).
Positive values favor e-ATS, while negative values favor comparatorc. Lower regret is always better.

Each mechanism and geometry contains 91 randomly generated environments and 27 fixed stress
environments. The stress environments use prespecified boundary settings of the generator parameters
rather than randomly sampled settings. The primary regret analysis uses the random environments,
giving9×20×91 = 16,380non-stationary groups and1×20×91 = 1,820stationary groups.
The fixed environments form a separate census of 5,400 groups and are not used for the primary mean
or confidence interval. The pooled contrast across their 4,860 non-stationary groups was instead used
as a predeclared breadth check. Development, validation, and evaluation used disjoint environments
and potential-reward tables.
How does an environment change with K and T ?The K= 4 environments illustrate each
mechanism but are not copied into the larger geometries. At every K, the generator creates a new
K-arm mean trajectory from a deterministic evaluation seed. The horizon is T=KB , so increasing
K at fixed B increases the total horizon while retaining the same nominal number of rounds per arm.
For mechanisms with discrete changes, the round at which each reward probability changes is
specified as a fraction of T . At larger geometries, the generator creates new trajectories under
the same mechanism rules. Discrete changes are located relative to the horizon through t/T , and
mechanisms designed to change one arm continue to affect only one arm.
Does authorization help on the registered suite?Removing only the authorization latch gives
the open-ramp policy, gk,t =r k,Nk(t−1). The short-memory posterior is then eligible to influence
decisions as soon as its relevance becomes positive.
We chose this comparison to isolate the contribution of authorization. The two policies are otherwise
identical, but the open ramp allows the relevance score to act without first requiring an e-process
crossing. We fixed this comparison and its margin before examining the evaluation results. The
margin was cleared only if the lower one-sided 95% confidence bound for ∆(open ramp) exceeded
0.0002. We formed this bound by resampling environments within each mechanism and geometry
and recomputing the paired regret difference.

the open ramp. The paired contrast was

What happens when the arms are stationary?Only the registered suite contains stationary regret
experiments. In these environments, every arm’s reward probability remains fixed throughout the
horizon, although its binary rewards still vary. Across the 1,820 random stationary groups, mean regret
was 0.0115697 for e-ATS and 0.0115673 for OTS. Both round to 0.01157. During these experiments,
e-ATS authorized exactly one arm. The open ramp had mean regret0.02937, about two and a half
times that of either policy.
Separate from both experimental suites, we also examined how often any arm crossed the authorization
threshold under the prior-predictive null of Theorem 1. We used three (K, T)settings: (4,2500) ,
(32,20000) , and (8,50000) , each with 4,000 runs. In every run, each arm’s reward probability was
drawn independently from Beta(1,1) and then fixed. At least one arm crossed the authorization
threshold in4of the4,000runs at(K, T) = (4,2500),1of the4,000runs at(32,20000), and4of
the4,000runs at(8,50000).

How often does the permanent latch activate?By the end of the registered non-stationary runs,
the mean fraction of authorized arms was9.97%. The fraction was 5.77% in gradual drift and 11.93%
in mixed abrupt change and drift. On the replay suite, the overall fraction was 38.27%, including
15.38%in random drift and14.42%in the mixed family.
Each fraction counts every arm that was authorized at least once before the run ended. Since
authorization is never withdrawn, an arm remains counted even if its later rewards again agree
with the stationary model. The fractions therefore describe whether authorization ever occurred, not
whether the evidence remained current at the time when the run ended.
How do alternative authorization rules compare?We next ask whether the policy’s behavior
depends on how authorization is granted. We replace the e-process crossing with two generalized
likelihood-ratio (GLR) rules and leave the rest of the policy unchanged. All three policies use the
same candidate change-points, relevance score, decision weight, posterior updates, potential-reward
table, and initial policy seed. Thus, only the rule used to turn on the authorization latch changes.
For candidatej, the Bernoulli GLR statistic is

Does the registered result transfer to the replay suite?No. On the replay suite, the open ramp
was lower in 15 of 18 cells, eight of nine families, and 3,204 of 4,500 groups. Its mean regret
advantage was 0.01154, with interval [0.01089,0.01218] . The two negative replay suite points in the
left panel of Figure 3, in orange, show that the open ramp was lower at both evaluated arm counts.
We added the replay gate comparison to ask whether the registered result carried to the environments
used for the baseline comparison. It did not. On the registered suite, e-ATS beat the open ramp, while
the open ramp beat e-ATS on the replay suite. The practical GLR remained close to e-ATS on both
suites, whereas the certified GLR had higher regret. Authorization therefore matters, but its effect on
regret depends on the environments being studied. The comparison only estimates authorization’s
overall effect on regret. It cannot determine whether the difference comes from delayed adaptation,
short-memory forgetting, or subsequent action choices. Note that these experiments also do not
establish any authorization rule as universally best.
D.2 How does e-ATS compare with established baselines?
How is the replay suite constructed?The replay suite evaluates nine non-stationary families
at two geometries, (K, T) = (4,2500) and (32,20000) . Both have B=T /K= 625 , the same
nominal number of rounds per arm, giving a total of 18 family–geometry combinations. Within each
combination, every policy is evaluated on the same 250 experimental settings, yielding a total of
4,500 matched comparisons.
Periods and segment lengths are multiplied by K/4 when moving from K= 4 to K= 32 . Switching
hazards are multiplied by 4/K, and diffusion increments are divided by
p
K/4. These changes keep
the amount of non-stationarity relative to the rounds available per arm approximately comparable.
The larger geometry is a newly generated environment, not an eightfold repetition of the four-arm
trajectory.
Table 5 summarizes how the replay families are constructed, while Figure 2 shows one frozen
four-arm example from each family.
Each slow-sine, fast-sine, and abrupt-cycle geometry holds one mean trajectory fixed across 250
reward simulations. For each of the other six families and their geometries, we generate 50 different
mean trajectories. We then simulate rewards five times from each trajectory, again giving 250 policy
comparisons for that family and geometry. This separates differences across mean trajectories from
randomness in the rewards generated from the same trajectory.
The original replay analysis calculated each interval from 50,000 repeated draws. Within every
combination of environment family and geometry, each draw selected 50 groups of five policy
comparisons with replacement. In the twelve settings with random trajectories, each group contained
one mean trajectory and its five reward repetitions. In the six settings with a fixed trajectory, each
group contained five repetitions of that trajectory.

In our experiments, the geometries differ in arm count, total horizon, and generator scaling. The rank
change cannot be attributed to any one of those differences. The gate transfer also shows that the
open ramp outperformed e-ATS on this same replay population. The first- and second-place cells do
not establish that the e-process gate caused those rankings.
Overall, authorization changes regret, but whether it helps depends on the environments being studied.
On the registered suite, authorization reduced regret relative to opening the short-memory channel
immediately, except at K= 4 . On the replay suite, the open ramp performed better, while e-ATS still
improved substantially over OTS and performed especially well in two K= 32 cells. The practical
GLR closely matched e-ATS. The certified GLR had higher regret and provided the stronger fixed-
mean guarantee, while the fitted GLR slightly outperformed e-ATS after receiving geometry-specific
calibration. In these suites, e-ATS differed little in regret from the untuned practical detector while
retaining its prior-predictive guarantee.

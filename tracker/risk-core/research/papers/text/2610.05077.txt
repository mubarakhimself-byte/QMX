How Execution Assumptions Change Short-Horizon Sharpe
Rankings: Evidence from a Synthetic Trading Benchmark
Weicheng Xue
Virginia Tech
weich97@vt.edu
Abstract
Backtests of LLM trading agents often assume that every order
fills at the closing price. We ask whether this choice changes only
reported returns or also the order of the agents. Five prompted
LLM signal policies and seven classical baselines trade the same
synthetic price paths under six execution settings, from near-ideal
fills to latency, spread, participation, and impact stresses. The main
experiment contains2 ,462runs with matched decision frequen-
cies and paired market paths. On the compressed two-asset board,
agreement between the near-ideal and default-stress rankings falls
to Kendall𝜏𝑏 = 0.21in the high-volatility regime, compared with
0.82in the calm regime. The seed-bootstrap intervals, [0.00, 0.52]
and[0.48, 0.94], are wide and overlap. On a fixed 11-policy board,
agreement rises from 0.24 with two assets to 0.85 with ten; the two-
asset point estimate differs substantially from the wider settings
we tested. Rank changes are related to turnover, and comparisons
with buy-and-hold also depend on how that anchor is initialized.
The experiment does not compare LLM trading skill. It shows that,
on a short horizon, an execution convention can become part of the
benchmark’s headline. Execution assumptions and rank stability
should be reported alongside returns.

1 Introduction
LLM trading agents are often ranked by backtested returns. The
model observes a market state, proposes a trade, and receives a
score after the fact. Between the proposal and the score, however,
the backtester has to decide when and how the trade fills. Many
studies use complete fills at the observed close without discussing
the choice.
That simplification would be harmless if execution costs moved
every strategy by roughly the same amount. There is little reason
to assume that they do. Orders cross spreads, wait, fill only in
part, or move prices; strategies also trade at different rates. Market-
microstructure research has studied these effects for decades [1, 4,
9], and practical backtests routinely vary their cost assumptions.
LLM-agent evaluations rarely report whether their rankings survive
the same exercise.
To measure this effect, twelve policies trade identical market
paths under six execution settings. Five obtain signals from LLMs
and seven are classical baselines. Market paths and decision fre-
quencies are shared, so the comparison changes execution without
changing the information initially shown to an agent.
The largest rank change occurs in a short, high-volatility ex-
periment with only two assets. It is also the setting in which the
leading policies are hardest to distinguish. Wider universes have
higher agreement point estimates, and uncertainty is large. The
finding is about the benchmark, not about which family of policies
trades better. Here “LLM agent” means a prompted signal policy:
the model produces one bounded signal per asset, while portfolio
construction, risk controls, and execution are shared. Agents with
tools, roles, or persistent memory are outside the main experiment
(Section 7).
The paper’s contribution is an empirical, paired comparison
that holds market paths and decision timing fixed while changing
execution. We follow the low-agreement case through turnover,
universe size, horizon, anchor initialization, and an empirical-price
check. Run-level results and the code used to build the tables and
figures are publicly released (Section 8).
2 Related Work
Financial language models [19, 23] and agent frameworks such as
TradingAgents [20], FinRobot [24], and FinMem [25] have made
backtested trading a common evaluation task. Because these sys-
tems differ in their use of tools, memory, and role-based reasoning,
we treat the model only as the source of a bounded signal and keep
the downstream portfolio and execution stack fixed.
Execution itself has a much older literature. Work on informed
trading, optimal liquidation, empirical impact, and transient order-
book effects shows why costs depend on both the order and the
market [1, 2, 4, 7, 9, 17]. Configurable transaction costs are also
standard in trading environments such as FinRL-Meta [13]. Realized
trading costs can differ substantially from simple estimates and can
change which strategies appear viable [5, 6].
The closest precedent is work on benchmark sensitivity. Backtest
selection and multiple testing can produce unstable conclusions
[3, 8, 14]. LLM evaluations are also sensitive to prompt wording
and protocol choices [11, 15, 18], and multi-step agent benchmarks
introduce dependencies on the protocol and state [12]. Finance-LLM
surveys and broader benchmarks summarize model performance
[10, 16, 21]. What has received less attention is whether a trading
leaderboard survives a change in its execution assumptions. That
is the question studied here.

4 Experimental Setup
Agents.The seven deterministic baselines are buy-and-hold, signal-
weighted allocation, naive momentum, five-bar mean reversion,
12-bar risk parity, 24-bar minimum variance, and a seeded random
allocator. They are long-only. All but buy-and-hold rebalance at
every step. Per-name weights are capped at 0.35, except minimum
variance, for which we use 0.08 to prevent a near-singular two-asset
covariance estimate from concentrating the portfolio in one asset.
In the two-asset buy-and-hold portfolio, each asset receives 35%
and the remainder stays in cash.
The five LLM policies use four provider-routed aliases (gpt-5.5,
gemini-3.1-pro, claude-opus-4.7, and glm-5) and one direct-API
model (deepseek-v4-pro). The routed aliases do not identify stable
backend checkpoints. We treat them as observations of the inter-
faces available during collection, not as reproducible measurements
of named model versions. The direct API also uses an undated
identifier, so it does not establish a fixed backend version.
Each LLM receives recent OHLCV bars, portfolio state, and risk
feedback in a structured JSON prompt. It returns one signal in
[−1, 1] per asset, not target weights. A shared strategy converts
those signals into weights before the common risk and execu-
tion layers run. The prompt does not name the execution setting
or expose its parameters. The first decision is therefore identical
across settings. Later decisions may differ because earlier fills have
changed the portfolio state.
Responses are cached by provider:model:sha256(prompt),
with timestamps removed from the prompt. Provider caches are not
redistributed. The public release contains the run-level data used
in the paper together with hashes for those files.
Market regimes.A seeded simulator generates calm-trend,
high-volatility, and jump/tail paths. The generated paths cannot
have appeared verbatim in a model’s training data, which reduces
direct test-set memorization. Section 5.9 adds a deterministic check
on real OHLCV data; we do not use that historical window to make
claims about LLM performance.
Repetition and pairing.We use ten seeds in every (regime,
level, agent) cell. A seed fixes the market path, and every agent takes
12 decisions on that path at each execution level. The main matrix
contains12×3×6×10 = 2,160runs. Another 302 LLM runs support
the sampling-variance check, for 2,462 records and 14,424 provider
calls in total. The robustness experiments are counted separately.
We compute Sharpe for each run and then average across seeds
within a cell.

5.1 Execution Assumptions Change the
Ranking
Figure 1 compares the E0 ranking with each stressed ranking. The
calm board is fairly stable, with 𝜏𝑏 between 0.73 and 0.85. The
jump/tail board is less stable (0.30–0.58). The largest change occurs

only when we vary a common parameter grid from liquid to illiquid

This distinction changes how the result should be read. Total

movement comes from estimating risk on a short window, not from

5.3 Which Policies Move
Table 2 shows where the high-volatility change comes from. Under
E0, the top five are all classical policies. Under E1, every LLM policy
moves up between two and seven places, and four enter the top five.
The two weakest classical policies remain at the bottom. Turnover
differs, but not cleanly: classical policies average 18–20 E1 fills
and the LLM policies average 15–18, with similar fill rates of 0.80–
0.86. The confidence intervals overlap substantially. What changes
clearly is the ordering; the table does not separate the trading ability
of the two groups.
Some LLM policies even gain a small amount of Sharpe and
total return from E0 to E1; deepseek-v4-pro moves from+0.050to
+0.076total return. This need not reflect better decisions. Frequent
rebalancing is already costly in this short experiment, and E1 sup-
presses some of those trades. A lower-turnover policy can move up
in relative terms even when execution becomes less favorable for
the board as a whole.
5.4 Ranking Changes Within Turnover Groups
We group the policies into terciles using realized E1 turnover and
recompute E0–E1 agreement within each group. The mean within-
tercile𝜏𝑏 is 0.63. In the high-volatility regime, all three terciles give
0.67 even though the full board gives 0.21. Much of the full-board
movement lies between turnover groups, although policies with
similar turnover still reorder. This is a descriptive check: each tercile
contains only four policies, and E1 turnover is itself an outcome of
the experiment.

5.7 Which Frictions Bind
At the $100,000 account size, a position is at most about 350 shares
per asset, while synthetic bar volume is around one million shares.
The 5% and 1% participation caps therefore allow roughly 50,000
and 10,000 shares. No deterministic fill reaches either cap, realized
participation stays below 0.1%, and linear impact is well below one
basis point. Eleven of the 12 policies produce identical records under
the two cap settings. The remaining difference disappears when the
direct model is replayed from a fixed response cache, confirming
that it came from resampling.
E1 fill rates lie between 0.78 and 0.86 for both LLM and classical
policies. They fall to 0.37–0.54 in the harsh setting, mainly because
of three-bar latency. Similar fill rates do not imply similar rank
changes. In high volatility, latency lowers the fill rate to 0.48 but
leaves𝜏𝑏 = 0.58; E1 leaves the fill rate near 0.83 but gives𝜏𝑏 = 0.21.
The spread setting has the largest slippage and gives 𝜏𝑏 = 0.39.
What matters is which policies a cost penalizes, especially how
often and how much they rebalance.
Consistent with this, a regression of E0–E1 return loss or rank
shift on a policy’s own fill rate and slippage cost gives𝑅2 < 0.02
(𝑝> 0.5for every coefficient, 𝑛= 36cells). These diagnostics do
not provide a reliable after-the-fact correction for the leaderboard.
Finally, we repeat the deterministic experiment at $30M. The
participation cap now binds on 33–95% of fills, but high-volatility
E0–E1 agreement remains𝜏𝑏 = 0.81at both account sizes. For this
policy set, per-trade costs and turnover matter more than whether
the cap binds.

5.8 Parameter Sensitivity
In the high-volatility regime, we also vary impact, participation,
and latency together along a liquid-to-illiquid grid. A liquid setting
(impact 0.05, 10% participation, no latency) leaves the deterministic
ranking unchanged (𝜏𝑏 = 1.0). A typical-equity setting gives 0.81,
and the illiquid end (impact 0.30, 1% participation, three-bar latency)
gives 0.43. Unlike the heterogeneous E2 settings, this controlled
grid produces a monotone pattern.
5.9 Real-Price Robustness
We run the seven deterministic policies on real Yahoo OHLCV
using the same execution settings. The data cover eight liquid
ETFs and bitcoin during a 2022 rates drawdown and a 2025 cross-
asset window, with weekly adjusted closes, 12 decisions, and three
offsets. Across both windows, E0-to-stress𝜏𝑏 ranges from 0.71 to
0.90. The top three change only under the spread setting (Jaccard
0.5; otherwise 1.0). This is milder than the synthetic two-asset result,
but it shows that deterministic rankings can also move on empirical
price and volume paths. It is not a historical test of the LLM policies.
6 Discussion
The practical point is narrower than the familiar statement that
execution matters. In the high-volatility synthetic cell, one simu-
lator choice changes the apparent leaders from classical policies
to LLM policies. The intervals overlap, so neither ordering demon-
strates that one group is better. Instead, the comparison shows that
a benchmark can turn an implementation choice into its headline
result.
A useful evaluation should state its fill rule, costs, latency, par-
ticipation limits, decision frequency, and anchor initialization. It
should then rerun the whole board under at least one plausible
alternative and report rank agreement, not only changes in return.
Shared market paths and paired statistics make that comparison
more informative, and run-level records let others inspect it.
Subtracting an estimated cost after the run is not equivalent. A
policy’s own fill rate and slippage explain almost none of its rank
shift (𝑅2 < 0.02). The shift depends on how the same friction acts
across the full set of policies. A leaderboard should therefore be
rerun, rather than adjusted one score at a time.
7 Limitations
Our strongest result is also the narrowest. The headline board has
two assets, 12 decisions, and several policies with similar Sharpe
estimates. In the fixed 11-policy universe experiment, high-volatility
E0–E1𝜏𝑏 rises from 0.24 at two assets to 0.85 at ten. A same-seed
deterministic ten-asset experiment gives 0.79–0.93 across the five
stressed settings. The value 0.21 is a result for the compressed board,
not a general effect size. We exclude a provider-backed ten-asset E2
run because its E0 and E2 seeds do not match; a fully paired LLM
ladder at that size remains to be run.
The short horizon makes both Sharpe and terminal handling
noisy. Pending orders are canceled after 12 decisions, so latencyℓ
can censor up toℓ/12of late orders. We did not run the matched

The LLM experiment is closed-loop. Once fills differ, a model
sees a different portfolio state and may change its next signal. The
deterministic policies provide a mechanical comparison because
their targets are identical across execution levels and the drawdown
switch never fires (0/840steps). For the direct LLM board, replaying
the E0 decisions under E1 gives𝜏𝑏 = 0.56, compared with 0.44 when
the policies decide again. Mechanical execution changes the ranking
on its own. The additional difference is consistent with feedback

and fresh provider sampling, but these two𝜏𝑏 values do not separate
their contributions.
Four of the five headline LLM policies are routed. None of the
hosted API identifiers binds the observations to an immutable back-
end version. The frozen records preserve the analyzed snapshot,
but those responses cannot be collected again byte for byte. The
conclusions apply to the provider interfaces observed during collec-
tion and to prompted signal policies, not to stable model versions
or richer agents with tools, reflection, and multiple roles.
Finally, synthetic paths support pairing and avoid training-data
contamination, but they do not establish live performance. The real-
OHLCV experiment covers only deterministic policies on nine ETF
and crypto instruments. Running the LLMs on the same historical
window would introduce a possible memorization confound. A
post-training-cutoff forward test and quote-level fill replay remain
outside this study.
8 Reproducibility
The code, execution settings, seeds, run-level outcomes, and analy-
sis scripts used in the paper are available at https://github.com/
weich97/execution-assumptions-rankings. The release includes
each regime-by-level leaderboard, bootstrap intervals, and per-
policy turnover, fill rate, and slippage cost. The tables and figures
can be rebuilt from the frozen records without an API call, and the
deterministic experiments can be rerun from the start. Fresh LLM
collection requires provider access; raw provider responses and
per-step trajectories are not included.
9 Conclusion
We asked whether a trading-agent leaderboard survives when the
agents, market paths, and performance metric stay fixed but exe-
cution changes. On the compressed two-asset board, it often does
not; the wider-universe checks have higher agreement. The result
concerns what the benchmark measures, not which policy family
trades better. Execution rules belong to the benchmark specification
and should be varied before small rank differences are interpreted
as differences in agent quality.

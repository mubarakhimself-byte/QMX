ABSTRACT
Factor mining seeks to discover signals from financial data that predict future
asset returns and guide portfolio construction. Automated factor mining now
spans genetic programming, reinforcement learning, generative models, and large
language model agents. Yet it remains unclear whether advances across these
paradigms yield more generalizable, distinct, and economically useful financial
signals. We introduce FactorBench, a portfolio-aware benchmark comparing
roughly five thousand mined factors from nine automated mining methods across
five equity markets. A shared data and evaluation contract supports both symbolic
expressions and executable Python factors, connecting heterogeneous discovery
algorithms to common signal combination and portfolio construction procedures.
FactorBench traces the outputs of mining systems across three levels: factor va-
lidity, temporal generalization, and predictiveness beyond measured risk and style
exposures; within- and across-method pool distinctness, including similarity to
the benchmark Alpha101; and composite-signal quality and after-cost long-only
and long–short portfolio performance. After systematically assessing whether ad-
vances in factor mining translate into signal quality and portfolio performance,
FactorBench finds that no paradigm consistently dominates.
1 INTRODUCTION
Quantitative investment relies on factors: functions that transform information available at a given
time into stock-level scores that predict future returns. These scores are used to rank securities and
serve as inputs to portfolio construction. Discovering a factor traditionally requires forming a finan-
cial hypothesis, expressing it as a formula or executable program, and evaluating the resulting signal
on historical data. Automated factor mining casts this process as a machine-discovery problem in
which a system searches a large space of candidate transformations and returns a pool of signals for
downstream selection and combination. The task is challenging because financial signals are weak
and non-stationary, while repeated search over the same historical observations can readily identify
patterns that do not persist.
Automated factor mining has developed through several generations of search methods. Genetic
programming (GP) explores structured spaces of symbolic expressions through evolutionary opera-
tions. Reinforcement learning (RL) and neural generative methods learn how to construct or sample
expressions, often considering how multiple factors work together (Yu et al., 2023; Zhu & Zhu,
2025; Shi et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). More recently, large language model (LLM) based agents
have expanded the process beyond symbolic search by formulating financial hypotheses, generating
expressions or executable code, and revising candidates through empirical feedback (Li et al., 2026;
Tang et al., 2025; Han et al., 2026). However, methodological innovation has outpaced standardized
evaluation. Published results are produced under method-specific data, objectives, and backtesting
pipelines, making performance difficult to compare across studies. More fundamentally, the score
optimized during discovery is only an intermediate proxy: a mining system ultimately delivers a
pool of signals whose value depends on how they behave outside the search environment and when
used together in investment decisions. It therefore remains unclear whether newer search paradigms
represent progress in practically useful financial discovery.

To address this gap, we introduce FactorBench, a portfolio-aware benchmark for assessing progress
in automated factor discovery. Our contributions are threefold.
We enable broad comparison across discovery systems.FactorBench evaluates roughly 5,000
factors from 9 methods spanning genetic programming, reinforcement learning, generative search,
and LLM agents across 5 equity markets. A shared data and evaluation contract accommodates both
symbolic expressions and executable code while preserving each method’s native discovery process.
We define progress beyond the search objective.FactorBench treats discovery performance as an
intermediate result and evaluates mining systems at three connected levels: the factors they generate,
the pools they deliver, and the portfolios those signals support. This systems-level view connects
predictive quality to signal distinctness and downstream investment value.
We show that advances in search do not translate consistently downstream.Relative advantages
change as outputs move from the discovery phase to out-of-sample evaluation, from individual fac-
tors to factor pools, and from signals to portfolios. Across markets and evaluation stages, we find
no consistent progression in which newer mining paradigms deliver more generalizable, distinct,
and economically useful signals than earlier approaches. As the search for consistently better fac-
tors continues, FactorBench has the potential to become the evaluation toolkit for the field. The
benchmark platform is accessible at repository1.
2 RELATEDWORK
Automated factor discovery.Automated factor discovery searches for signals from market ob-
servations that predict future asset returns. A discovered factor is typically represented either as a
symbolic expression composed of market-data fields and mathematical operators, or as executable
code that maps a market-data panel to a stock-level signal. Classical libraries such as Alpha101 col-
lect manually specified formulaic factors and have become widely used references for developing
and evaluating automated discovery methods (Kakushadze, 2016). Genetic programming provides
an early approach to exploring this combinatorial space through mutation, crossover, and selection.
More recent methods formulate expression construction as sequential decision making. AlphaGen
optimizes the marginal contribution of a newly generated factor to an existing factor collection (Yu
et al., 2023), while AlphaQCM uses distributional reinforcement learning to address the sparse and
non-stationary rewards encountered during factor search (Zhu & Zhu, 2025). AlphaCFG instead
imposes an alpha-oriented context-free grammar and combines syntax-aware representations with
Monte Carlo tree search (Yang et al., 2026). These methods primarily differ in how they represent
and explore the space of symbolic factors, as well as in whether their search objective evaluates
individual factors or their contribution to a collection.
Generative approaches place greater emphasis on producing diverse collections of candidate factors.
AlphaForge combines a generative–predictive model with dynamic factor selection and combination
(Shi et al., 2025), whereas AlphaSAGE uses structure-aware representations and generative flow net-
works to sample multiple high-reward expressions (Chen et al., 2026). More recently, large language
models have expanded factor discovery from symbolic search toward automated research workflows.
R&D-Agent coordinates hypothesis generation, executable implementation, empirical feedback, and
factor–model refinement (Li et al., 2026). AlphaAgent introduces originality, semantic-alignment,
and complexity constraints to reduce factor homogenization and decay (Tang et al., 2025). Quan-
taAlpha further combines LLM-based generation with trajectory-level evolutionary operations that
revise and recombine previous discovery processes (Han et al., 2026). Collectively, these systems
extend automated discovery from searching over expression syntax to reasoning about hypotheses
and revising candidates through experimental feedback.
Benchmarks in Finance.Benchmarking literatures in finance have developed along two comple-
mentary directions. Financial-language benchmarks evaluate capabilities such as document under-
standing, numerical reasoning, forecasting, and decision making (Islam et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024).
Quantitative-investment platforms and benchmarks instead standardize data processing, predictive
modeling, market environments, and strategy backtesting (Yang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Wang
et al., 2025). At the trading-agent level, KTD-Fin controls access to memorized market information
and attributes portfolio returns to market, style, and stock-selection components (Zhu et al., 2026).

Recent benchmarks focus more directly on automated factor discovery. AlphaBench evaluates the
factor-mining capabilities of LLMs through tasks covering factor generation, factor evaluation, and
factor search (Luo et al., 2026). AlphaEval instead provides a backtest-free framework for assessing
generated formula factors along predictive power, temporal stability, robustness to market perturba-
tions, financial logic, and diversity (Ding et al., 2026). Moving toward downstream strategy design,
AlphaForgeBench asks LLMs to produce executable factors and factor-based strategies, enabling
their outputs to be evaluated through a deterministic execution pipeline (Zhang et al., 2026). Our
FactorBench broadens the unit of evaluation from individual model capabilities to the full path from
discovery to investment decisions. By placing heterogeneous mining systems under a shared proto-
col, it examines whether advances in discovery translate into generalizable and distinct signals and,
ultimately, downstream portfolio value in diverse equity markets.
3 FACTORBENCH: BENCHMARKDESIGN
This section describes how FactorBench makes heterogeneous factor-mining systems comparable
while preserving their native discovery processes. We first present the benchmark workflow, then
introduce the shared data setting and evaluated mining methods, and finally define the factor-, pool-,
and portfolio-level evaluation protocols.
3.1 BENCHMARKOVERVIEW
FactorBench is organized as a four-block pipeline that connects various factor discovery systems
to common downstream evaluation, as illustrated in Figure 1. The first block defines the shared
benchmark setting. All methods operate on the same markets, predict the same forward return, and
follow a common chronological split. This shared setting ensures that methods are evaluated on the
same financial prediction problem and under the same temporal information boundaries.
The second block contains the heterogeneous mining systems. FactorBench covers genetic pro-
gramming, reinforcement-learning and guided search, generative search, and LLM-based agents.
Each method retains its native search algorithm, objective, and discovery procedure, allowing the
benchmark to compare the systems as they are intended to operate. Their resource use and delivered
output are recorded alongside the discovered factors, making the scale and cost of discovery visible.
The third block provides the interface between discovery and evaluation. Mining systems may
deliver symbolic expressions or executable programs, but a common execution contract converts
both representations into standardized date-by-asset signal matrices. The downstream benchmark
therefore evaluates the signals that the systems actually produce, independent of how those signals
were represented or generated.
The fourth block performs the portfolio-aware evaluation. FactorBench first examines the validity,
temporal behavior, and overlap with measured style exposures. It then evaluates whether the deliv-

3.2 BENCHMARKSETTING ANDFACTORPRODUCTION
We construct daily equity panels from five market indices: the S&P 100 of the United States, Hang
Seng Index of Hong Kong, CSI 300 of mainland China, FTSE 100 of the United Kingdom, and
Nikkei 225 of Japan. Each panel contains adjusted price-and-volume observations and derived vari-
ables computed only from information available at signal formation. All mining methods receive
the same input fields and predict the close-to-close return over the subsequent 20 trading days. We
use a chronological split with 2017–2021 for training, 2022–2023 for validation, and 2024–2025 for
testing. Training data support factor discovery, validation data may inform model selection and other
pre-test decisions, and the test period is reserved for final trading evaluation. Full index constituents,
preprocessing rules, field definitions, and temporal buffers are provided in Appendix A.
We evaluate nine factor-mining methods spanning four broad discovery paradigms. The compari-
son includes a genetic-programming baseline; reinforcement-learning and guided symbolic-search
methods represented by AlphaGen, AlphaQCM, and AlphaCFG (Yu et al., 2023; Zhu & Zhu, 2025;
Yang et al., 2026); generative methods represented by AlphaForge and AlphaSAGE (Shi et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2026); and LLM-based agents represented by R&D-Agent, AlphaAgent, and
QuantaAlpha (Li et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2025; Han et al., 2026). Methods are selected to cover
major developments in automated factor discovery and to provide an author-released implementa-
tion that can be adapted to the shared data contract. We preserve each method’s search process
and objective while recording its method-specific budget and delivered output. Eight methods pro-
duce symbolic expressions, whereas R&D-Agent produces executable Python code; both forms are
passed to the shared execution contract that maps every delivered factor to a date-by-asset signal
matrix. We additionally evaluate the implementable subset of Alpha101 as a classical, non-mined
reference (Kakushadze, 2016). Complete implementation details, upstream versions, configurations,
and deviations are reported in Appendix B.
3.3 EVALUATIONPROTOCOLS
Factor-level evaluation.We first assessfactor validity. A factor–market instance isexecutable
if its symbolic expression or Python implementation runs successfully under the shared execution
contract. We evaluate the resulting factor values separately on each trading date, where the cross
section consists of the stocks in the corresponding market. A date isscorableif at least two stocks
have finite factor values and forward-return targets, and both the factor values and the targets have
non-zero cross-sectional variance. We retain an executable factor–market instance for predictive
evaluation only if it has at least 100 scorable dates across each time split. This requirement excludes
factors whose outputs are predominantly missing or constant across stocks. For each scorable fac-
tor, we assesstemporal generalization. We compute the daily cross-sectional information coefficient
(IC), defined as the Pearson correlation between its signal and the subsequent 20-day stock return,
and summarize it separately over the training, validation, and test periods. Factor orientation is de-
termined from its training IC and held fixed thereafter, so validation and test performance measure
temporal generalization and preserve out-of-sample sign reversals. We further examine whetherfac-
tor predictivenessextends beyond the measured risk and style exposures. On each date, we regress
the signed factor cross-sectionally on market beta, volatility, momentum, short-term reversal, and
liquidity. We use adjustedR 2 to quantify exposure overlap, and residual IC to measure predictive-
ness after removing these exposures. More details are in Appendix C.
Pool-level evaluation.FactorBench evaluates whether a mining system delivers a broad and novel
collection of signals rather than repeated variants of the same idea. We measure similarity from the
cross-sectional correlation between factor signals, using Spearman correlation as the primary mea-
sure because the signals are ultimately used to rank stocks. Pool-level evaluation considers three
forms of distinctness. First,within-method redundancymeasures whether factors produced by the
same run duplicate one another. Second,across-method distinctnessexamines whether different
mining systems converge on the same signal space. Third,similarity to Alpha101assesses whether
mined factors reproduce previously published formulaic signals. The appendix evaluates the tem-
poral stability of these patterns across the training, validation, and test periods and examines their

robustness to replacing Spearman correlation with Pearson correlation. Further details are provided
in Appendix D.
Portfolio-level evaluation.A mining system delivers a pool of signals, whereas investment deci-
sions require a single portfolio-level ranking. FactorBench therefore evaluates the full translation
from a delivered pool to an implementable strategy. Under a unified selection rule, it first chooses
valid factors using predictive evidence available before the test period, while limiting redundancy
among the retained signals. From the selected pool, FactorBench constructs composites using equal
weighting, validation-IC weighting, and a regularized ridge regression that learns factor weights
on training data with its regularization chosen on validation data. The composite is evaluated first
before portfolio construction, allowing us to separate the information preserved through factor se-
lection and combination from the economic performance that remains after portfolio constraints,
turnover, and trading costs. To assess whether downstream value depends on investment constraints,
FactorBench applies each composite to both long-only and dollar-neutral long–short portfolios. The
long-only setting measures active stock-selection value relative to an equal-weight benchmark that
holds every eligible stock in the market. The long–short setting holds equal dollar exposure in the
top-ranked and bottom-ranked stocks, reducing the contribution of broad market movements and
testing whether the composite distinguishes relative winners from relative losers. Comparing the
two reveals whether a signal improves a fully invested portfolio and whether its cross-sectional value
remains when market direction is substantially reduced. Finally, FactorBench conducts sensitivity
analysis under varying combination method, rebalancing frequency, and transaction costs, which
identifies what design choices preserve or erode portfolio value. More details are in Appendix E.
4 EXPERIMENTALEVALUATION
Following the benchmark hierarchy, we first evaluate individual factors, then the distinctness of
delivered pools, and finally whether the resulting composite signals produce after-cost portfolio
value; additional market and configuration related results are reported in the appendix.
4.1 FACTOR-LEVELEVALUATION
Can mining systems reliably deliver valid factors?Table 1 summarizes experimental coverage,
resource use, and the validity of the factors delivered by each system. All nine automated methods
complete 3-seed runs for each market on one A100 GPU, yielding 135 mining runs and 4,914 can-
didate factors. All LLM-based methods use Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026). Of these factors,
4,838 (98.5%) execute successfully and 4,698 (95.6%) satisfy the common scorability requirement
of at least 100 valid dates across each split. The Alpha101 reference includes 71 formulas and
excludes those requiring industry neutralization information that our data do not contain; all are
executable, and 70 are scorable across all five markets. Average wall-clock time ranges from 0.14
hours per run for GP to 27.90 hours for QuantaAlpha, while the number of delivered factors ranges
from 107 to 1,619. These values describe the resource footprint and output scale of the evaluated
systems. For LLM-based systems, wall-clock time also reflects external API latency and should
not be interpreted solely as computation time. Execution failures are concentrated in R&D-Agent,
AlphaAgent, and QuantaAlpha, whereas several methods with perfect execution still deliver factors
that cannot be scored reliably. More details can be found in Appendix C.1.
Do discovered factors generalize over time?Figure 2 compares the predictive performance of
the same scorable factors across temporal splits. GP, RL, and generative methods frequently attain
substantially higher IC during training, but much of this advantage disappears by validation and
generally does not recover in testing. LLM-based methods start from lower training IC and con-
sequently exhibit smaller absolute declines, but their held-out IC is not consistently higher. Their
narrower train-to-test gap therefore reflects less in-sample performance to lose rather than stronger
out-of-sample predictiveness.
This contrast reflects differences in how predictive feedback guides search. GP, RL, and generative
methods repeatedly optimize training-period IC or related objectives, producing stronger in-sample
fit but greater exposure to adaptive overfitting, while LLM agents use IC-related feedback through
limited, multi-objective proposal-and-revision cycles. Temporal non-stationarity and factor decay

4.2 POOL-LEVELEVALUATION
Figure 5 first shows that nominal pool size does not necessarily represent effective signal breadth.
AlphaGen and AlphaQCM exhibit the greatest within-pool redundancy, with GP also producing
highly correlated pools in several markets. Other methods generally deliver more internally diverse
collections, although the results remain market-dependent: AlphaSAGE, for example, is highly re-
dundant in CSI 300 but substantially more diverse elsewhere.
The between-method panel reveals a broader family structure. Non-LLM methods tend to resemble
other non-LLM methods, while the three LLM-based systems are more similar to one another than
to most symbolic search methods. The strongest overlap occurs between AlphaGen and AlphaQCM,
consistent with their related expression spaces, RL environments, and IC-based objectives. Simi-
larity among the LLM methods may instead reflect their shared foundation model and common
financial-language priors. These results suggest that a method’s representation and search mecha-
nism shape the region of factor space it explores.
The Alpha101 comparison further shows that LLM-generated pools are typically closer to published
classical factors than those produced by non-LLM search. The pattern is strongest for R&D-Agent
and is also visible for AlphaAgent and QuantaAlpha. This is consistent with pretrained models
favoring established financial concepts and familiar trading heuristics. Overall, distinctness does
not improve monotonically across mining paradigms: newer methods may generate internally di-
verse pools while still remaining close to familiar factor families. More results can be found in
Appendix D.

4.3 PORTFOLIO-LEVELEVALUATION
The preceding evaluations examine whether mined factors remain predictive, distinct, and informa-
tive beyond measured exposures. We now ask whether this information survives a unified factor se-
lection, signal combination, portfolio construction, and backtesting with trading costs. Using train-
ing and validation information, FactorBench removes highly correlated candidates and combines
the selected factors into a composite signal. The results reported in main text use ridge regression, a
20-day rebalancing interval, and a cost of 10 basis points per dollar traded. We report the composite
factor’s IC, RankIC, ICIR and residual IC neutralized against same styles introduced in Section 4.1.
Table 2 follows each composite from test-period predictiveness to long-only and long–short portfo-
lio performance. The long-only portfolios all produce positive average net returns, with annualized
returns between 22.4% and 31.2% and Sharpe ratios between 1.03 and 1.31. R&D-Agent obtains
the highest net return, while AlphaQCM obtains the highest Sharpe ratio and lowest turnover. Al-

pha101 remains competitive with the mined factor pools. The long–short results provide a more
discriminating test by reducing the contribution of broad market movements. Returns are substan-
tially smaller and differences across methods become more visible. R&D-Agent and AlphaQCM
obtain the highest Sharpe ratios, while AlphaAgent produces a slightly negative Sharpe ratio.
The composite diagnostics help explain why portfolio returns alone provide an incomplete account
of progress. Although most composites have positive raw IC, their RankIC values are generally
small and are negative for several methods, indicating limited monotonic ordering of the stock
cross-section. Predictive performance also declines markedly after controlling for measured ex-
posures. For example, R&D-Agent has the highest raw composite IC, but its residual IC falls from
0.0253 to 0.0011. GP, AlphaGen, AlphaForge, AlphaSAGE and AlphaAgent have negative residual
IC despite positive raw IC. In contrast, AlphaQCM retains most of its raw IC after neutralization,
while Alpha101 is nearly unchanged. While R&D-Agent and AlphaQCM have similar raw ICs and
long–short Sharpe ratios, their residual ICs suggest different sources of performance: R&D-Agent’s
raw predictiveness is largely associated with the measured exposures, whereas AlphaQCM retains
predictive information beyond them and keeps lowest turnover. More generally, the transition from
raw IC to residual IC and then to after-cost portfolio performance does not produce a consistent
ordering across methods or mining paradigms. The experiments therefore provide no evidence of
monotonic downstream improvement from genetic programming and reinforcement learning to gen-
erative methods and LLM agents. Results by market and seed, along with sensitivity to transaction
costs, rebalancing frequency, and combination rule are reported in Appendix E.
5 DISCUSSION ANDLIMITATIONS
What do our results imply about progress in factor mining?Across the three evaluation levels,
FactorBench does not identify a clear winner among factor-mining paradigms. At the factor level,
methods differ in their ability to deliver valid signals, and stronger discovery-period performance
does not consistently survive temporal evaluation or adjustment for measured exposures. At the
pool level, larger collections do not necessarily provide greater effective signal breadth, and dis-
tinctness does not improve monotonically across paradigms. At the portfolio level, broadly positive
long-only performance contrasts with weaker long–short outcomes, and method rankings do not
align consistently across predictive quality, exposure-neutralized predictiveness, and portfolio per-
formance. Newer search paradigms therefore exhibit strengths on particular dimensions, but none
consistently dominates across the full evaluation chain.
How far do these conclusions generalize?Our conclusions apply to the evaluated implementa-
tions under FactorBench’s shared task rather than to the fundamental capabilities of their broader
paradigms. The evidence covers five large-cap equity markets, one daily 20-day prediction target,
one principal chronological split, and three random seeds per automated method. Because methods
retain their native discovery processes, differences in runtime and output scale do not constitute a
controlled comparison of computational efficiency. Residual IC controls only the five measured ex-
posures and should not be interpreted as pure alpha, while the absence of industry classifications
limits both neutralization and Alpha101 coverage. Finally, the portfolio tests use static universes
and simplified cost and borrowing assumptions without fully modeling survivorship, capacity, mar-
ket impact, or borrow availability. The results therefore support the conclusion that no paradigm
consistently dominates within this benchmark, rather than a universal ranking.
6 CONCLUSIONS
We introduced FactorBench, a portfolio-aware benchmark that evaluates approximately 5,000 fac-
tors produced by nine automated mining methods across five equity markets. FactorBench pre-
serves each method’s native discovery process while placing its outputs under a shared data, target,
temporal-split, and execution contract. Its three-level evaluation follows the delivered factors from
validity and temporal generalization, through exposure-neutralized predictiveness and pool distinct-
ness, to long-only and long–short portfolio performance after trading costs.
The results do not identify a clear winner among genetic programming, reinforcement-learning,
generative, and LLM-based approaches. Stronger discovery-period performance frequently deteri-

orates out of sample; larger factor pools do not necessarily contain broader information; and much
of the composite predictiveness diminishes after measured exposures are removed. Broadly posi-
tive long-only outcomes also do not translate into a consistent advantage in the long–short setting.
These findings show that progress in factor mining is multidimensional and cannot be established by
a search objective or isolated predictive score alone. Future work can extend FactorBench through
rolling temporal evaluations, point-in-time investment universes, richer exposure and market-impact
models, and controlled studies of how factor quality changes with model and search compute.

B METHODIMPLEMENTATION
B.1 COMMONSETUP ANDIMPLEMENTATIONSUMMARY
Shared experimental setup.We evaluate the nine automated mining methods under the same market
panels, input fields, 20-day forward-return target, and chronological partition: 2017–2021 for training, 2022–
2023 for validation, and January 2024–October 2025 for testing. Each method is run independently on each
of the five markets with three repetitions, producing 15 runs per method (135 in total). Hyperparameters and
search budgets are held fixed across markets. For stochastic non-LLM methods, the repetitions use random
seeds 0, 1, and 2. For LLM-based methods, these identifiers denote independent runs but do not ensure exact
reproducibility because model generation and API execution remain nondeterministic.
Methods retain their native search algorithms, objectives, expression spaces, and pool-construction procedures.
FactorBench replaces method-specific data loading with adapters to the shared market panels and maps each
method’s prediction target to the benchmark target. Training data may be used for search and parameter es-
timation, while validation data may be used when required by a method’s native feedback or model-selection
procedure. Reported test outcomes are not used for factor acceptance, pool selection, hyperparameter tuning,
or downstream composite construction.
Implementation provenance.We use the authors’ released implementations of AlphaGen, AlphaCFG,
AlphaQCM, AlphaForge, AlphaSAGE, R&D-Agent, AlphaAgent, and QuantaAlpha. Each implementation
is vendored at a fixed upstream commit, and the corresponding FactorBench wrapper records the repository,
commit, configuration, search budget, runtime, and delivered factors for every run. GP follows the baseline
implementation released with AlphaGen. Alpha101 is evaluated as a fixed reference library rather than as
a mining method. Exact operator spaces, hyperparameters, and adaptations are documented in the method-
specific descriptions below.
Table 3 summarizes the principal configuration used for each method. The reported budgets apply to one
market–seed run. They preserve the scale specified by the released implementation or paper where feasible;
consequently, they are not intended to equalize the number of candidate evaluations, generated factors, or units
of compute across paradigms.
LLM configuration.R&D-Agent, AlphaAgent, and QuantaAlpha use the same Qwen3.8-27B model end-
point, so differences among them arise from their agent workflows, prompts, feedback mechanisms, and search
budgets rather than from the underlying language model. The methods retain their native prompting and reason-
ing structures. R&D-Agent generates executable Python factors, whereas AlphaAgent and QuantaAlpha gen-
erate expressions in their respective domain-specific languages. Model and endpoint settings are fixed across
markets, although external API latency and nondeterministic generation affect runtime and exact reproducibil-
ity. For AlphaAgent and QuantaAlpha, IC-based feedback is computed against the common 20-trading-day
forward return. Their native one-day portfolio metrics are retained as additional feedback signals, while the
original prompts refer more generally to predicting returns over the next several days.
Common output contract.Every run is serialized with its method, market, repetition identifier, temporal
split, target, search budget, runtime, provenance, and complete set of delivered factors. Symbolic factors retain
both their expression and the grammar needed for execution; code-based factors retain their Python source and
entry point. Method-specific adapters convert both representations into a date-by-asset signal under the shared
execution interface. A delivered factor is marked executable when this procedure produces a valid output and

B.2 METHOD-SPECIFICIMPLEMENTATIONS
This subsection records the implementation choices that distinguish the evaluated methods. Unless stated
otherwise, the released source code is vendored without modification, and FactorBench supplies an external
wrapper for loading the shared market panels, recording run metadata, and exporting the delivered factors.
Changes that alter the method’s target, feedback, or stopping rule are stated explicitly.
Alpha101.Alpha101 serves as a fixed reference library rather than an automated mining method. We im-
plement the published formula definitions and evaluate every applicable formula on each market under the
same execution and scoring contract as the mined factors. Because the benchmark data do not contain industry
classifications, formulas requiring industry neutralization are excluded, leaving 71 of the original 101 formulas.
The implementation raises execution failures rather than silently removing formulas. For rolling correlations,
the required number of observations is bounded by the requested window length, and undefined correlations
are replaced by zero, following the implementation used to construct this reference library. Alpha101 involves
no search, model fitting, or random seed; each formula identifier is dispatched to its fixed implementation.
Genetic programming.The GP baseline is based on the implementation released with AlphaGen and
searches directly over AlphaGen expressions. Programs are constructed from the six price-and-volume fields
{open,high,low,close,volume,vwap}, constants, arithmetic operations, cross-sectional transforma-
tions, and rolling time-series operators. Fitness is the mean training-period IC with the shared 20-day forward
return. We use a population of 1,000 programs for 40 generations, tournament size 600, initial tree depths
between 2 and 6, crossover probability 0.3, subtree-mutation probability 0.1, hoist-mutation probability 0.01,

and point-mutation probability 0.1. Expressions are limited to 20 operator nodes. The delivered pool contains
at most 20 high-IC expressions subject to a pairwise-correlation threshold of 0.7.
The original GP implementation can assign fitness to expressions that cannot subsequently be parsed or that
yield effectively constant signals. Before export, we therefore require every candidate to contain an input field,
pass a round trip through the shared AlphaGen parser, and exhibit cross-sectional dispersion on at least 95% of
training dates. These checks use training data only and are treated as output-validity requirements rather than
additional predictive selection criteria.
AlphaGen.We use the official AlphaGen implementation from Yu et al. (2023). AlphaGen formulates
expression construction as a masked sequential decision problem and trains a PPO policy to generate valid
expressions. The policy uses a two-layer LSTM feature extractor with hidden dimension 128 and dropout 0.1.
PPO is trained for 200,000 environment steps with discount factor 1, entropy coefficient 0.01, and batch size
128. Candidate factors are inserted into an MSE-based linear alpha pool of capacity 10, and the reward reflects
the improvement in the training-period pool objective. The official expression grammar, masking rules, pool
update, and optimization procedure are retained.
FactorBench replaces the original qlib data loader with an adapter to the shared panels. The method’s native
target,close t+20 /close t −1, is identical to the benchmark target and therefore requires no substitution. Pool
updates and factor generation depend on training data. Split-level diagnostics produced by the upstream logging
callback do not alter the policy, reward, or exported pool.
AlphaCFG.We use the official AlphaCFG implementation from Yang et al. (2026). The evaluated con-
figuration is the paper’scfg-sem-kvariant in pool mode with a TreeLSTM policy and value network. Its
context-free grammar restricts the search to syntactically and semantically valid expressions, while Monte Carlo
tree search constructs expressions using guidance learned from self-play. We use a maximum expression length
of 10, a pool capacity of 10, 200 training iterations, 50 self-play games per iteration, and 64 MCTS simulations
per action.
FactorBench invokes the released experiment entry point with the common temporal splits and 20-day predic-
tion horizon. The qlib-backed stock-data class is rebound to the shared panel adapter; the grammar, tree search,
policy/value training, and pool construction are unchanged. The final pool is read from the last recorded pool
snapshot and exported using the shared AlphaGen-compatible expression grammar.
AlphaQCM.We use the official AlphaQCM implementation from Zhu & Zhu (2025). Among its distribu-
tional RL variants, we evaluate the implicit quantile network (IQN) agent with the released configuration: 2
million environment steps, batch size 128, 64 sampled quantiles, prioritized experience replay, discount factor
1, pool capacity 10, andλ std = 1. The agent operates over the method’s AlphaGen-derived expression space
and estimates the distribution of the pool-level reward rather than only its expectation.
The shared data adapter replaces qlib input while preserving the official target and pool construction. Following
the released model-selection rule, we export the pool snapshot with the best validation IC, falling back to
the final pool if no validation-best snapshot is available. We also guard two rare numerical failures in the
released implementation: a singular quantile-moment calculation and a boundary error in prioritized replay
sampling. In both cases, the wrapper retries the stochastic draw; computations that succeed under the original
implementation are unchanged.
AlphaForge.We use the official AlphaForge implementation from Shi et al. (2025). AlphaForge alternates
between a predictor that estimates candidate quality and a generator trained to produce promising and diverse
expressions. Expressions contain at most 20 tokens. The generator and predictor are trained for at most 200
and 100 epochs per round, respectively. Candidate admission uses an absolute-IC threshold of 0.03, an ICIR
threshold of 0.1, and a correlation threshold of 0.7. The target factor-zoo size is 100.
Two adaptations affect the research configuration. First, the original delayed VW AP-to-VW AP target is re-
placed by the common 20-day close-to-close return so that AlphaForge optimizes the quantity on which every
method is evaluated. Second, the released mining loop has no stopping condition when the zoo cannot reach
its target size. We impose a maximum of 50 rounds and export the factors available when this limit is reached.
FactorBench evaluates the stage-one factor zoo; AlphaForge’s native dynamic combiner is not used because all
methods are passed to the benchmark’s common signal-combination procedures. Because AlphaForge uses its
own fork of the expression grammar, its factors are tagged with a separate grammar and evaluated in an isolated
process.

trajectory-balance GFlowNet. Its dense reward combines predictive quality, a self-supervised structural signal,
and novelty relative to the current pool. We run 10,000 sampling episodes with pool capacity 50, optimization

This section supplements the factor-level results in the main paper with run-level reliability diagnostics, alter-

All analyses use the same delivered factors, temporal splits, and training-period signal orientation as the main

C.1 FACTORVALIDITY
The aggregate results in Table 1 describe the average resource footprint and total output of each method. A
deliveredfactor is any output returned by a mining system. Anexecutablefactor can be parsed or compiled
and produces a date-by-asset signal under the shared execution contract. Ascorablefactor additionally yields
a finite IC on at least 100 dates in each of the training, validation and testing period. Requiring sufficient
coverage in all three periods defines a common factor cohort for paired temporal comparisons: the same factors
are evaluated in every split, preventing apparent changes in aggregate performance from being driven by factors
entering or leaving the sample because of missing, constant, or numerically degenerate outputs.
Validity funnel and failure modes.Across the nine automated methods, FactorBench records 4,914
delivered factor instances. Of these, 4,838 (98.5%) execute successfully and 4,698 (95.6%) satisfy the common
scorability requirement. Alpha101 contributes 71 unique applicable formulas; all execute successfully, and 70
are scorable across all five markets. Table 4 reports these unique formulas rather than counting their market-
specific evaluations separately.
We divide attrition after delivery into three mutually exclusive categories. Anexecution failureis a factor that
fails during parsing, compilation, or execution and never produces a usable signal. AnIC-incompletefactor
executes but fails to produce a finite IC in at least one temporal split, most commonly because its cross-section
is constant or numerically degenerate. Aninsufficient-historyfactor produces an IC in all three splits but has
fewer than 100 valid dates in at least one split. The remaining factors form the scorable sample used by the
paired temporal analysis. The failure composition differs across method families. All 76 execution failures
occur in the LLM-based pipelines: 19 for R&D-Agent, 24 for AlphaAgent, and 33 for QuantaAlpha. By
contrast, the principal source of attrition for several symbolic search methods is numerical degeneracy after
successful execution. AlphaCFG, AlphaForge, and AlphaSAGE produce 11, 25, and 47 IC-incomplete factors,
respectively. QuantaAlpha exhibits both failure types, with 33 execution failures and 27 executable factors that
do not yield an IC in every split.

rather than as preprocessing details. Removing failed factors before reporting results would hide the execution

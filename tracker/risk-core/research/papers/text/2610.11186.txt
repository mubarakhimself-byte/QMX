Abstract
E-values provide a flexible framework for statistical inference, but the univer-
sal threshold 1/α can be conservative when the null distribution has additional
structure. We study how structural restrictions limit the worst-case concentration
underlying Markov’s inequality, using a nondecreasing density model to constrain
tail allocation and an L-Lipschitz density model to control local concentration. The
nondecreasing model gives to the minimax rejection threshold, and the Lipschitz
condition yields a sharper closed-form threshold with an O(L−1/2) relative im-
provement over 1/α. We further show that this threshold is asymptotically sharp
as α → 0, in the sense that no uniformly valid threshold can improve on it by a
fixed positive L-dependent amount. We then incorporate the Lipschitz calibration
into multiple testing procedures with FDR control and use the same structural in-
formation to construct calibrated conditional e-values for anytime-valid sequential
inference. Numerical experiments illustrate the resulting gains in discoveries and
the trade-offs of the sequential procedures.
1 Introduction
An e-value is a nonnegative random variable E satisfying E0[E] ≤ 1 under the null hypothesis;
large values indicate evidence against the null (V ovk and Wang, 2021). This expectation-based
criterion provides a framework for constructing and combining statistical evidence on a common
scale. E-values arise in likelihood-ratio and Bayes-factor testing (Shafer et al., 2011) and have
also been studied for composite hypothesis testing (Zhang et al., 2024). Their use extends from
individual hypotheses to collections of decisions: e-BH controls the false discovery rate (FDR) under
arbitrary dependence (Wang and Ramdas, 2022), and e-value-based methods provide confidence
regions and bounds on false discoveries (V ovk and Wang, 2023). Applications include online multiple
testing (Xu and Ramdas, 2024) and knockoff-based variable selection (Ren and Barber, 2024). When
observations arrive sequentially, conditional e-values and e-processes provide evidence that remains
valid under optional stopping and continuation (Grünwald et al., 2024; Ramdas et al., 2023), with
nonnegative supermartingales supplying time-uniform error guarantees (Howard et al., 2020). In these
settings, the evidence can be used as data accumulate, without fixing the stopping time in advance.
Across these uses, statistical decisions depend on how the evidence is calibrated against a rejection
threshold. A central issue in applications of e-values is that the universal rejection threshold 1/α may

This naturally motivates us to ask:
How much can additional structural information sharpen the rejection threshold?
To answer this question, we focus on two features underlying the Markov worst case: placing tail
mass near the rejection threshold and concentrating probability mass in sharp local spikes. These two
features suggest two complementary ways of restricting the null distribution. More specifically, a
nondecreasing density constrains how tail mass can be distributed, whereas an L-Lipschitz density
limits how sharply probability mass can concentrate locally. The resulting thresholds show how these
two forms of structure lead to different degrees of improvement over the Markov calibration.
We further extend the Lipschitz calibration to multiple testing and sequential inference. In multiple
testing, the improved threshold is incorporated into e-BH procedures with FDR control. In sequential
inference, Lipschitz information is used to calibrate a new conditional e-value sequence whose product

retrospective study of S&P 500 constituents examines the behavior of the sequential construction and
its detection trade-offs. To summarize, we make the following contributions:

1.1 Related work
E-values have been developed as a general measure of statistical evidence, with close connections to
likelihood ratios, Bayes factors, test martingales, and game-theoretic statistics (Shafer et al., 2011;
V ovk and Wang, 2021; Ramdas et al., 2023). Recent work has further studied the construction and
power of e-values for composite hypotheses (Zhang et al., 2024), as well as broader frameworks for
safe and anytime-valid inference based on e-values and e-statistics (Grünwald et al., 2024; Pérez-Ortiz
et al., 2024). These developments provide the theoretical background for the use of e-values in both
fixed-sample and sequential inference.
In fixed-sample multiple testing, e-BH and related procedures establish FDR control under broad
dependence conditions (Wang and Ramdas, 2022), extending the classical Benjamini–Hochberg
framework (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001). Related developments
include stepwise, closed-testing, and post hoc procedures (Holm, 1979; Simes, 1986; Shaffer, 1986;
Marcus et al., 1976; Blanchard and Roquain, 2008; Blanchard et al., 2020). Subsequent work has
developed broader confidence and discovery procedures based on e-values (V ovk and Wang, 2023),
2
online multiple testing (Xu and Ramdas, 2024), and knockoff-based variable selection (Ren and
Barber, 2024). Power improvements for e-BH have also been studied through randomization and
conditional calibration (Xu and Ramdas, 2026; Lee and Ren, 2024). Our fixed-sample results sharpen
the calibration of e-value rejection thresholds using additional regularity of the null distribution.
The sequential setting requires additional care. Nonnegative supermartingales provide a classical
route to anytime-valid inference, with Ville’s inequality controlling their running maxima (Ville, 1939;
Howard et al., 2020). Modern work connects these martingale ideas to e-processes and anytime-valid
inference (Ramdas et al., 2020, 2023), and develops safe testing, powerful e-statistics, and methods
for combining sequential e-values (Grünwald et al., 2024; Pérez-Ortiz et al., 2024; V ovk and Wang,
2024). Randomized Ville calibration retains the standard boundary during monitoring and adds an
independent randomized check at the terminal stopping time (Ramdas and Manole, 2026). In our
sequential construction, Lipschitz regularity is used to construct new conditional e-value sequence
whose product forms a valid supermartingale.
Most directly related, Blier-Wong and Wang (2024) obtain approximately factor-two small-α thresh-
old reductions for decreasing or unimodal densities and also study boosting e-values for e-BH under
additional distributional assumptions. Motivated by the Markov worst case, we study a nondecreasing
density to restrict tail allocation and a Lipschitz density to control local concentration. This use
of Lipschitz regularity follows the standard nonparametric idea of describing a density through a
smoothness class (Tsybakov, 2009).

3 Rejection Thresholds under Structural Constraints
Markov’s inequality uses only the first-moment bound E[E] ≤ 1. Its extremal distribution places all
tail mass exactly at the threshold and the remaining mass at zero; the mean then equals the threshold
multiplied by the tail probability. The two structural classes in Section 2 restrict different parts of this
construction. For Eb, nondecreasingness forces tail mass to extend towards the support bound. For
EL, Lipschitz regularity forces mass near zero to occupy an interval of positive width. We develop
the two calibrations separately: the first admits an exact minimax characterization, while the second
gives an explicit threshold with additive asymptotic sharpness.
3.1 Thresholds under Nondecreasing Densities

support, and how far it can be reduced while keeping the rejection probability at most α for every
null distribution in the class.

4 Applications of the Lipschitz Calibration
The Lipschitz threshold refines the Markov calibration for structured e-values (V ovk and Wang, 2021).
We show that the same structural calibration enters both multiple testing and sequential inference: in
multiple testing, it gives a uniformly sharper replacement for the e-BH threshold (Wang and Ramdas,
2022); in sequential inference, it yields an explicit update interface for constructing anytime-valid
processes (Ville, 1939; Howard et al., 2020; Ramdas et al., 2023).
4.1 Multiple Testing
We first incorporate the Lipschitz threshold into the standard step-up framework (Benjamini and
Hochberg, 1995). Since e-BH rejects by comparing ordered e-values to Markov-based thresh-
olds (Wang and Ramdas, 2022), a natural modification is to replace those thresholds by their
Lipschitz-calibrated counterparts. The resulting procedure denoted e-LC rejects the hypotheses
corresponding to the R largest e-values, with R defined in Eq. (1) and tk ≜ T (L)kα/K. The next
proposition establishes FDR control when the inputs are independent.

5.2 Sequential and Anytime-Valid Inference
We then investigate Lipschitz calibration in sequential testing via a controlled Gaussian mechanistic
demonstration and a retrospective S&P 500 analysis. The Gaussian demonstration uses a single
shared likelihood-ratio e-value path Et = exp(θXt − θ2/2) with θ = 0.3. The weak-signal phase
is constructed so that Et < τL for every LC calibration, while the common strong-signal phase
is constructed so that Et > 24/23. All methods use the same realized inputs in both phases. We

LC calibration. The S&P 500 experiment uses adjusted-close prices from January 2017 to June
2026, where each stock forms a sequential path and consecutive selected prices define the ratio inputs
Ebasek,j = Pk,j /Pk,j−1. For each path, we estimate a working Lipschitz scale from a fitted lognormal
density and evaluate the same scale multipliers. The stock experiment is retrospective because the
conditional e-value and conditional density assumptions are not established by the historical data.
Selective LC calibration can convert stronger late evidence into earlier Ville-boundary crossings. To
preserve the null-expectation constraint, LC suppresses weak observations through (Et − τ )+ and
uses the calibrated scaling ηL(τ ) to place more weight on sufficiently strong observations. Under our
choice τ = 0.5/

while observations above it lie in the LC amplification regime. The resulting factor of Section 4.2
therefore remains valid while allowing larger multiplicative increments when the observed evidence
is strong. As shown in the upper panel of Section 5.2, the weak-signal phase is constructed such
that Et < τk for all LC calibrations, so (Et − τk)+ = 0 and all LC variants use the common factor

the monitoring horizon, which can translate into a higher rejection rate. Consistently, Section 5.2
shows higher rejection rates for all LC variants than vanilla.
It should be noted that although Section 5.1 and Section 5.2 both show that gains from Lipschitz
calibration can translate into more rejections or detections, the two setting, however, use Lipschitz
information differently and therefore the source of the gains are different. In Section 5.1, the gain
comes from a lower rejection threshold established in Theorem 3.3. Since the same e-values are
compared against a smaller threshold than in standard e-BH, more hypotheses can satisfy the rejection
condition and be discovered. Furthermore, the relative threshold reduction is of order O(L−1/2), so a
smaller L allows a larger reduction and potentially larger discovery gains. In Section 5.2, the gain
comes from the multiplicative update applied to each evidence term. The vanilla update uses the
factor (1 − λ) +λE, while the LC update consider (1 − λ) +ληL(τ )(E − τ )+. Under the conditions
of Theorem 4.4 and with both processes compared against the same Ville boundary, this calibrated
update preserves the supermartingale validity, while ηL(τ )(E − τ )+ can exceed the vanilla term
E for sufficiently strong observations. The cumulative LC process can therefore grow faster along
favorable paths and cross the same Ville boundary more often, which provides a mechanism for the
higher rejection rates observed in Section 5.2.
6 Discussion
This work starts from the worst-case construction behind Markov’s inequality and asks how much
the rejection threshold can improve once different parts of that construction are restricted. A
nondecreasing density limits how tail mass can be placed and gives an exact threshold characterization.
A Lipschitz density limits sharp local concentration and yields an explicit improvement with small-α
asymptotic sharpness. We further incorporate Lipschitz information into both e-BH procedures and
anytime-valid sequential inference. These results show how modest distributional information can be
translated into sharper and more broadly useful e-value procedures.
Future work includes developing valid data-driven upper bounds for L from empirical or prior
information, beyond settings where L can be derived analytically. Another direction is to develop
practical guidelines for choosing sequential strategies under different data settings, signal patterns,
and inferential goals.

Null Crossing Probabilities. For each of the 18 batch sizes b from 1 to 1000 shown in Figure 4b,
we simulate 5,000 paths with a common budget of 36,000 raw observations, giving 36,000/b up-
dates. We estimate Pr(max0≤t≤36,000/b Mt ≥ 1/α) at α ∈ {0.1, 0.05, 0.01}. In Figure 4b, LC
crossing estimates stay below their nominal levels; bands are pointwise 95% Wilson intervals across
independent paths, not simultaneous confidence bands. This finite-horizon check complements the
conditional-model argument, which supplies the anytime-valid guarantee. The expectation of a
running maximum need not be at most one, even for a valid nonnegative supermartingale, so it is not
used as a validity criterion.
Stopping Samples and Censoring. For b = 1, generate Xt ∼ N(µ, 1) at 200 equally spaced
means µ ∈ [0, 2], retaining θ = 0 .1. Each mean uses 1,000 paired EP/LC paths, α = 0 .1, and
horizon H = 50,000 observations. We record first crossings and treat paths without a crossing by H

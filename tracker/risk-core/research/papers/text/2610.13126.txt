Abstract
This paper studies exit timing for SPX short-straddle portfolios us-
ing a two-stage supervised and reinforcement-learning framework.
Historical episodes first generate ex-post optimal hold/exit labels for
training a multilayer perceptron (MLP) using only contemporane-
ously available state variables at deployment. The trained MLP then
initializes reinforcement-learning actors that are fine-tuned with
a risk-aware trading reward. Out-of-sample evaluation over 2024–
2026 shows that the supervised MLP substantially outperforms
passive holding and representative fixed-time and profit-and-loss-
based exit rules. Across seven independent seeds, MLP-initialized
Actor–Critic averages 37.54% cumulative return and a Sharpe ratio
of 1.705, compared with 35.01% and 1.651 for the supervised MLP.
Actor–Critic trained from scratch averages only 0.18% return. These
findings show that oracle-guided supervised learning provides a
stable foundation for reward-based policy fine-tuning in a sparse
and path-dependent option-exit problem.

1 Introduction
Short-volatility strategies seek to earn the premium embedded
in option prices but remain exposed to large market moves and
volatility expansions. A short straddle, which sells a call and put
with the same strike and expiration, is a canonical example linked to
the variance risk premium [4, 7]. Its asymmetric loss profile makes
the timing of position closure central to practical performance.
This paper studies when an existing SPX short straddle should
be closed before expiration. At each decision time, a policy observes
contemporaneous market, quote, and position variables and chooses
whether to hold or exit. We propose a two-stage framework. First,
ex-post optimal exits from historical training episodes generate
labels for a supervised multilayer perceptron (MLP). Second, the
trained MLP initializes reinforcement-learning actors that are fine-
tuned using a risk-aware trading reward. Future information is

All policies are evaluated on the same locked 2024–2026 out-
of-sample episodes under common bid–ask execution, commis-

This paper makes three contributions. First, it formulates SPX
short-straddle exit timing as a sequential learning problem under
realistic execution and portfolio accounting. Second, it introduces
an oracle-guided supervised policy that initializes reward-based
reinforcement-learning fine-tuning. Third, it provides multi-seed
evidence against passive, mechanical, and raw-RL controls, together
with reward-shaping, synthetic-fill, and equal-notional robustness
checks.
2 Background and Related Work
2.1 Short Straddles and Exit Timing
A straddle is an options strategy that combines a call and a put
option with the same underlying asset, strike price, and expiration
date [27]. This paper focuses on short straddles on SPX index op-
tions. In a short straddle, the trader sells both the call and the put
and receives the initial option premium. The strategy benefits when
the underlying index remains close to the strike and the option
time value decays before expiration.
For a short straddle with strike 𝐾, call premium 𝐶0, and put
premium𝑃0, the expiration profit before transaction costs can be
written as
Π𝑇 =𝐶 0+𝑃 0−|𝑆 𝑇−𝐾|.(1)
This study examines the voluntary exit timing problem for ex-
isting short-straddle positions before expiration. Since SPX index
options are European-style and cash-settled [8], terminal payoff is
determined by cash settlement rather than physical delivery. Hold-
ing too long may expose the portfolio to tail losses, while exiting
too early may sacrifice remaining premium. Exit timing is there-
fore central to converting the short-volatility premium into a more
stable trading strategy.
Simple time-based and profit-and-loss-based exits provide impor-
tant benchmarks for evaluating a learned exit policy. Fixed-horizon
exits test whether performance can be explained by shortening

dependent exit timing from the passive variance-risk premium and
from improvements that could be reproduced by a constant calendar
or profit-and-loss rule.
2.2 Variance Risk Premium and Volatility
Selling
Short-option strategies are often motivated by the variance risk
premium. In equity index markets, implied volatility in option prices
tends to exceed subsequent realized volatility, so option sellers may
earn compensation for bearing volatility and tail risk [4, 7]. A short
straddle is a natural volatility-selling strategy because it receives
premium upfront and profits when the realized movement of the
underlying index remains limited.
This premium comes with substantial downside risk. Short-
volatility strategies can generate frequent small profits but suffer
large losses when the underlying index moves sharply or volatil-
ity expands. Existing literature links option returns and volatility
risk premia to compensation for systematic volatility and down-
side risk [3, 9]. Therefore, average return alone is not sufficient for
evaluating a short-straddle strategy.
Accordingly, this paper evaluates each exit policy using portfolio
return, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown. The purpose of
exit timing is to retain the premium-harvesting benefit of short
straddles while reducing exposure to large adverse moves.
2.3 Machine Learning for Trading Decisions
Machine learning provides a natural way to model trading decisions
from market and contract-level state variables. In this paper, the
exit decision is formulated as a binary classification problem. At
each decision time𝑡, the model observes a state vector𝑠𝑡 that sum-
marizes the current short-straddle position and market condition,
and outputs the probability of closing the position:
𝑓𝜃(𝑠𝑡)=Pr(𝑎 𝑡 =exit|𝑠 𝑡).
The state vector includes variables such as moneyness, time-to-
maturity, option quote features, spread-related measures, volatility-
risk-premium signals, and position holding-time features.
The training labels are generated from ex-post optimal exit deci-
sions within historical contract episodes. For each historical short-
straddle episode, the best feasible exit time is computed using the
realized future path under the same transaction-cost and settlement
assumptions used in the backtest. States before the selected exit
time are labeled as hold, while the selected exit state and subse-
quent states are labeled as exit. This transforms a noisy sequential
decision problem into a supervised classification problem.
This construction is related to imitation learning and learning
from demonstrations, in which a policy is trained to reproduce
actions supplied by an expert or oracle [ 1, 24]. The setting here
is specifically offline oracle-guided classification: the oracle labels
a fixed historical dataset, and the MLP is not allowed to query
the oracle interactively or observe future outcomes at deployment.
Unlike interactive imitation-learning methods such as DAgger, the
proposed procedure does not aggregate new oracle labels from
states visited by the learned policy.
Future information is used only offline to construct training
labels and to calculate a non-tradable diagnostic ceiling. At test time,
the MLP observes only contemporaneously available state variables
and does not observe future prices or future option values. The
supervised MLP is a tradable policy, whereas the ex-post optimal
exit rule is neither tradable nor included as a competing deployable
strategy.
2.4 Reinforcement Learning for Trading
Reinforcement learning (RL) studies sequential decision problems
in which an agent interacts with an environment and learns from
rewards [26]. The exit-timing problem can be represented as a
Markov decision process with state 𝑠𝑡 , action 𝑎𝑡 , reward𝑟𝑡 , and
transition dynamics. In this paper, the action space is binary:
𝑎𝑡∈{hold,exit}.

over actions. The objective is to maximize expected discounted

In trading applications, RL is attractive because it can optimize se-

and path-dependent risk. However, direct RL training from random
initialization is often unstable in financial markets, where rewards
are noisy, nonstationary, and dominated by rare adverse events.
This is especially relevant for short-option strategies, which may
generate frequent small gains but occasional large losses. Therefore,
this paper uses RL primarily as a second-stage optimizer initialized
from a supervised MLP exit policy, rather than relying only on raw
RL from scratch.
3 Data and Trading Environment
3.1 Data Source and Coverage
The option data consist of licensed Cboe SPX index-option quota-
tions accessed by the research team through an institutional data
agreement held by one of the co-authors. The dataset contains 30-
minute observations from 2016 through 2026 for near-the-money
SPX options with moneyness ratios between 0.95 and 1.05 and re-
maining maturities from 0 to 21 trading days. The available fields
include call and put bid and ask quotations, strike price, expiration,
implied volatility, option Greeks, and the contemporaneous SPX
index level.

3.2 SPX Short-Straddle Episodes
The raw option panel is filtered before episode construction. Obser-
vations with non-finite bid, ask, implied-volatility, or underlying-
index values are removed. The underlying index level must be
positive, option bids must be non-negative, and observations for
which the ask is below the bid are excluded. Duplicate contract-time
observations are removed, and call and put records are matched
by expiration, strike, and quote timestamp. A straddle observation
is retained only when both option legs are available at the same
timestamp. These filters prevent non-finite, negative, or internally
inconsistent quotations from directly determining the reported
entry and exit fills.
Across the complete episode set, synthetic continuation bars ac-
count for 5.40% of observations. Their shares are 5.69% in the 2016–
2023 training period and 5.03% in the 2024–2026 out-of-sample
period. A detailed audit of episode coverage, strategy exits, and
conservative synthetic-fill assumptions is reported in Appendix A.2.
The empirical environment is constructed from daily SPX short-
straddle episodes. On each entry day, the strategy opens a short
call and a short put with the same strike price 𝐾 and expiration
date. The target maturity is approximately 20 trading days. The
contracts are SPX index options, which are European-style and
cash-settled [8]. After entry, the strike and expiration are fixed, and
the position is observed through time until it is either closed by the
trading policy or settled at expiration.
At each decision time 𝑡, the policy observes the current state
of the open position, including the underlying index level, money-
ness, remaining time-to-maturity, quote and spread information,
volatility-related signals, holding time, and unrealized profit and
loss. The available action is binary:
𝑎𝑡∈{hold,exit}.
If the policy chooses hold, the position remains open. If it chooses
exit, the short straddle is closed and the episode ends. If no exit
occurs before expiration, the position is cash-settled at terminal
intrinsic value.
3.3 Portfolio Construction
The backtest is conducted at the portfolio level rather than as iso-
lated single-contract episodes. The portfolio uses 20 reusable capi-
tal slots to represent overlapping TTM-20 short-straddle positions.
Since a new candidate trade is introduced each trading day and
each contract has an approximately 20-day target maturity, the 20
slots form a rolling portfolio structure.
Each slot can hold at most one active short-straddle position.
The same trained exit policy is applied to every active slot. When
a position exits or expires, the corresponding slot is reused for
the next entry assigned to that slot. Thus, the 20-slot structure
should be interpreted as portfolio capital allocation rather than 20
independently trained agents.

3.5 Transaction Costs and Settlement
Transaction costs and bid–ask execution are included throughout
the portfolio simulation. The implementation uses the same execu-
tion rule for all tradable strategies. When a short straddle is opened,
the strategy sells the straddle at the bid price:
𝐹 entry𝑡 =𝐵 straddle𝑡 .
Here,𝐵straddle𝑡 is the combined bid price of the call and put legs.
When an active short straddle is closed before expiration, the strat-

If a real quote is not available during a simulated continuation
segment, the exit cost is replaced by the simulated short-exit cost
used by the environment. Synthetic continuation bars are explicitly
flagged in the state and are never presented as observed market

3.6 Evaluation Metrics
All strategies are evaluated using the same out-of-sample portfo-
lio simulation. The performance panel reports cumulative return,
annualized compounded return (ARC), annualized standard devia-
tion (ASD), maximum drawdown magnitude (MD), maximum loss
duration (MLD), three reward-to-risk measures, Sharpe ratio, Cal-
mar ratio, hit rate, and number of trades. Mean episode profit and
loss is reported in the text and in the robustness tables. ARC an-
nualizes portfolio growth over the evaluation horizon, while ASD
measures annualized return volatility. MD measures the largest
peak-to-trough portfolio loss, and MLD measures the longest pe-
riod during which portfolio NAV remains below its previous high.
All NAV-based metrics are computed after mapping each portfolio
path to the common real 30-minute quote grid over the shared
out-of-sample calendar window. ARC uses the common calendar
horizon𝑇≈2.42years.
The three additional reward-to-risk measures are defined as
IR∗ = ARCASD,IR ∗∗ = ARCMD,IR ∗∗∗ = ARCMLD.
Under these definitions, IR∗∗ is numerically equal to the Calmar
ratio. Hit rate is the fraction of entered short-straddle episodes with
positive net dollar PnL, and the trade count is the number of entered
straddles. Cumulative return is retained for economic interpreta-
tion, while ARC provides the horizon-consistent annualized return
measure required for comparison with annualized risk statistics.
Maximum drawdown and loss duration are particularly relevant
for short-volatility strategies because losses may be both severe
and persistent [22, 23].
The deployable benchmark strategies include hold-to-expiry,
three representative rule-based exit policies, and raw reinforcement
learning from scratch. The rule-based policies consist of a fixed-time
exit, a profit-target and stop-loss rule, and a combined rule. The
proposed tradable models include the supervised MLP exit policy
and MLP-initialized reinforcement-learning variants. Separately,
the ex-post best-exit oracle is reported as a non-tradable diagnostic
ceiling. Because it observes each episode’s realized future path, it
is excluded from the main comparison of deployable strategies.

4.2 Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning
Fine-Tuning
The second stage refines the supervised exit policy using reinforce-
ment learning. After Stage 1, the trained MLP provides a strong
initialization for the RL actor. In other words, the same neural
network parameterization is reused as the policy network, but its
weights are no longer optimized against hindsight labels. Instead,
they are further updated to improve trading performance in the
short-straddle environment [17, 26].

5 Experimental Design
5.1 Train/Test Split
The empirical analysis uses a chronological train-test split rather
than a random split. This is important because option markets
are time ordered, and randomly mixing observations across time
could leak future market information into the training process. All
model training and parameter estimation are performed using data
before the out-of-sample evaluation period, while the final portfolio
performance is measured on a later test period.
The main out-of-sample test period is 2024–2026. Historical data
before the test period are used for supervised label construction,
MLP training, and reinforcement-learning fine-tuning. When a vali-
dation stage is required for model selection or checkpoint selection,
the data are divided into three chronological blocks: a training pe-
riod, a validation period, and a locked out-of-sample test period.
When pilot validation is used, it is restricted to pre-test data; the
2024–2026 period is held out for final evaluation and is not used
for hyperparameter or checkpoint selection.

The supervised MLP labels are constructed only within the train-
ing data. For each training episode, the ex-post best exit time is
computed using that episode’s realized path, and the resulting la-
bels are used to train the classifier. These hindsight labels are not
used during out-of-sample trading. In the test period, the trained
policy observes only the contemporaneous state𝑠𝑡 and produces a
hold or exit decision.
Future prices in the test period are used only after the decision
is made to compute realized portfolio performance.
The same principle is applied to feature standardization. The
mean and standard deviation used to normalize the MLP inputs are
estimated from the training sample only. These training-sample sta-
tistics are then applied unchanged to validation and out-of-sample
observations. This prevents the model from using information about
the distribution of future test data.
All strategies are evaluated under the same out-of-sample port-
folio simulator. The hold-to-expiry benchmark, representative rule-
based exit policies, raw RL baseline, supervised MLP policy, and
MLP-initialized RL variants are applied to the same test-period
episode pool and portfolio construction rules. This ensures that
performance differences reflect the exit policy and its resulting
capital reuse rather than differences in data, execution assumptions,
or evaluation protocol.
5.2 Evaluation Protocol and Main Comparisons
All exit strategies are evaluated using the same out-of-sample port-
folio simulator over 2024–2026. Each strategy is applied to the
same pool of SPX short-straddle episodes and uses the same daily
candidate-selection rule, initial capital, 20-slot portfolio structure,
stress-budget position sizing, bid–ask execution, commissions, mar-
gin requirements, and maintenance-liquidation rule. The compo-
nent that differs across strategies is the exit decision. Because earlier
exits release portfolio slots sooner, the resulting number of accepted
episodes and the rate of capital reuse are endogenous consequences
of each exit policy.
In the main specification, cumulative portfolio return is size-
weighted and incorporates dynamic compounding within each slot.
Under stress-budget sizing, the number of contracts assigned to a
new position is computed from the slot’s current NAV. A profitable
slot can therefore support a larger subsequent position, while losses
reduce its later position size. The reported main return consequently
reflects both exit-policy decisions and the portfolio consequences
of reinvesting gains under the common sizing rule. To separate exit-
policy performance from this dynamic compounding channel, an
equal-notional robustness specification fixes each entered position
at one short-straddle contract.
The evaluation includes five groups of exit policies. The first is a
hold-to-expiry benchmark, in which each short straddle remains
open until cash settlement. This benchmark measures the passive
short-volatility premium without discretionary exit timing.
The second group consists of three representative rule-based exit
benchmarks. The fixed-time rule closes a position after 65 intraday
bars, corresponding to approximately five trading days. The thresh-
old rule exits when profit reaches 25% of the entry premium or when
loss reaches 50% of the entry premium. The combined rule exits
when either the fixed holding horizon or one of the profit-and-loss

6.5 Reward-Shaping Ablation
To test whether the Actor–Critic ranking is an artifact of the hand-

by one reward-shaping component. The CVaR term does not gen-

that the shaping components influence the return–drawdown trade-

supervised-first policy.

7 Limitations
The 2024–2026 out-of-sample period contains no sustained volatil-
ity shock comparable to 2008 or March 2020. The observed approxi-
mately 4% drawdowns should therefore not be interpreted as upper
bounds on naked short-straddle tail risk. The simulator includes
bid–ask execution, margin constraints, maintenance liquidation,
and risk-aware training, but it cannot fully reproduce crisis-period
price gaps, volatility jumps, spread widening, liquidity shortages,
or forced deleveraging. Evaluation over longer and more severe
volatility regimes remains necessary.
The synthetic-fill sensitivity check is limited to the supervised
MLP and one AC+MLP run. It establishes that these audited paths
do not execute exits on synthetic bars, but it does not imply that
synthetic continuation states are irrelevant to every possible policy
or market regime.
The incremental AC+MLP advantage is smaller under fixed-one-
contract sizing, indicating that part of its economic magnitude in
the main stress-budget specification is associated with the timing
of gains and subsequent slot-level capital scaling. Although the av-
erage policy ordering is preserved, the equal-notional episode-level
difference between AC+MLP and the supervised MLP is not statis-
tically significant. The reported RL increment should therefore be
interpreted as an average economic improvement that is amplified
by dynamic position sizing rather than as a uniform episode-level
advantage.
8 Conclusion
This paper develops a two-stage framework for SPX short-straddle
exit timing in which an oracle-guided supervised MLP initializes
reinforcement-learning policies. The supervised policy substan-
tially outperforms passive holding and representative mechanical
exits. Across seven seeds, Actor–Critic fine-tuning provides a pos-
itive average economic improvement, although its episode-level
increment is not statistically significant. Raw Actor–Critic performs
poorly, while the reward and equal-notional checks show that the
main average ranking is not solely generated by one shaping com-
ponent or dynamic position sizing. Overall, supervised initialization
is what makes reward-based fine-tuning viable in this sparse and
path-dependent trading problem.

A.1 Environment and State Variables
The trading environment uses an exit-timing formulation for SPX
short-straddle positions. At each decision time, the policy observes
a vector of market, quote, and position-state variables. In the fi-
nal reinforcement-learning runs, the observation setting uses the
position-augmented state representation. The base state contains
time-to-maturity, time-of-day, variance-risk-premium, relative bid–
ask spread, model score, recent realized-volatility change, quote-
availability indicators, synthetic-bar indicators, simulated time-
value information, current position, and normalized running PnL.
The position-augmented representation adds holding time, entry
maturity, remaining maturity relative to entry maturity, current
exit value relative to entry value, underlying return since entry,
and moneyness drift since entry.
All observations are numerically cleaned and clipped to the inter-
val[−20, 20]. The final position-augmented state has dimension 17.
Invalid actions are remapped to the hold action. Exits are permitted
only when the quote is exitable and, except at terminal settlement,
after the minimum holding period has elapsed.

Anytime-Valid Certification of Robustness Margins
for Safe Control via E-Values
Panagiotis S. Trakas ∗, Omid Mirzaeedodangeh ∗, and Lars Lindemann
Abstract— Robust control uses the error between a known
model and the true system as a robustness margin. However,
this error is usually unknown: conservative estimates restrict
feasibility and performance, while underestimates can result in
violations of the control property. We estimate this robustness
margin from online data using e-values, a statistical tool for
hypothesis testing, in the setting of discrete-time control barrier
functions (CBFs). Here, we measure the robustness margin as
the difference between the observed CBF and the model-based
CBF prediction. We then design a robust CBF controller in
three steps by: (1) selecting a set of candidate margins, (2)
obtaining validity certificates for these candidates via sequential
tests based on e-values, and (3) updating the CBF controller
with the smallest certified candidate. The deployed tests ac-
cumulate evidence against the hypothesis that an exceedance
risk, the probability of the robustness margin exceeding the
candidate margin, remains at or above a pre-specified failure
probabilityα. Our tests are anytime valid and thus enable the
use of certified candidates in the CBF controller, guaranteeing
that the probability that the exceedance risk of a certified
margin exceedsαis at most a user-chosen confidenceδ. We
validate our approach on navigation problems across different
environments with visualizations in NVIDIA Isaac Sim.
I. INTRODUCTION
Learning-based controllers are increasingly deployed in a
variety of applications, but often lack formal safety guaran-
tees. This has motivated the use of safety filters via control
barrier functions (CBFs) [1]. However, their reliability de-
pend on the availability of an accurate model of the system.
In the presence of model mismatches, robust CBFs can still
ensure safety by tightening the CBF safety constraint via a
robustness margin, which must be larger than the model error
[2]. Unfortunately, the model error is typically unknown and
depends on the system’s operating range. Robustness margins
that are chosen too small may provide insufficient protection,
whereas unnecessarily large choices lead to conservative or
infeasible problems. This issue, and hence our contribution,
is not limited to CBFs and applies to other tools, e.g., robust
model predictive control and control Lyapunov functions.
Data-driven estimates of the model error can be obtained
in practice, but are often heuristic and approximate, resulting
again in unsafe behavior. Therefore, recent work has studied
how probabilistic safety guarantees can be obtained using
Gaussian Processes [3], [4] and conformal prediction [5].
While Gaussian Processes rely on a well-calibrated model
assumption, conformal prediction struggles in dealing with
shifts of the data generating distribution due to the closed-
loop dependence between controller and data. This issue

A certificate is now issued if sufficient evidence against
this hypothesis is obtained. Such a certificate tells us that
the exceedance risk for the corresponding candidate was
below the pre-specified failure probability at some point in
the past. To transfer this certificate into a safe control action,
we have to account for the fact that the exceedance risk may
increase in the future, which is done by adding an exceedance
buffer. The CBF controller then selects the smallest certified
candidate. Our main contributions are as follows:
1) We develop anytime-valid certificates for robustness
margins of CBF controllers using online data and
sequential tests based on e-values. We also derive
upper bounds for the expected certification time.
2) We propose a robust CBF controller that transfers cer-
tified robustness margins into safe control actions. This
transfer relies on a bound on the increase in exceedance
risk, which introduces conservatism. Therefore, we
permit parallel tests for efficient control design.
3) We compare our method against baseline algorithms
on unicycle navigation problems in corridor and maze
environments, visualized in NVIDIA Isaac Sim.
A. Related Work
Robust and stochastic safety filters account for model
uncertainty through error bounds or additional distributional
information. Predictive safety filters use model-error bounds
that can be learned from data [13], while adaptive CBFs use
state-estimation error bounds [14]. Probabilistic CBFs use
known moments or i.i.d. disturbance samples [15], while
stochastic CBFs rely on a known disturbance distribution
[16].An open challenge is to quantify error bounds and to
certify robustness margins directly from online observations.
Conformal prediction (CP) is increasingly used in control
to construct uncertainty bounds from observed prediction
errors without assuming a parametric error distribution [17].
Under i.i.d. assumptions, standard CP approaches provide
marginal coverage, so guarantees averaged over offline cal-
ibration trajectories and a test trajectory. One of the main
challenges in control, however, is dealing with non i.i.d. data.
Online conformal prediction methods adapt uncertainty
bounds in tube-based MPC [18] or switch thresholds in
learned safety filters [19]. However, these provide safety
guarantees that are only valid on average over time. For
CBF controllers, [6] provides an iterative procedure to deal
with distribution shifts by updating robustness margins using
large sets of i.i.d. calibration trajectories. Instead, we use
anytime-valid tests that require neither known disturbance
distributions, offline calibration data, nor i.i.d. observations.
Sequential hypothesis testing methods based on e-values
have been developed for change detection [20] and risk-
controlling prediction sets [21]. Conformal e-prediction pro-
vides anytime-valid coverage for i.i.d. data arriving in
batches [22]. In robotics, martingale tests detect distribution
shifts with guarantees on false alerts [23], while multiple
hypothesis testing can help identify the type of distribution
shift [24]. Sequential tests can also be used to compare robot
policies using i.i.d. evaluation trials [25]. Closest to our work
are [16], [26]–[29], which formulate safety through enforcing
a martingale condition. However, verifying the validity of
this martingale condition requires precise knowledge of the
disturbance distribution and must be performed offline. To
the best of our knowledge, no one has used anytime-valid
sequential certification of robustness margins from online
data to design safe feedback controllers.

A. Composite Null Hypotheses and E-Values
We first review the statistical background used throughout;
further information is provided in [9]–[12]. A null hypothesis
H0 is a set of probability measures on(Ω,F)satisfying
a specific event. It is called simple if it contains a single
probability measure and composite otherwise. Throughout
this section,Q∈H 0 denotes a generic probability measure
satisfying the hypothesisH 0, andE Q denotes expectation
underQ. This notation is used only in this section, the
closed-loop probability measure remainsP.
For example, letZ t ∈ {0,1}indicate whether a specific
event (e.g.,Y t ≤c t) occurs at timet. Fixα∈(0,1)and
consider the composite null hypothesis
H0 :={Q:Q(Z t = 1| Ft)≥α}, t∈N 0.(9)
This is a composite null hypothesis because many probability
measures may satisfy the inequalityQ(Z t = 1| Ft)≥α.
Under any such probability measure, the event indicated by
Zt = 1occurs with probability at leastα.
Ane-variableconverts observed data into a nonnegative
measure of evidence against the null hypothesis. Formally, a
nonnegative random variableEis an e-variable forH 0 if
EQ[E]≤1for everyQsatisfyingH 0. (10)
The realized value ofEafter the data are observed is called
ane-value. Equation (10) ensures that large e-values cannot
occur frequently whenH 0 is true, so a larger e-value provides
stronger evidence againstH 0.
To make this interpretation precise, fix an error confidence

B. Test Supermartingales
We seek to accumulate evidence as new data becomes
available. One-step e-variables can be multiplied over time
to form atest supermartingale, provided theirF t-conditional
expectation under the null hypothesis are at most one [11].
This construction allows evidence to be assessed at every
time, making it suitable for online monitoring systems (1).
A process(E t)t≥0 is adapted to the filtration(F t)t≥0
ifE t isF t-measurable, meaning that its value uses only
the information available at timet. A nonnegative adapted
process withE 0 = 1is atest supermartingaleforH 0 if, for
everyQ∈H 0, it is integrable and satisfies
EQ[Et+1 | Ft]≤E t, t∈N 0.(11)
Thus, under the null hypothesis, the conditional expected
value of the evidence cannot increase, although its observed
value may rise or fall. Taking expectations recursively gives
EQ[Et]≤1, so eachE t is an e-variable.
The key advantage is a validity guarantee over all times
t∈N 0. For anyδ∈(0,1), Ville’s inequality [31] gives

VI. NUMERICALEXAMPLES
We evaluate the safety controller (26) with repeated cer-
tification in two robot-navigation scenarios: a corridor with
cones (Corridor) and a maze with walls and cones (Maze).
We assess the theoretical guarantees empirically and compare
safety and navigation performance with existing methods.
For clarity of presentation we present the Maze results in
this section, and the Corridor results in Appendix F-C.
A. Setup
We model the robot as a second-order unicycle navigating
a maze with walls and cones. The goal of the robot is to
reach a goal, using a nominal controlleru nomt , while avoiding
the obstacles. The barrier function accounts for obstacle
clearance at a fixed point ahead of the robot and its braking
distance. The robot model and environment are detailed
in Appendix F-A. The true system differs from the model
through an additive disturbancew t, which models a bounded
position displacement with a time-varying magnitude dis-
tribution. Its direction is uniformly distributed over[0,2π)
conditional on its magnitude andF t. To compute the risk-
increase bounds (30), we use this directional model, a known
magnitude bound, and a bound on changes in the conditional
disturbance distribution. The magnitude distribution itself is
unknown to the controller. We compute the risk-increase
bounds (30) using the analysis in Appendix D-B. The param-
eter values and simulation details are given in Appendix F-A.
The trajectories are simulated in Python, and the trajectories
are visualized in NVIDIA Isaac Sim. The simulation code,
data, and supplementary ablation report are available at
github.com/omidmirza/ecbf-ablation.
A setting(α 0, α, δ, K)consists of the CBF-violation prob-
abilityα 0, the margin insufficiencyα, the error confidence
δ, and the test periodK(measured in control stepst). The
tests follow Section V-B, with the deployable setD t in (31).
We compare five settings, S1-S5, with four variants of our
method at each setting, as described below. The settings and
results are reported in Table I. Additional parameter sweeps
are provided in the supplementary ablation report [36].

differ only in the risk-increase boundd (r)t (c). We derive these
bounds using the nominal dynamics, obstacle geometry, and
a bound on changes in the disturbance distribution. Recall

shape of nearby obstacles, here walls and cones. V-L instead
tightens the bound on risk changes caused by changes in the
distance between the predicted nose point of the robot and
nearby obstacles. V-C combines both refinements and also

Unbounded Losses, Unchecked Inputs: Measuring the

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents now place options or-
ders at retail brokers, and developers publish open-source
options agents faster than anyone has examined them. Prior
security work on trading agents scored academic equities de-
signs by whether an attacker can flip a buy-or-sell decision.
We present the first measurement of the options agents that
brokers and developers ship, scored by the safeguards they
enforce and by the worst loss their documented order paths
can reach. We screened 765 GitHub repositories, broker doc-
umentation, and the research literature and found 215 eligible
repositories and 8 broker-native agent interfaces. We then
scored 26 systems, including every broker interface and every
open-source project with at least 20 stars, on seven robustness
and eleven security safeguards with cell-level evidence. The
ecosystem is new: 198 of the 215 repositories were created in
2026, and 129 (60.0%) appeared in the two weeks around one
options-trading hackathon. Of the 22 systems that can place
orders, 10 enforce a pre-trade limit outside the model, yet
none of the 26 authenticate their text sources or cross-check
market data. Of the 26, 17 can execute with no mandatory
human step and 13 can reach unbounded loss, including 6 of
the 8 broker interfaces. Because an attacker who writes the
agent’s inputs can move an order’s strike, expiry, volatility,
and legs while its direction stays correct, options agents need
loss limits enforced at the order boundary.
1 Introduction
A large language model (LLM) agent can now open an options
position at a retail broker with no human touching the order.
Robinhood announced that agentic options trading is available
to all US customers [1,2], on a platform with about 27 million
funded customers [3]. The tastytrade Model Context Proto-
col (MCP) server exposes equity, index, and futures options,
including multi-leg orders, to agents with write access [4, 5],
and 7 of the 14 brokers reviewed by StockBrokers.com in
September 2026 run a first-party agent server [6]. Develop-
ers are building on these interfaces even faster. Of the 215
public options-agent repositories we found on GitHub, 198
were created in 2026 (§5.1). The market they trade is large
and short-dated, since listed options averaged 72.8 million
contracts a day in 2026 Q2, up more than 19% from a year
earlier [7], and zero-days-to-expiry (0DTE) contracts reached
a record 66.2% of S&P 500 index-options volume in July
2026 [8].
An attacker who can publish text that an options agent
reads can steer its orders without touching the model or the
account, because indirect prompt injection [9] and MCP tool
poisoning [10] let news, social posts, filings, and tool outputs
control an agent’s actions. However, the worst case on options
keeps the trade’s direction. Whereas a stock position reaches
unbounded loss through an explicit short [11], the one variable
that equities risk checks already watch, an options position
reaches it when one leg is dropped while the direction and
the named strategy stay the same. If text frames the protective
leg of a bull put spread as a drag on returns and the agent
drops it, a position that risked $400 per contract becomes
an uncovered put that can lose $9,900, about 25 times more
(Figure 2). Human-run funds have failed this way. In 2018, a
fund that sold uncovered natural-gas options told about 290
clients that their balances were gone and that they could owe
their clearing broker more, after an 18% price spike [12].
Despite this exposure, no one has measured whether the
agents in use today can stop such an order. The closest
work, the FARSIGHT systematization of Wang and Sax-
ena [13], scored 15 academic equities designs by whether
an attacker can flip a buy-or-sell decision. Agent-security
benchmarks [14–16] count unintended tool calls, and attack
studies of trading agents [17, 18] target equities and pricing
agents. Because none of them examines the options agents
that brokers and developers ship, or prices a failure by the loss
the resulting order can reach, we ask three research questions.
• RQ1 (Ecosystem).Who builds LLM options-trading
agents, and how fast is that population growing?
• RQ2 (Exposure).What can these agents do to an account
without a human, and what is the worst loss their docu-
mented order paths can reach?
• RQ3 (Safeguards).Which robustness and security safe-
guards do they enforce outside the model, and do any check
the inputs an attacker writes?

We make the following contributions:
• We give the first measurement of LLM options-trading
agents, from a logged screen of 765 GitHub repositories to
26 scored systems, and show that most of the open-source
population appeared around a single hackathon (§4, §5.1).
• We define a threat model in which the attacker keeps the
trade’s direction and moves its strike, expiry, volatility, or
legs, and derive six option-only attack primitives, includ-
ing a counterparty attack that survives a ban on uncovered
writing (§3).
• We score every system on eighteen safeguards with cell-
level evidence and show that the worst case is reachable on
broker interfaces, while no system verifies its inputs (§5.2,
§5.3).
• We derive order-boundary controls for brokers and agent
builders that work without detecting manipulated text, and
we state five open problems for researchers (§6, §7).
Because of this measurement, a broker that opens options to
agents can check its order path against three controls that work
without detecting manipulated text: defined-risk structures for
agent orders, a maximum loss computed and enforced before
entry, which 4 of the 22 order-capable systems have today,
and a pre-trade limit enforced outside the model, which 10
have.

2 Background
Options matter for agent security because an option’s loss is
no fixed multiple of its price move. A US equity option gives
its holder the right to buy (a call) or sell (a put) 100 shares of
an underlying at a fixed strike on or before an expiry date, and
the holder may also let it lapse [19]. A long stock position
pays off linearly and loses at most the capital committed.
Options break both properties.

Payoff asymmetry and defined-risk structures.The op-
tion buyer’s maximum loss is the premium paid. The seller
collects that premium and takes on the obligation, so a short
put loses at most the strike minus the premium, while a short
call written without an offsetting position, anakedcall, has
no upper bound because the underlying price has none [19].
Traders cap that exposure by pairing legs. A vertical spread
sells one option and buys a further-out-of-the-money option
of the same expiry, so its maximum loss is the strike width
minus the net credit, and a covered call or cash-secured put
writes against shares or cash already held. Thus, the bound
comes from the joint configuration of the position. When the
protective leg is dropped, or the option is written uncovered,
a defined-risk position becomes a naked one with the direc-
tional view unchanged. Equities have no such transition that
keeps the direction, and it underlies the worst case of §3.2.
Short selling, margin, and expiry.Unbounded loss exists
on equities too, since a short sale has no loss ceiling [11] and
Regulation T limits initial leverage on stock to 2:1 [21]. The
difference is where the decision sits. While a stock position
becomes unbounded when the agent chooses the short side,
the variable that equities evaluations and risk checks already
watch, an options position becomes unbounded when one leg
of a structure is dropped. Its payoff of small, frequent gains
and rare, large losses then looks better on a win-rate basis
than the protected version. An option’s value also depends
on implied volatility (IV) and on the Greeks (delta, gamma,
theta, vega), which the Black–Scholes model [20] and its
descendants derive and which neither a retail user nor an LLM
checks at a glance. Because volume splits across many strikes
and expiries, a contract trades far less than its underlying and
quotes a wider relative spread [19]. Options also carry early
exercise, assignment of a short leg, and pin risk at expiry,
which zero-days-to-expiry contracts compress into a single
session.
Five decision variables.An options agent is a different target
because its order carries more decisions. An equities order
commits to one variable an attacker can corrupt, the direction,
plus a size. An options order commits to five, namely direction
(call or put, long or short), magnitude (the strike), time (the
expiry), volatility (the IV paid or sold), and structure (the
legs). Figure 1 shows the five variables, and Table 1 lists each
difference from equities with the attack risk it adds. Rows
2–5 of the table show that every variable other than direction
can carry an attack while the direction stays correct, and rows
6–8 list the market and lifecycle properties behind axes A3,
A5 and A6. Each added variable is one the attacker can move
while the direction stays correct.
Six amplification axes.Six properties explain why the same
agent error costs more, is harder to check, or is easier to ex-
ploit on options. They are leverage and non-linear payoff
(A1), the Greeks and IV knowledge gap (A2), wide bid-ask
spreads (A3), pricing by time decay and IV (A4), exercise,
assignment, and pin (A5), and order-flow observability (A6).
Table 2 pairs each with its closest equities analogue and the
agent failure mode it acts on. None of them needs a new kind
of agent error. Instead, the same error lands on a payoff with
a lower floor (A1, A4), on a number that is harder to check
(A2), or on an order that a counterparty can see (A6). A2
deserves emphasis because implied volatility is backed out
of an observed price through a pricing model [20] and the
Greeks are partial derivatives of that model. As a result, a
human approver cannot check a claim such as “IV rank is
at the 80th percentile” against a price chart, and the agent
usually receives such numbers precomputed from a tool.

a language model with market-data tools, memory, and an
order interface in an observe–reason–act loop over retrieved
text and numbers [24]. On the broker side, most of them
connect through MCP, which places each tool’s description

actions without touching the model [10]. Since options agents
read their chain data, Greeks, and buying power through these
same tools, a poisoned field reaches the order directly.
3 Threat Model
We fix one threat model and read every system in §5 against
it. Figure 1 traces an attack from the text the attacker writes
to the loss at expiry and marks the safeguards we measure at
each step.
3.1 Attacker and Victim
Victim.The victim is an LLM agent that reads text, calls
options tools for chains, quotes, and Greeks, and holds options
order authority directly or through a human who approves
its tickets. Its account sits at a broker whose quote, fill, and
position reports are honest. However, the agent also treats
ingested text and tool-returned numbers as honest, and that
assumption is what the attacker breaks.
Attacker goal.The attacker wants the agent to send an order
whose risk differs from the order it would have sent on honest
inputs. We judge success by three measurable properties of
the final order: (i) a defined-to-undefined conversion, in which
a short option leg is left without a covering position where the
pre-attack plan had bounded loss, (ii) the maximum potential
loss at expiry, labeled bounded or unbounded, and (iii) the
premium and spread cost lost to a mis-chosen strike, expiry,
or structure. Because the direction can stay correct, a decision-
flip rate misses all three.
Knowledge.Following Kerckhoffs’s principle, the attacker
knows which public sources a system ingests, how its prompt,
tools, and order path are designed, and every documented

commentary, unusual-options-activity alerts, and flow reports,

and IV , a channel in which tool poisoning reached up to 72.8%
attack success across 20 agents [10]. Through other agents
(S3), it controls an agent whose outputs the victim acts on,
such as an analyst agent that passes a volatility view to an
execution agent. The content may be fabricated or accurate

limit, margin check, or dry-run the system has. The attacker
cannot tamper with the broker’s reports, access the victim
account, modify model weights [32], or move the underly-
ing with its own capital, because each of these belongs to a
different attacker class or to ordinary market manipulation.
Counterparty attacker.One primitive (O-6) assumes a
stronger attacker that can also post and fill orders in listed
options, such as a trader with an options account or a liq-
uidity provider. Retail options orders are mostly routed to a
few wholesalers that pay for order flow [22], which internal-
ize them through exchange price-improvement auctions and
the limit order book [33, 34]. As a result, a non-wholesaler
reaches the agent’s order when it rests on an exchange book
or enters an auction, which is most likely in thin contracts.
Profiling from public prints works where an agent with fixed
rules dominates a contract’s flow.

Appendix A groups these escalations into three patterns.
O-1: risk-structure induction.O-1 is the worst case we
consider, the one corruption whose loss has no ceiling while
the agent’s direction stays correct. Consider a bull put spread
that sells the 100-strike put and buys the 95-strike put for a
net credit of $1.00 per share, so its maximum loss is (5−
1)×100=$400 per contract (Figure 2a). If the long 95 put
is removed, the position becomes an uncovered put with a
maximum loss of (100−1)×100=$9,900 . Removing the
protective call from a bear call spread leaves no upper bound at
all. The inducing text needs no falsehood, because presenting
the protective leg as a cost that rarely pays out is true in most
expiries, and an injected ticket or a falsified maximum-loss
field reaches the same end state without persuading the model.
O-2 to O-5: loss with the direction right.Strike and expiry
displacement (O-2) moves the agent to a farther out-of-the-
money strike, a nearer expiry, or a thinner contract, so the
position expires worthless more often while it still matches
the agent’s own analysis. Greeks and volatility misstatement
(O-3) targets the numbers that decide whether an option is
cheap, which few supervisors can check (A2). As a result,
the agent overpays for volatility that collapses after the event
(Figure 2b) or sizes a short position on an understated delta.
Expiry-pressure triggering (O-4) uses urgency or compla-
cency about time decay, which speeds up near expiry [19],
to make the agent chase, roll at a loss, or hold a decaying
position. Exercise and assignment exploitation (O-5) induces
an early exercise that forfeits time value, or leads the agent
to ignore early-assignment and pin risk on a short leg, which
can leave an unplanned position of 100 shares per contract.
O-6: profile and harvest.O-6 pays the attacker directly and
needs no uncovered leg. When a fixed-rule agent dominates a
thin contract’s flow, its displayed orders and the consolidated
prints [23] reveal its entry conditions, strikes, and timing (A6).
The counterparty attacker times an input from any precursor
class so that the agent enters the contract, and then fills the
order on the other side, collecting the spread and the induced
mispricing. Because each trade is small and ordinary, the
attack repeats.
Reachability.The broker’s write policy decides which primi-
tives an agent can reach. On a platform that forbids uncovered
writing, such as Robinhood’s documented order tool or Al-
paca (§2), O-1 is out of reach, O-2 to O-4 are bounded by the
premium or by the assignment exposure of a covered leg, and
O-5 and O-6 stay reachable. By contrast, all six are reachable
on tastytrade’s MCP server [4]. Hence, a ban on uncovered
writing removes one primitive and leaves five, which is why
§5.2 measures write scope and the worst reachable loss for
every system.

4.1 Data Collection
Sources and snapshot.We collected candidate systems from
three sources at one snapshot, and every count in this paper
refers to it. On GitHub, six repository searches through the
search API (strings in Appendix D) returned 1,232 hits and
765 unique repositories, each query staying under the API’s
1,000-result cap. For brokers, we collected first-party agent
interfaces, seeded by an industry test that found agent MCP
servers at 7 of 14 brokers [6], and read each broker’s docu-
mentation. For the research literature, we searched arXiv and
security, machine-learning, and finance proceedings with op-
tions terms combined with LLM and agent terms and followed
references from recent trading-agent work [13, 24].
Eligibility.A candidate is eligible if (I1) an LLM makes,
proposes, or gates an options decision (strategy, strike, ex-
piry, structure, entry, or exit), or an interface is published for
an LLM agent to do so, (I2) it reads option-chain or option-
position data, (I3) a public paper, README, or documenta-
tion page supports scoring it, and (I4) the artifact is available
on the snapshot date.

4.2 The Safeguard Codebook
We score each system with a design-level codebook built on
the two dimensions of FARSIGHT [13], robustness and secu-
rity, and redefined for the options order. As Figure 4 shows,
the codebook asks two questions of every system, whether
it stops a harmful order when nothing adversarial happens
and whether it stops one that an attacker induced, and then
records what the system can reach. Table 8 in Appendix B
defines every indicator together with the public framework
it operationalizes, namely the OW ASP Top 10 for LLM Ap-
plications [43], the market-access Rule 15c3-5 of the U.S.
Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) [44], and Rule
2360, under which FINRA, the U.S. self-regulator of broker-
dealers, approves customers for options [45].
Robustness (R1–R7).Seven indicators ask whether a system
stops the harmful orders that an honest agent can produce
on options when the market moves or the model errs. R1–
R5 follow the axes A1–A5, from a maximum loss computed
before entry (R1) and Greeks and IV derived from a pricing
model (R2) to rules for exercise and assignment (R5). R6
(stress) and R7 (portfolio limits) come from FARSIGHT. The
same checks also stop a manipulated order, whatever produced
it.
Security (C1–C11).C1–C7 guard the inputs that arrive
through the three surfaces of §3, covering source authentica-
tion, market-data integrity, tool trust, instruction–data separa-
tion, memory, peer agents, and supply chain. C8–C10 guard
the order with a pre-trade limit outside the model, least privi-
lege, and monitoring with a kill switch. C11 records whether
anyone tested either side adversarially.
Profile and impact.Five profile attributes record what a
system can reach without judging it. Autonomy (X2) runs
from A0 (advisory) to A4 (no gate), where A3 is a gate that
the agent can clear, that is off by default, or that a docu-
mented setting removes. Write scope (X3) runs from W0
(read-only) through W1 (long premium), W2 (defined-risk
spreads), and W3 (covered or cash-secured shorts) to W4 (un-
covered shorts), counting the broker’s own restrictions. Three
impact indicators are computed from the profile, namely the
worst loss the order path can reach (H1), the blast radius (H2),
and whether a control outside the model can stop a manipu-
lated order before execution (H3).
Scoring levels.Each robustness and security indicator takes
one of three levels. means that the safeguard is documented
and enforced outside the LLM, in code, configuration, or plat-
form policy. means that it is documented but depends on
the model following an instruction, is optional or off by de-
fault, or can be cleared by the agent itself. means that the
public artifacts document nothing, and C8 and C10 do not
apply to a system without an order path. The line between
full and partial is the one that matters for security, because an
instruction in a prompt sits in the same context the attacker
writes to.
Scoring process and validation.One coder scored the sys-
tems of an initial snapshot from their papers, repositories,
and documentation, and the 13 systems added at the final
snapshot were scored with LLM assistance from the same

The same event concentrated the population on one bro-
ker. Of the 215 repositories, 136 name Alpaca, 119 of them
created inside the two-week window. Because Alpaca rejects
uncovered options at every options level [31], the broker’s pol-
icy bounds this population at assignment, whatever the agents
themselves check. By contrast, agent code written against a
write-enabled broker inherits that broker’s scope. For exam-
ple, schwab-mcp and tasty-agent accept sell-to-open legs with
no coverage check, so their authority equals the options level
of the account they connect to (Appendix C). The same code
is bounded at assignment on one account and unbounded on
another.
Assignment is a bound, not a small loss. When a cash-
secured put is assigned, the account buys 100 shares per con-
tract at the strike, so a fall in the underlying passes to the
account in full, up to the strike times 100 less the premium
received. A covered call that is assigned sells shares the ac-
count wanted to keep. The hackathon population therefore
cannot lose without limit through Alpaca, yet a manipulated
put can still turn its cash into a falling stock position (O-5).

Unbounded order paths.These three broker interfaces fail
in three plain ways (Appendix C). OKX Agent Trade Kit’s
order tool posts straight to the exchange with no confirmation,
dry-run or preview parameter, and writing an option there is
an ordinary margined sell. Public MCP builds each order from
the raw tool arguments and accepts sell-to-open legs after a
syntax check. Webull MCP ships notional and quantity risk
controls in its code, but neither of its option-order validators
calls them. In each case one tool call can open a naked short
whose loss has no ceiling, on whatever account the customer
connected to the agent. Open-source servers repeat the pattern
at the account level. tasty-agent and schwab-mcp accept sell-
to-open legs with no coverage check, so the agent’s authority
equals the options level of the account it is given, and schwab-
mcp’s human approval step disappears behind a documented
command-line flag.

connector states that it never narrows the account’s permis-
sions. A customer who approves a credit spread that has lost
its protective leg sees a routine ticket that collects a premium,
which is exactly the order O-1 induces (§3.2). Of the 26 sys-
tems, 3 require such an approval in every configuration, so
human review is rare, and where it exists it bounds how often

Order side.A pre-trade limit enforced outside the model
(C8) is the most common full safeguard, present in 10 of
the 22 order-capable systems, followed by monitoring with a
kill switch (C10, 5) and least privilege (C9, 3). These limits

the same blind spot, because a person approving a ticket sees
the order and never sees the hidden instruction [9] or poisoned
tool output [10] behind it.
Options robustness.Options-specific robustness is mostly
partial. Assignment and pin handling (R5) and stress handling
(R6) are full in no system, maximum-loss computation (R1)
is full in 4, and Greeks and IV provenance (R2) in 3. A partial
score usually marks an instruction to the model, which catches
an honest error and gives no protection against an attacker
who writes into the same context.
Partial safeguards in practice.Two examples show the
pattern. tasty-agent’s only confirmation requirement lives in a
skill prompt, while its order tool executes on a single call with
dry-run off by default. lumibot scores partial on seventeen of
the eighteen safeguards, almost all of them rules the model
is asked to follow or settings the operator may turn on. An
attacker who writes into the agent’s context writes into the
same channel as these rules, so a partial score bounds an
honest model’s mistakes and leaves the induced order open.
The best-guarded systems.The best-guarded systems are
among the least adopted. Two repositories with zero and three
stars, alpaca-options-agent and Agentic-Trader, each enforce
eight safeguards outside the model, including a maximum-
loss computation (R1) and a pre-trade limit (C8). By contrast,
no broker interface enforces more than three, and the two
most-starred open-source platforms, openalgo and lumibot,
enforce two and none. Even the two best-guarded systems
leave the inputs to the model, since neither enforces source
authentication, market-data integrity or instruction–data sepa-
ration outside it. Adoption has therefore gone to the designs
that guard least, and none of the designs guards the text.
RQ3.0 of the 26 systems authenticate their sources or
cross-check market data, and 20 of the 22 order-capable
systems enforce no input-side control. The common safe-
guard is a size limit (10 of 22), which a manipulated order
that fits the budget passes.
6 Recommendations
Our findings point to the order as the place to bound an options
agent’s loss, because that is where the risk structure becomes
concrete. Beyond FARSIGHT’s case for invariants outside
the model [13], options add a sharper reason. Because the
manipulation we care about most asserts nothing false, the
text gives a filter nothing to catch. Content-level detection
looks for a malicious property of the input, such as an injected
instruction, a fabricated figure, or a spoofed source, and a
manipulation built from true statements has none. The harm
arises when accurate text is mapped to a risk structure, since
the same true earnings headline can justify a debit spread with
a known maximum loss or an uncovered short call with none.
The order is a small formal object whose worst-case loss at
expiry is computable exactly [19], so defenses belong at the
order boundary, with content filtering as a secondary layer.
Brokers own that boundary, so the first five controls are theirs.
Defined-risk structures for agent accounts.Agent-
originated orders should be limited to structures with a fi-
nite maximum loss, with uncovered writing gated behind a
separate approval that no agent tool call can grant. FINRA
Rule 2360 requires each customer to be approved for spe-
cific option activities, including uncovered writing [45], and
FINRA’s 2021 action against Robinhood found the firm ap-
proving options accounts through “option account approval
bots” under limited principal oversight [70]. Brokers should
extend that approval to the agent as a distinct principal with its
own options level. Robinhood’s two official sources describe
different agent scopes (§2), and whichever one the broker
enforces should be stated as the agent’s level.
Per-order and per-leg maximum loss.Before routing, the
broker should compute the worst-case loss of the whole order
13
and of each leg alone, then reject any order whose loss is
unbounded or exceeds the account’s cap. Today 4 of the 22
order-capable systems compute and enforce a maximum loss
before entry (R1). The check matters per leg because a spread
that is defined-risk as a package becomes naked if only its
short leg fills, or if its long leg closes first.
Confirmation outside the agent.The tastytrade MCP server
binds submission to a dry-run check that an unattended agent
can satisfy itself [4], so the gate stops a careless agent and lets
a steered one through. 7 of the systems we scored sit behind
such an agent-clearable gate (§5.2). A confirmation should
travel through a channel the agent does not control, bind to a
digest of the order, and show the broker-computed maximum
loss.
Dedicated agent accounts.Robinhood already places agents
in a dedicated account with spending limits [27]. The cap
should also cover margin, and an agent account should never
draw buying power from the owner’s main account.
Pre-trade risk controls.Under SEC Rule 15c3-5, a broker-
dealer with market access must apply pre-trade credit, capital,
and erroneous-order controls [44]. An agent MCP endpoint
deserves the same filters: price collars against the quoted
spread (A3), rejection of market orders on wide-spread con-
tracts, limits on order size and frequency, and tighter limits
near expiry, where decay and gamma dominate pricing (A4).
Agent builders control everything before the order reaches
the broker, from the text the agent reads to the order it sends.
Untrusted text and tools.Every news item, social post, and
tool output should be handled as attacker-controllable, be-
cause indirect prompt injection needs no model access [9]
and MCP tool descriptions are a documented poisoning vec-
tor [10]. Signed feeds, C2PA manifests [71], and RFC 3161
timestamps [72] narrow who can write into the agent’s con-
text, although what is written still goes unjudged. Today no
system we scored enforces source authentication (C1), and 8
rely on an instruction to the model.
Numbers re-derived from the chain.Greeks, implied
volatility, and theoretical prices quoted in text should never
enter a decision directly. The agent should recompute implied
volatility from the quoted bid and ask by inverting a pricing
model [19, 20], derive Greeks from it, and discard any text-
stated figure outside a tolerance, because neither the model
nor a reviewer can check these numbers by eye (A2). Just 3
of the 26 systems derive Greeks and IV this way today (R2).
Separate reasoning and order channels.CaMeL separates
control flow from retrieved data [73], spotlighting marks input
provenance [74], and StruQ and SecAlign train models to
ignore data-channel instructions [75,76]. None of the systems
we scored enforces such a separation outside the model (C4),
and 5 rely on prompt instructions. Even where deployed, these
defenses stop injected instructions and leave accurate text that
shifts judgment untouched, so the order-level checks remain
necessary.
Sizing outside the model.Position size, maximum loss per
trade, and per-day loss limits belong in deterministic code
between the model and the broker API, where a policy engine
that the model cannot rewrite gates each order. 10 of the 22
order-capable systems enforce such a limit today (C8), and in
7 more the agent can clear or disable it.
Order-flow hygiene and liquidity limits.Because an agent’s
orders are observable (A6), builders should avoid resting pre-
dictable orders in thin contracts, vary timing and size, and
refuse contracts below an open-interest or volume floor. These
controls lower what a profile-and-harvest attacker (O-6) can
collect without having to detect the manipulation.
Adversarial testing before release.No system documents
a full adversarial evaluation (C11), and 4 document a partial
one. Before going live, builders should replay the primitives
of §3.2 through the agent’s own order path on a paper-trading
account and publish the worst loss of each resulting order
with the code.
7 Open Problems
Our measurement shows which safeguards options agents
document and what their order paths can reach. Six problems
remain open before a broker or a builder can show that an
agent withstands the attacker of §3.
1. Attack success measured in dollars.Because we scored
designs from their public artifacts, the next measurement
replays the primitives of §3.2 against running agents
through their own order paths on paper-trading accounts,
and reports each primitive’s success per model and per
safeguard configuration. Such a benchmark has to score
the three properties of the final order defined in §3 and the
shift in net Greeks, because a decision-flip rate records no
attack whenever the direction stays correct.
2. Provenance for derived statistics.Just 3 of the 26 systems
derive Greeks and IV from a pricing model or an authorita-
tive chain with sanity checks (R2), and none cross-checks
market data against an independent feed (C2). Although
recomputing IV from the quoted bid and ask is cheap, text
and tools also carry statistics such as IV rank and expected
move that depend on history. An open question is whether
such a number can carry a signed computation chain that
an adaptive attacker cannot forge.
3. Leg-level atomicity.A defined-risk order becomes naked
when its short leg fills before its long leg, or when its long
leg closes first. One purposive system already builds every
spread as a single native multi-leg order (Appendix C). It
remains open how an order-level verifier should bound the
window between legs at brokers that route legs separately.
4. The price of unpredictability.Randomized order timing
and size, and refusing thin contracts, lower what a profile-
and-harvest attacker (O-6) can collect. The size of that

8 Related Work
Security of LLM trading agents.The closest work is FAR-
SIGHT, the equities systematization of Wang and Saxena [13],
which evaluated 15 academic equities designs and found that
80% fail a core robustness metric and all 15 have security vul-
nerabilities. Its unit of harm is a flipped buy-or-sell decision
on a linear position. TradeTrap perturbs single components
of equities trading agents and reports extreme concentration
and large drawdowns in closed-loop backtests [17], and Mar-
ket Signal Injection changes formatting, ordering, or com-
mentary to shift LLM pricing-agent decisions in simulated
oligopolies [18]. OQL, the closest options system, translates
natural-language strategy descriptions into a domain-specific
language that an engine validates and instantiates deterministi-
cally, and it is evaluated for translation accuracy [47]. Table 7
compares our study with FARSIGHT dimension by dimen-
sion. In contrast, we measure the options agents that brokers
and developers ship today and price each failure by the worst
loss of the order it produces.
Agent-security benchmarks and systematizations.Agent-
Dojo [14], InjecAgent [15], and the Agent Security Bench [16]
measure whether injected content triggers unintended tool
calls, in the indirect prompt-injection setting of Greshake et
al. [9], and MCPTox measures tool poisoning on real MCP
servers [10]. Kim et al. [78] systematize attacks and defenses
across agentic AI systems, and a systematization of agentic
commerce [79] treats autonomous agents that transact on a
user’s behalf. Defenses such as CaMeL [73], spotlighting [74],
StruQ [75], and SecAlign [76] separate instructions from data.
In contrast, we measure which protections deployed options
agents enforce, and we score actions whose loss depends on
the legs of an order and is counted in dollars at expiry.
Adversarial trading and order-flow leakage.Trading
against a victim whose orders leak is a known attack class.
Flash Boys 2.0 documented front-running bots on decentral-
ized exchanges [80], and a systematization of decentralized-
finance attacks organized the class [81]. In contrast, our coun-
terparty primitive (O-6) targets the observable options orders
of an LLM agent in thin listed contracts, where the attacker
first induces the order that it then fills.
9 Discussion
The broker as a point of control.Because an agent’s worst
case is set by the broker’s write policy and the account’s
options level (§5.1, §5.2), one broker decision protects ev-
ery agent on the platform at once. Robinhood and Alpaca
already exclude uncovered writing, and their agents stop at
assignment whatever the agent code checks. The other bro-
kers let an agent inherit the options level of the human who
connected it. FINRA Rule 2360 approves that level by as-
sessing the customer’s experience and finances [45], which
15
says nothing about the software that will place the orders. An
agent-specific approval level that excludes uncovered writing
would therefore remove the unbounded tail from 6 of the 8
broker interfaces we scored without changing a line of agent
code.
Size limits and loss limits.The pre-trade controls that
market-access rules require are written as caps on size, no-
tional value and credit [44], and C8 is the safeguard we found
most often. On options, however, a cap on contracts or no-
tional leaves a naked short unbounded, and 4 of the 13 systems
that reach unbounded loss enforce such a cap. The control that
bounds the induced order is a maximum loss computed from
the legs before entry (R1), which 4 of the 22 order-capable
systems enforce. Brokers that already compute margin per
position hold the numbers this check needs.
Evaluating direction-preserving attacks.Agent-security
benchmarks count unintended tool calls [14–16], and trading-
agent evaluations count flipped buy-or-sell decisions [13]. An
options attack that keeps the direction and drops a protective
leg triggers neither, because the agent called the intended tool
and chose the intended side. Evaluations of options agents
should therefore score the risk class of the resulting order,
the change in maximum loss between the honest and the
induced order, rather than whether the decision changed, and
no system we scored documents a full adversarial evaluation
(C11).
A fast-turnover ecosystem.Most of the open-source pop-
ulation appeared within two weeks around one event (§5.1),
and much of it is likely to stop being maintained once the
event ends. Its designs nevertheless persist through forks and
templates, and openalgo alone has 1,275 forks. Review effort
is better spent on the shared layers that many agents reuse,
namely broker SDKs, MCP servers and starter templates, than
on individual repositories, because a missing coverage check
in one shared order tool reaches every agent built on it.
Generality.Index, futures and crypto options share all five
decision variables, so the threat model and codebook apply to
them unchanged, and our scored set already includes futures
options (tastytrade) and crypto options (OKX, a Deribit agent).
Although the enabling layer is deployed (§2), we found no
public report of a realized loss from an autonomous options
agent, so the window for building controls into the order path
is open now.
Limitations.Three limitations bound how far the counts ex-
tend. First, we score documented designs at one snapshot, so
the RQ2 and RQ3 counts describe what each paper, repository,
or broker page commits to, and a running deployment may
add or drop safeguards. Second, one coder and LLM-assisted
passes produced the scores and we report no inter-coder agree-
ment, so cells near the line between full and partial in Table 5
carry one coder’s judgment. Third, proprietary desks are ab-
sent, because our search finds public systems.
10 Conclusion
We measured the LLM options-trading agents that brokers
and developers ship and scored each by the worst loss its
order path can reach. Most of the open-source population
appeared in 2026 around one hackathon. None of the 26 sys-
tems verified their inputs and 13 could reach unbounded loss,
so controls for options agents belong at the order boundary,
outside the model.

A Attack Classes That Reach the Prim-
itives
Table 4 in §3.2 lists nine publicly documented attack classes,
grouped by the FARSIGHT attack taxonomy [13], that reach
an options agent with the attacker’s capabilities unchanged. A
class enters the table if a peer-reviewed or public preprint ex-
emplar demonstrates it on some LLM victim, if the attacker’s
action uses a surface of §3, and if its escalation follows from
payoff mechanics [19, 20] and names at least one axis of §2.
The effect column reports what each exemplar measured on
its own victims, none of which traded options, and the escala-
tion column states what the same corruption costs on options.
Each class feeds at least one primitive of Table 3, and the
escalations fall into three patterns.
Direction-preserving loss.On an equities agent, an
information-source attack pays when it flips the buy-or-sell
decision. Rows M1, M4, and M5 instead impose a loss while
the agent picks the correct direction. An option priced on
implied volatility and time (A4) loses value when a move
arrives late or smaller than the premium implies, so inflating
the expected size of a move sells the agent an overpriced
bet in the right direction, and a wide spread (A3) makes a
manufactured flow narrative cost the spread on entry and exit.
20
New order fields.Rows M7 to M9 act on the order itself. An
equity order has a side, a size, and a price, while an options
order adds legs, ratios, strikes, expiries, an opening or closing
flag, and exercise decisions (A5). An injected instruction or a
poisoned tool response therefore has more fields to hide in,
and a single altered leg changes the risk class of the whole
position (A1) while the order still looks routine. On an options
account, the falsified state field of TradeTrap’s M8 result [17]
would be a margin requirement or a maximum-loss figure.
Framing without falsehood.Row M3 matters most for
O-1, because writing an option collects a premium kept in
most outcomes and gives back a large multiple of it in the
rare adverse one [19]. Instruction-tuned models exhibit the
certainty effect [37], which overweights outcomes presented
as near-certain [36], and “this put expires worthless in most
months” can be literally true while pointing the agent toward
the payoff that hides its tail. A fact-check finds nothing to
reject, because each stated fact checks out.
B Safeguard Codebook
Table 8 defines every indicator, the rule that separates a full
score from a partial one, and the public framework or axis the
indicator operationalizes. For every robustness and security
indicator, applies when the safeguard is documented but
depends on the model following an instruction, is optional
or off by default, or can be cleared by the agent itself, and
applies when the public artifacts document nothing. Scores
are design-level, from public artifacts at the snapshot, with
cell-level evidence in Appendix C. Five rules make the pro-
file and impact cells mechanical, and a consistency check
rejects any cell that violates them. (1) X2 and X3 describe
the most permissive configuration that the documentation or
code lets an operator enable, and X3 counts the broker’s own
restrictions and the account’s highest options level. (2) A gate
yields A2 only if it applies to every order in every documented
configuration. A gate that the agent can clear, that is off by
default, or that a documented setting removes yields A3. (3)
C8 is full when at least one control outside the model that the
agent cannot clear applies to every order, partial when such
controls are optional or agent-clearable or when only broker
eligibility rules apply, and none otherwise. C8 and C10 do
not apply to a system without an order path (A0 or W0). (4)
H1 is none for A0, A1, or W0 and otherwise premium (W1),
assignment (W2, W3), or unbounded (W4), and H2 is plat-
form for broker-native and adopted open-source systems. (5)
H3 is interceptable for A0 to A2, partial for A3 or when C8
is full or partial, and not interceptable otherwise. One coder
scored the systems of the initial snapshot, and the 13 systems
added at this snapshot were scored with LLM assistance and
re-checked against their sources in a second LLM-assisted
pass. Every cell changed by these rules keeps its previous
value in the evidence file. We have not run a second-coder
pass and report no inter-coder agreement.

D Search Protocol
Snapshot.Searches, star counts, READMEs, and documen-
tation pages reflect the snapshot. Repositories and broker
documentation change monthly, so the scores describe that
snapshot.
Sources and query strings.(i)Academic: arXiv and se-
curity, machine-learning, and finance proceedings, with the
terms “options trading”, “option strategy”, “implied volatil-
ity”, “Greeks”, and “derivatives” each combined with “large
language model”, “LLM”, or “agent”, plus backward and
forward references from the equities SoK [13] and recent
trading-agent work [17,18,24,82]. (ii)GitHub: the repository
search API with six strings,options trading LLM , option
greeks llm , llm options trader , options trading
agent, options trading ai agent , and options mcp
server, which returned 1,232 hits in total. Every query
stayed under the API’s 1,000-result cap, and the union of all
six holds 765 unique repositories. (iii)Broker documentation:
first-party agent interfaces, seeded by an industry test that
found first-party agent MCP servers at 7 of 14 brokers, all
launched in 2026 [6].
Screening.Figure 3 in §4 summarizes the GitHub screen,
and this paragraph gives its details. An automated keyword
filter runs first: a repository survives if its name, description,
topics, and README (first 30 kB) match at least one of 21
LLM- or agent-related terms (e.g., “llm”, “mcp”, “agentic”,
or a named model family such as “claude” or “gemini”) and
at least one of 29 options-related terms (e.g., “greeks”, “iron
condor”, “0dte”, “covered call”), case-insensitively. Both lists
are recall-oriented by design: a bare “expiry” or “mcp” can
fire on unrelated software, so this stage over-includes on pur-
pose and the reading pass below removes false positives. Of
the 765 candidates, 468 survive for reading against four in-
clusion criteria: a candidate is eligible if (I1) an LLM makes,
proposes, or gates an options decision (strategy, strike, expiry,
structure, entry, or exit), or an interface is published for an
LLM agent to do so, (I2) the system reads option-chain or
option-position data, live or historical, (I3) a public paper,
README, or documentation page supports scoring the candi-
date against the codebook (Appendix B), and (I4) the artifact
is available on the snapshot date.
Exclusion criteria.Every other Stage 1 survivor is coded
with one reason. (E1) LLM trading framework without op-
tions support (11): the most-starred general frameworks we
checked, TradingAgents (108,459 stars), ai-hedge-fund
(63,736), FinGPT (21,288), and FinRobot (8,080), mention
no option chains, Greeks, IV , strikes, or expiries in their
READMEs. (E2) Options bot with no LLM in the decision
path (52). (E3) Public documentation insufficient to score (24).
(E4) Attack, simulation, or taxonomy work rather than an op-
25
tions agent: no GitHub repository was excluded for this reason
(0), but the same code separately removes TradeTrap [17] and
Market Signal Injection [18], which study equities trading and
pricing agents, the agentic-commerce SoK [79], which has
no options-specific treatment, and a market-simulation study
of LLM traders [82], all found through the academic search
rather than GitHub. (E5) Not a trading system: education, data
tools, or libraries without an LLM decision path (114). (E6)
Duplicate, fork, or mirror of an included project (46). (E7)
Empty or placeholder repository (6).
Outcome.Academic search surfaced two systems meeting
I1 to I4, OQL [47] and Kuang and Lin [46], and we scored
both. Broker documentation and the industry test yielded 8 of-
ficial interfaces: Robinhood [2,28] and tastytrade [4], plus six
more: OKX Agent Trade Kit [48], Public MCP [49], Webull
MCP [50], TradeStation MCP [52], Alpaca MCP [30], and
IBKR AI integration [51]. Seven scored repositories entered

Theta Desk [66], tastytrade MCP, schwab-mcp [56], trad-
ing_skills [59], Public MCP, and Webull MCP. Beyond the
scored set, 201 eligible repositories remain unscored. Of the

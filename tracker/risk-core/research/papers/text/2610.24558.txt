Optimal Trading Filters and the Value of Predictive Signals
Matteo Smerlak∗
Capital Fund Management, 23 rue de l’Université, 75007 Paris, France
The value of a forecast depends on its accuracy, but also on its actionability. In trading, an
investor must translate return forecasts into positions while accounting for trading costs and the
lingering impact of earlier trades. We solve the stationary quadratic trading problem with transient
price impact using tools from prediction theory: Wiener–Hopf factorization gives the optimal policy
as the composition of a conditional forecast and an explicit causal linear filter. We recover the
Gârleanu–Pedersen aim-portfolio under exponential resilience and derive fractional trading filters
under power-law impact decay, requiring memory beyond finite-dimensional LQR control. At
fixed positive risk penalty, signals of equal instantaneous predictive strength have equal frictionless
Markowitz value. With trading costs, net attainable value is determined by the mean square of the
friction-weighted conditional forecast. Under power-law impact with exposure bleeding, we find an
intermediate optimal mean-reversion rate for Ornstein–Uhlenbeck signals.
I. INTRODUCTION
Given predictions of future returns, an investor must
determine how to act on them and what they are worth
after trading costs. Transient price impact connects these
decisions across time: a trade earns exposure to the pre-
dicted return but also changes the cost of subsequent
trades. The investor must choose how quickly to build,
maintain, and unwind a position as both forecasts and
impact evolve.
Transient impact has both theoretical and empirical mo-
tivation. In the resilient-order-book model of Obizhaeva
and Wang [1], depleted liquidity recovers between or-
ders. Bouchaudet al. [2] documented persistent order
signs alongside approximately diffusive prices; within their
propagator model, individual-trade impact must decay
with age. Bouchaudet al. [3] inferred algebraic decay
after accounting for order-flow correlations, which con-
found the measured response to a trade with subsequent
trading. We use exponential and power-law kernels as
tractable models, retaining linearity in trading rate as an
assumption; the detailed decay law remains market- and
estimation-dependent.
In this paper we solve the stationary quadratic version
of the trading problem using tools from prediction the-
ory. We consider ongoing investment with an established
trading history and no terminal liquidation date, allowing
exposure to bleed between trades. (Such exposure bleed-
ing occurs, for example, when an options book loses vega
as maturity approaches, so maintaining a fixed exposure
requires costly replenishment [4].) Using Wiener–Hopf
factorization, we decompose the optimal policy into a
conditional forecast of a friction-adjusted signal followed
by a causal linear filter [5–8]. From this construction we
obtain both the policy and its net value, together with
the loss relative to knowing the entire predictor path. In
the Gaussian examples, we obtain a linear policy with an
explicit frequency response.

We recover the target portfolio of Gârleanu and Peder-
sen[9,10]andderivefractionaltradingfiltersunderpower-
law impact. Exponential impact admits finite-dimensional
relaxation dynamics. Power-law impact gives a nonra-
tional response, precluding an exact finite-dimensional
linear time-invariant realization even for an Ornstein–
Uhlenbeck (OU) predictor. These fractional policies ap-
ply to stationary investment, complementing the finite-
horizon liquidation results of Fordeet al.[11]. Matérn
predictors allow us to study history-dependent forecasting
alongside market memory.
We compare signals at fixed instantaneous predictive
strength. Under frictionless Markowitz allocation with
a fixed positive inventory penalty [12], optimal value
depends only on predictor variance, so all signals in this
comparison have the same frictionless value. We show that
attainable value equals half the mean square of a friction-
weighted conditional forecast of future drift. This gives
a market-dependent criterion for comparing predictors:
their value depends on how strongly they forecast the
future drift components that the investor can profitably
exploit.
Under power-law impact, replenishing bleeding expo-
sure penalizes persistent positions, while adjustment costs
penalize rapid changes. The balance selects an intermedi-
ate optimal mean-reversion rate for OU signals. Coher-
ence provides a separate source of value: concentrating
signal variance at a frequency of minimum friction gives
a sharp bound over stationary signal laws. Perfectly pre-
dictable oscillations at costly frequencies can nevertheless
have arbitrarily small value. We also compare predictor
smoothness after optimizing signal speed, distinguish-
ing this comparison from holding the predictor decay
rate fixed. Empirical use requires estimating predictive
strength jointly with signal dynamics and trading costs.
The quadratic trading problem extends the single-
period mean–variance framework of Markowitz[12] and
dynamic portfolio theory [13–15]. Execution costs are
studied by Bertsimas and Lo[16], Almgren and Chriss
[17], Gatheralet al. [18]. Gârleanu and Pedersen
[9, 10], Henry and Bouchaud[19] study dynamic portfo-
lio choice with predictable returns and trading frictions,

VI. CONCLUSION
We have obtained a causal stationary trading rule by
using Wiener–Hopf factorization to separate forecasting
from causal execution. For OU signals, the forecast is
proportionaltothecurrentobservation, sowecanexamine
the market response directly.
We obtain an explicit fractional trading policy under
power-law impact even for this one-state predictor. Its
nonrational transfer precludes an exact finite-dimensional
linear time-invariant realization. Exponential impact ad-
mits finite-dimensional execution dynamics, while history-
dependent Matérn forecasts can make the full trading
response nonrational. Predictor memory changes the re-
sponse even with local execution costs. We treat both
sources of memory with the same factorization.
Exposure bleeding makes maintaining a position costly.
In the power-law examples, slow signals require sustained
replenishment, while fast signals require costly adjust-
ments. We therefore find an intermediate OU signal
speed that gives the highest net value. For ordinary hold-
ings with an inventory penalty, slower signals are favored
in the markets we study.
We have used stationary trading to study the value
of information in systems with memory. Even for fully
predictable signals, value depends on the relation between
their temporal structure and the dynamics of the response.
Extending the analysis beyond stationary quadratic mod-
els would clarify which aspects of this dependence persist
under more general dynamics.

Abstract
Testing by betting is a powerful framework that recasts hypothesis testing as a betting game. A
popular approach spreads the initial wealth over fixed bets according to a prior distribution, using a
universal portfolio algorithm. For suitable priors, this approach achieves the optimal expected rejection
time as the error probability goes to zero at a fixed alternative. Yet, priors with this guarantee can
behave quite differently near the null. This motivates the question: Which prior should we use in
testing-by-betting algorithms? We study bounded scalar payoffs and, under technical conditions, prove a
sharp formula for expected rejection time, uniform over all bounded alternatives as the error probability
tends to zero. The formula shows how the prior’s mass around the zero bet determines the cost of
adapting to an unknown alternative. We characterize the priors that attain the best possible leading
constant when the error probability and alternative vary together. We also show that no valid test can
uniformly improve on this cost across ranges of alternatives near the null. In particular, the uniform
prior can require arbitrarily more observations than suitable priors that concentrate around the zero
bet. A general inversion argument converts our results into sharp expected times to reach a prescribed
confidence-sequence width. We also give a concrete prior choice and an FFT-based algorithm with almost
linear computational complexity.
1 Introduction
Suppose a hypothesis lets us place bets that are fair whenever it is true. The observations need not themselves
be the quantity of interest: a bet may be based on a bounded score, a difference between two measurements,
or an e-value [Vovk and Wang, 2021] supplied by another procedure. Our wealth over time represents the
evidence against that hypothesis: if we gain enough money, we can reject the hypothesis while keeping the
chance of a false alarm small. This is the testing-by-betting framework [Shafer and Vovk, 2001, Shafer, 2021,
Ramdas and Wang, 2025].

2 Related work
The connection between betting and probability goes back to Ville [1939], whose martingale inequality
supplies the validity guarantee used here. The oracle betting strategy when the payoff distribution is known
is Kelly’s criterion, which maximizes the expected log wealth [Kelly, 1956, Breiman, 1961]. When we do
not know this probability distribution, universal portfolio algorithms show how to compete with the best
constant allocation in hindsight [Cover, 1991, Cover and Ordentlich, 1996]. The same betting viewpoint
later became a tool for parameter-free online learning [Orabona and P´ al, 2016, Cutkosky and Orabona,
2018]. These developments connect probabilistic validity, stochastic growth, and adversarial regret, but each
supplies a different guarantee.
Shafer and Vovk [2001] develop a game-theoretic foundation for probability: forecasts specify betting
prices, and nonnegative capital processes test their agreement with subsequent outcomes. Their bounded
forecasting protocol (Sections 3.2–3.3) is exactly the betting setup we use here. Modern accounts of testing
by betting include Shafer [2021], Ramdas and Wang [2025]. The log-growth viewpoint underlies growth-
optimal e-values [Gr¨ unwald et al., 2024, Larsson et al., 2025] and e-processes [Wang et al., 2025, Waudby-
Smith et al., 2026]. For bounded means, Waudby-Smith and Ramdas [2024] characterize test martingales
under the constant-conditional-mean null as predictable betting products, and Clerico [2025] identifies these
products as the admissible e-processes. This resolves which wealth processes are undominated, but it leaves
open which betting strategies have the best expected rejection times.
F rom betting guarantees to concentration and prior choice.The construction of priors for se-
quential LIL boundaries was developed by Darling and Robbins [1967], Robbins [1970]. The game-theoretic
development in Shafer and Vovk [2001, Section 5.2, especially equation (5.15)] similarly combines nonnega-
tive capital processes across betting scales to obtain a LIL bound. They credit the earlier proofs of Dawid
and Vovk [1999] and Vovk [1988], and trace the underlying idea back to Ville [1939]. These pathwise guaran-
tees motivate adaptation across scales, but do not characterize the expected rejection time associated with
a prior. An early connection to online betting appears in the technical report of Orabona [2018]. His proof
method works by localizing a log-squared prior in a relative interval of bets, and then turning the resulting
wealth guarantee into an empirical Bernstein LIL bound for bounded martingale differences. We will use his
localization argument in our expected-time proof.
Jun and Orabona [2019, Section 7.2] show how noisy coin-betting potentials give time-uniform concentra-
tion through a nonnegative supermartingale. Their log-squared prior gives LIL-rate bounds for sub-Gaussian
martingale differences, and their online-learning reduction extends the construction to Banach spaces. Build-
ing on this connection, Orabona and Jun [2024] derive bounded-mean confidence sequences from universal-
portfolio regret and obtain the sharp LIL leading constant with a prior adapted from Robbins [1970]. The
mixture proposed in Orabona and Jun [2024, Remark 2] already combines logarithmic worst-case regret,
LIL behavior, and optimal i.i.d. alternative growth. This idea is developed in Agrawal and Ramdas [2026],
who give explicit regret bounds and a sharp asymptotic regret bound for their full-support Robbins prior
on paths with bounded mixture wealth and diverging intrinsic variance. Ryu and Bhatt [2024] subsequently
develop efficient approximations to universal-portfolio wealth.
The relation between prior concentration and boundary growth has itself been studied systematically.
For mixtures of constant-proportion product strategies, Sato et al. [2018] give Robbins-type priors yielding
the sharp self-normalized LIL and establish a correspondence between classes of prior densities near zero
and upper-class boundary functions. Their results apply to bounded forecasting and concern pathwise
boundaries. Instead, our criterion classifies the joint expected rejection-time cost of full-support priors. For

Finite-time LIL concentration and anti-concentration bounds already capture the transition between
confidence and adaptation costs [Balsubramani, 2015]. Kaufmann and Koolen [2021] combine confidence
and iterated-logarithm time corrections in mixture bounds for exponential families, including Bernoulli
observations. Related developments include LIL-based bandit bounds [Jamieson et al., 2014] and almost-
sure iterated-logarithm regret [Agrawal and Ramdas, 2025]. The finer logarithmic terms have classical
antecedents in upper-class integral tests [Erd˝ os, 1942, Feller, 1943]. Priors are also connected to coding:
describing the logarithmic betting scale incurs an iterated-logarithm cost [Rissanen, 1983], and the prior
governs asymptotics under prior averaging more generally [Clarke and Barron, 1990].
Expected-time optimality .The small-error and near-null regimes also have a history of their own.
Farrell [1964] proves an iterated-logarithm lower bound near the null in regular parametric models, and
Robbins and Siegmund [1974] study power-one tests based on prior-weighted likelihood ratios. Fellouris and
Tartakovsky [2012] study how to choose a mixture prior by expected sample size. They optimize a higher-
order minimax criterion based on KL-weighted expected sample size for exponential-family alternatives in
a compact interval bounded away from the null. Our criterion instead distinguishes priors through their
leading cost as the alternative approaches the null. Chen et al. [2017, Lemma 4.1 and Remark D.4] give a
lower bound combining confidence and the entropy of the unknown scale, up to constants, for sign testing,
including a Bernoulli extension. Theorem 5 identifies the sharp sum in our bounded model, uniformly as
both the error level and betting scales vanish.
Nonasymptotic expected-time bounds also combine the two costs. Shin et al. [2021] analyze generalized-
likelihood-ratio tests with iterated-logarithm boundaries, including bounded observations through their cu-
mulant bounds; their information measure is determined by those bounds rather than by the full alterna-
tive law. For bounded adapted observations, Durand and Wintenberger [2025] derive power and expected
rejection-time guarantees from regret, allowing conditional means to change and vanish over time. That
setting differs from a sequence of i.i.d. alternative laws approaching a fixed null, which we consider.
For general composite hypotheses, Agrawal et al. [2026] establish the small-error information lower bound
and a nonparametric Farrell-type lower bound near the null, together with sufficient conditions for matching
upper bounds. Their bounded-mean specialization uses the same KL distance to the null as our oracle rate,
and their Proposition 5.1 combines tests attaining the two guarantees in their respective limits. Waudby-
Smith et al. [2026] obtain the sharp fixed-alternative expected-time limit from a deterministic sublinear
portfolio-regret bound and a moment condition. Section 4.2 compares these expected-time guarantees with
our uniform formula, including their assumptions and orders of limits. Related information bounds arise in
best-arm identification [Garivier and Kaufmann, 2016]; the bounded-support divergence studied by Honda
and Takemura [2010] for cumulative bandit regret coincides with our oracle log wealth.
Expected-time criteria also arise for confidence sequences, which can be obtained by inverting betting
tests [Hendriks, 2018, Orabona and Jun, 2024, Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024]. Shekhar and Ramdas
[2023] define effective width through the expected time to reach a prescribed width, give an information lower
bound, and bound the width of betting confidence sequences with logarithmic regret when the observation
law is supported strictly inside [0,1]. Effective width concerns two endpoints rather than a specified null
mean. In Section 5, we obtain a sharp formula for the expected time to reach a prescribed width, improving
over their result.

4.4 Comparison with growth and LIL guarantees
If eff(τ) = 1, (10) gives the fixed-alternative limit (2). The converse fails:F-weighted portfolios with Beta

6.1 Comparison with regret-based tests
The fixed positive CO96 weight also retains logarithmic portfolio regret: Wealth Fwn ≥wWealth FCO96n adds

6.2 Numerical evaluation by a regional grid and FFT
Evaluating the wealth in (24) can be computationally demanding. Waudby-Smith et al. [2026, Remark 2.2]

mixture efficiently.

Before observing data, partition the allocation interval intoKregions. On each region, map the coor-

cosine transforms computed by FFT. The ratio of the two integrals gives the posterior mean ofz, which we
map back to the allocation interval.

masses. In exact arithmetic, each regional wealth is the continuous conditional mixture, so their weighted
sum is exactly WealthFn . Appendix E gives the integration rule, pseudocode in Algorithms 1–2, and a proof.
Why the grid improves numerical stability .Note that the grid is not necessary for the exactness of
the algorithm in exact arithmetic. However, after a long run favoring one direction, fixed-allocation wealths
can differ by many orders of magnitude. Then, cosine interpolation and its transpose combine signed terms:
roundoff that is small relative to the largest entries can overwhelm small contributions that become important
after a reversal [Liu et al., 2026, Appendix E.6]. This affects both CO96 and R70 prior. A single common
rescaling prevents overflow but does not restore lost relative accuracy.
For this reason we use the decomposition in a grid that permits independent rescaling of products and
functionals in different parts of the bet interval. Note that we do not claim any bound of the error due to
the floating point truncations, but in our experiments this method substantially improved the accuracy. We
store each region’s accumulated wealth logarithmically and combine regions by positive weights, so a region
can regain posterior weight after a reversal. Within a narrow region, we compute the posterior mean of the
local coordinatezbefore mapping it back. This avoids computing an allocation from nearly proportional
numerator and denominator functionals; an error in the local mean is also multiplied by the region’s half-
width. We place more boundaries near the zero bet and the support endpoints, where small betting scales
and concentrated likelihoods need resolution.
Calculations of the moments.The R70 singularity at zero requires a dedicated moment calculation.
We isolate it in an interval centered at zero, use its analytic prior mass, and pair the contributions atβand
−βin the change of variables described in Appendix E. The continuous prior retains all its mass near zero.
The FFT integration weights can still be signed, and products can still concentrate inside a region, so these
measures do not give a uniform roundoff guarantee.

time results; their extension to the rounded strategy requires a separate analysis.
7 Conclusion
We have presented a novel characterization of how a prior’s density near zero determines expected rejection
time in bounded i.i.d. testing. Under Definition 1, the expected-time formula is uniform over all alternatives
asα→0 for finite indices, and the efficiency identity eff(τ F ) =a(F) also covers infinite indices. The
adaptation cost depends on the oracle bet through LL(|β ⋆Q|), including when variance vanishes. Thus, priors
with index 1 are optimal under this criterion, certifying existing Robbins-type constructions. These priors
also attain the joint minimax constant over ranges of small oracle bets among all valid sequential tests. We
also present a rule for combining priors showing that different components can supply the required mass on
the two sides of zero: two priors with infinite efficiency can together attain efficiency 1.
The uniformL 1 limit also gives a general route from rejection times to confidence-sequence widths through
test inversion and balanced endpoint benchmarks. This yields sharp expected times to reach a prescribed
width, including at laws with vanishing variance.
Our characterization also certifies the expected-time optimality of the prior proposed in Orabona and
Jun [2024, Remark 2]. For this prior, we combine a spatial grid with FFT recursion inside each region,
retaining betting validity. Given exact prior moments, this computes the continuous mixture exactly in
exact arithmetic, using eO(T) operations per candidate mean.
It remains open how to distinguish between different priors with index 1. For example, higher-order
expected-time bounds could guide the choice of their parameters and hedging weights.

E Details on OPT-FFT
The algorithm in Section 6 divides the allocation interval into regions, computes an allocation within each
region, and combines these allocations using their accumulated wealth. The main task inside a region is
to integrate products of returns. These products are polynomials, so finitely many prior moments suffice
to integrate them exactly up to a prescribed horizon. We first construct the integration rule, then explain
how the FFT recursion updates it as observations arrive. The final part describes the regions and numerical
moments used by OPT-FFT for CO96 and R70.

Rescaling and extending the horizon.Long products can overflow or underflow. We can rescale a
block product by any positive scalar without changing the ratio that determines the allocation. We can also
rescale both integration-weight vectors of a region by the same positive scalar. The pseudocode omits these
rescalings; the implementation uses them and stores regional wealth logarithmically. If either inner product
is nonfinite, it uses the region’s midpoint. The projection onto [−1,1] in Algorithm 2 keeps the allocation
inside its region; it is inactive in exact arithmetic whenever regional wealth is positive.
The supplied implementation uses a fixed horizon. To continue after roundDin exact arithmetic, prepare
new integration rules for horizon 2Dwith the same prior, and replay the firstDobservations to reconstruct
the recursive state. Discard the allocations produced during replay, then resume betting at roundD+ 1 with
the existing wealth. Exactness makes the combined allocations independent of the horizon and partition
whenever total wealth is positive. Summing the work over doubled horizons preserves the arithmetic and
memory orders in Proposition 28, using the largest region count reached.

has magnitude at mostπ/4. Thus,O(ln(1/ϵ)) terms give absolute truncation error at mostϵfor normalized

Preprocessing cost and accuracy .For fixed quadrature oversampling, computing the moments and
integration rules takesO(K(D+ ln(1/ϵ)) ln(D/ϵ) ln(1/ϵ)) operations. Storing the moments and rules takes
O(KD) memory. The central R70 quadrature hasO(D+ ln(1/ϵ)) panels and orderO(ln(D/ϵ)), and the
implementation stores all its nodes. Processing regions one at a time therefore requires an additionalO((D+
ln(1/ϵ)) ln(D/ϵ)) workspace, which isO(DlnD) at fixed tolerance. With our default region count, total
memory remainsO(Tln 2(T+ 1)), and preprocessing takes eO(T) operations at fixed tolerance.
The bounds above control the approximation near zero and the truncation of the Fourier expansion. We
check the remaining Gaussian quadrature error by increasing its order. Accordingly,ϵis a numerical target,
not a certified bound on all moments or accumulated wealth. The implementation also accepts conditional
moments computed by another routine; Proposition 28 applies whenever those input moments and masses
are exact.
Default hyperparameters.Our default partitions use squared-sine spacing on each side of the zero bet,
with a polylogarithmic number of intervals rounded to a power of 2. At horizons 512, 2,048, and 8,192, this
gives respectively 64, 256, and 512 CO96 regions, and 63, 255, and 511 R70 regions. These are continuous
regions, not point masses. The degree within each region grows with the horizon. A leaf size of 32 avoids
transforms for short products, and the numerical moment routine uses an accuracy target of 10 −13. The
spacing targets the sensitive parts of the domain; the region count and leaf size balance their resolution
against transform overhead. Appendix E specifies the choices.
We empirically validated these hyperparameter choices on 15 paths: Bernoulli and bounded reversals,
Bernoulli alternatives with means 0.505 and 0.7, and Beta(4,3) observations, each at lengths 512, 2,048, and
8,192, withµ 0 = 1/2. Against refined positive quadrature, the largest discrepancy in mixture log wealth was
6.7×10 −11, and no regional allocation required projection. Analytic Bernoulli calculations and independent
integration provide additional checks.

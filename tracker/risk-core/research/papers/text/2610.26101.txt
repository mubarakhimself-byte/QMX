Abstract
LLM agents can turn natural-language financial research
requests into executable and backtested strategies, but
a successful run may still implement the wrong rule,
use information unavailable at decision time, or obscure
how repairs and candidate selection changed the strat-
egy. Because these failures arise throughout develop-
ment, final code and performance metrics alone are
insufficient to establish either fidelity to the request
or the validity of the development process. We there-
fore address the reliability of financial strategy devel-
opment at the trajectory level: the system must control
how intermediate outputs become downstream state
and preserve the evidence needed to justify the termi-
nal outcome. We introduce QuantHarness, a two-layer
agent harness in which a finance-specific Workflow
structures specification, data grounding, construction,
testing, and review, while Harness Core enforces stage-
specific information, action, and commitment bound-
aries. We also introduce FinRelBench, a benchmark that
evaluates strategy-development reliability through ex-
ecution success, requirement satisfaction, and runtime
evidence. QuantHarness achieves the highest Task Suc-
cess at 90.72%, compared with 74.23%–84.54% for the
comparison systems. Controlled stress tests show that its
boundaries contain targeted violations, while structured
run records improve evidence retrieval and support de-
cision replay under the evaluated protocol.

1 Introduction
Recent LLM-based systems can carry quantitative strat-
egy development from a natural-language research re-
quest to an implemented and backtested strategy [9, 29].
Yet successful execution alone does not establish that
the resulting strategy is faithful to the original request.
Generated code may implement a different trading rule
from the one requested [ 7], use information unavail-
able at decision time, or introduce temporal misalign-
ment [11]. Problems may also emerge after the initial
implementation has been produced. A repair can re-
move an execution error by changing the trading rule
itself. Repeated selection based on backtest results can
turn evaluation into search over historical outcomes
[2, 19]. Because these problems can arise at different
stages, the final code and performance metrics alone
may not reveal their source or the decisions that al-
lowed them to persist. We call one execution of strategy
development arun. The ordered path from its research
request to its terminal outcome is the run’sdevelopment
trajectory. We therefore examine reliability across this
trajectory: whether the requested strategy is faithfully
implemented and the development process is supported
by evidence that justifies its terminal outcome.
Prior work approaches parts of this problem from two
directions. Financial agents and benchmarks support
trading decisions, factor discovery, executable research,
and strategy evaluation [4, 7, 9, 18, 21, 29], whereas gen-
eral agent-harness research studies how context, tools,
persistent state, and runtime control shape agent behav-
ior [12, 16, 24]. These directions motivate the question of
how an LLM agent can reliably carry a financial strategy
request through specification, data grounding, imple-
mentation, testing, and final acceptance.

2 Related Work
2.1 LLM Agents for Quantitative
Finance
Recent work on LLM-based financial agents spans
market-facing decision support and executable quan-
titative research. Decision-oriented systems organize
market information through mechanisms such as mem-
ory, reflection, and role-based collaboration [21, 26–28],
while platforms such as FinRobot [23] support a broader
range of financial-analysis tasks. For executable re-
search, Qlib [25] and FinRL-Meta [10] provide reusable
data and market-simulation infrastructure. R&D-Agent-
Quant [ 9] combines structured specifications with
implementation, backtesting, iterative feedback, and
retained research artifacts, while FactorMAD [4] uses
agent debate for interpretable alpha-factor discovery.
As these systems automate more of the research process,
reliability concerns extend beyond the performance
of the resulting strategy to the decisions and evidence
accumulated during its development. Successful ex-
ecution and financial results alone do not establish
that a request was appropriately interpreted, grounded
in data, and carried through to a justified outcome.
QuantHarness addresses this broader need for task
fidelity, process control, and supporting evidence across
the full development trajectory.
2.2 Evaluation of Financial Agents
Recent evaluation efforts for financial LLM systems
cover both broad financial capabilities and executable
research. FinBen [22] illustrates this breadth, covering
financial language tasks, forecasting, risk management,
and trading. More specialized evaluations address the
outputs of quantitative research: AlphaForgeBench [29]
evaluates generated factors and strategies through stan-
dardized backtesting, while BacktestBench [ 18] com-
pares automated backtesting outputs with reference
answers. At the code level, QuantCode-Bench [7] and
AlphaQT-Bench [11] assess properties such as alignment
with task descriptions, temporal causality, functional
correctness, and structural compliance. Together, these
benchmarks assess financial capabilities and the qual-
ity of research outputs. However, output-level assess-
ment alone does not establish whether task constraints
were respected throughout development. We therefore
use FinRelBench to assess whether the requested strat-
egy is faithfully implemented and its required behavior
is demonstrated in execution. We also examine how
agents reached that result, asking whether development
respected task constraints and left sufficient evidence
for subsequent audit.
2.3 Agent Harnesses and Runtime
Governance
Beyond the capabilities of individual models, recent
work on LLM agents examines the surrounding har-
ness: the supporting execution environment that orga-
nizes context, tool access, state, and workflow execution.
Frameworks such as AutoGen [ 20], SWE-agent [ 24],
OpenHands [17], and AIOS [12] provide different combi-
nations of these capabilities. Recent studies also examine
2
how to represent and improve the harness itself. Natural-
Language Agent Harnesses [15] expresses control poli-
cies as editable documents executed through a shared
runtime, with explicit handoffs, state updates, and vali-
dation gates. Meta-Harness [8] optimizes harness code
by inspecting earlier candidates, evaluation scores, and
execution traces. Alongside this work on harness de-
sign, AgentSpec [16] enforces configurable rules during
agent execution. Together, these approaches provide
mechanisms for organizing, improving, and constrain-
ing agent behavior. For financial strategy development,
these mechanisms must be grounded in trading require-
ments and market-data constraints. The challenge is to
carry those requirements through construction, testing,
and acceptance while retaining the evidence needed to
justify each outcome. QuantHarness investigates this
problem across the full development trajectory.
3 QuantHarness
3.1 System Overview
Turning a natural-language request into a runnable fi-
nancial strategy requires both an explicit development
process and control over how intermediate results are
used. QuantHarness governs the development trajectory
through two interacting layers: an agent-facing Work-
flow organizes strategy development, while Harness
Core governs its execution and preserves the resulting
run record. Figure 1 summarizes how the two layers in-
teract. The following subsections describe the Workflow
and Harness Core in turn.
3.2 Finance-Specific Workflow
Within QuantHarness, the Workflow spans the process
from clarifying a request to producing and testing a
runnable strategy implementation. The remainder of
this subsection presents the stages of this process in
sequence.
Making the requested strategy explicit.A natural-
language strategy request often leaves information re-
quired for implementation unspecified. If the model fills
these gaps only while writing code, its assumptions be-
come part of the implementation without being stated
explicitly. Later stages then cannot compare the code
with an explicit description of the intended strategy.
QuantHarness therefore first documents its interpre-
tation of the request, including any assumptions and
unresolved information. The resulting structured de-
scription is thetask contract. The Workflow then uses
the contract to produce astrategy specification. Whereas
the task contract captures what the system understood
from the request, the strategy specification states the
trading rules and constraints that the eventual imple-
mentation must satisfy. The specification is the common
reference for connecting the strategy to available data
and implementing it in code. Any later change in strat-
egy meaning must first be reflected in the specification.
Grounding strategy requirements in available data
and output formats.A strategy specification identifies
the data a trading rule needs, but it does not establish
whether a compatible source and field exist among
the available datasets. Deferring this check to code
generation may cause the implementation to refer
to nonexistent fields or use data at an incompatible
frequency. QuantHarness therefore inspects the avail-
able data before implementation rather than assuming
that every required input exists. Adata bindingmaps
each requirement to a confirmed data source, field,
and frequency; requirements that cannot be resolved
remain explicit rather than being bound to guessed
inputs. A data binding alone is not sufficient for testing
because the backtester must also know how to interpret
the strategy’s decisions. Anoutput protocolspecifies
whether these decisions are represented as asset scores
or trade orders and defines the fields expected by
the backtester. Together, the data binding and output
protocol define how the strategy receives data and
communicates decisions without changing the trading
rule itself.
Constructing a runnable strategy implementation.
The preceding stages fix the strategy requirements,
data inputs, and output protocol, but they do not yet
specify the computation that will produce the trading
decisions. If this procedure is determined only during
code generation or model training, the choices that
define it remain implicit in the resulting implementation.
QuantHarness therefore makes these choices explicit in
animplementation planbefore construction begins. The
plan states how the bound inputs will be transformed
into trading decisions under the strategy specification
and output protocol. QuantHarness realizes this plan
through one of two supported paths. Rule-based strate-
gies are implemented as code that follows the specified
calculation. Model-based strategies use a separate
construction path. To keep model construction within a
testable interface, the current path is limited to a pre-
defined library of machine-learning and deep-learning
templates provided by QuantHarness. The system first
constructs training data from the bound inputs and
fits a model instantiated from the selected template.
The fitted model then produces strategy scores in
the declared output format. The resulting strategy
implementation, whether rule-based or model-based,
becomes the candidate examined in the next stage.

Testing the candidate against established requirements.
The implementation plan guides construction, but the
resulting candidate may still fail to satisfy the struc-
tural, data, or execution requirements established earlier.
QuantHarness therefore tests the completed candidate
against those requirements before it can proceed. The
testing stage has two required steps: (1)validationand
(2)backtesting. Validation first checks whether the can-
didate is structurally complete and compatible with the
execution environment. It then checks whether the can-
didate uses the declared inputs and provides trading de-
cisions in the required form. The results, including any
failed checks, are listed in a validation report. Only can-
didates that pass validation proceed to backtesting. Back-
testing applies the candidate to the market data specified
by the data binding and passes its trading decisions to
the backtester under the declared output protocol. The
resulting backtest report indicates whether backtest ex-
ecution succeeds and whether the trading decisions can
be processed by the backtester. When backtest execution
succeeds, the report also includes the resulting financial
performance metrics. These financial values do not over-
ride the validation and execution checks. A candidate
proceeds only after both checks pass.
Optional strategy optimization.Passing validation
and backtesting establishes that a candidate can be exe-
cuted with the bound inputs and declared output proto-
col. At this point, QuantHarness supports an optional,
budget-limited exploration of whether its financial qual-
ity can be improved without changing the task require-
ments, data binding, or output protocol. Within this
loop, candidate variants must pass validation before be-
ing backtested on the optimization period and compared
under a preset objective. The selected candidate is then
evaluated once on a separate final period. This result
is not returned to the loop, and its financial values sup-
port quality analysis rather than determine engineering
acceptance. The candidate then proceeds to Judge re-
view with the corresponding validation and backtest
reports. The full optimization protocol is provided in
Appendix D.4.
Candidate review and Workflow resolution.After can-
didate development and testing are complete, the Work-
flow must decide whether the candidate can be accepted
or requires further action. QuantHarness assigns this
review to theJudge, which considers the candidate to-
gether with the artifacts and reports produced earlier
in the Workflow. The review leads to three routes: re-
covery, termination, or freezing. When the Judge identi-
fies a correctable problem, its report states which ear-
lier stage needs to be revisited. Recovery returns the
run to that stage, after which the affected downstream
stages are repeated within a fixed attempt budget. If the
problem cannot be resolved or the budget is exhausted,
the run terminates without an accepted strategy. If the
available evidence supports acceptance, freezing creates
afrozen strategy artifactthat preserves the candidate
4
implementation and the evidence supporting its accep-
tance. Harness Core checks the evidence supporting
the Judge’s recommendation before it allows the corre-
sponding change to take effect.
3.3 Harness Core: Runtime Mediation
and Governance
The Workflow organizes strategy development into
stages, but stage order alone does not control how each
stage operates or how its results affect later stages.
QuantHarness therefore introduces Harness Core, a
runtime control layer that mediates stage execution
across the Workflow. Theinformation boundarydeter-
mines which existing run information is supplied to a
stage. A separateaction boundarylimits the external
capabilities that the stage may invoke. Once a stage
produces an output, thecommitment boundarydeter-
mines whether later stages may rely on it. Across these
three boundaries, Harness Core preserves the artifacts
and lifecycle decisions needed to explain how the run
progressed.
Providing stage-specific context.Each Workflow stage
requires information tailored to its current task. The full
history mixes current artifacts with superseded can-
didates, evidence from earlier failures, and control or
audit metadata. Because the relevance of this material
changes across stages, supplying the full history would
leave the agent to determine what remains valid. Har-
ness Core instead constructs a distinctRuntime Card
for each agent-facing stage. Its agent-visible portion
states the stage goal, required outputs, and applicable
constraints, together with the task information and feed-
back relevant at that point in the run. Control and au-
dit information remains private to Harness Core. This
stage-specific selection gives the agent a defined context
without requiring it to interpret the full run history.
Mediating tools and external effects.Not every exter-
nal capability is appropriate at every Workflow stage.
Unrestricted access could allow a stage to trigger an
operation intended for another part of the Workflow or
to act before its required inputs exist. QuantHarness en-
forces the action boundary by representing each agent-
accessible capability as atoolmediated by Harness Core.
Atool registrygives every tool a stable name and a de-
clared input structure. For each stage, Harness Core
exposes only the registered tools allowed for its cur-
rent task. Calls to these tools are checked against their
declared input structures and routed through Harness
Core to the corresponding capabilities. Tool access is
therefore enforced by Harness Core rather than left to
prompt instructions.
Authorizing and committing lifecycle changes.A
Workflow stage may recommend what should happen
next. Allowing this recommendation to change the
run could advance the Workflow before the required
outputs and evidence have been checked. QuantHarness
separates the stage’s proposal from the authorization
and state update performed by Harness Core. The
run’slifecycleis its progression through Workflow
stages from creation to a terminal result. Before a
proposed transition changes this lifecycle, Harness
Core intercepts it through alifecycle hook. Aguard
then checks whether the proposed route is allowed
and whether the artifacts and evidence required by the
target stage are available. The guard approves or rejects
the proposal without altering the run. On approval, the
runtimecommits the transition by updating the current
stage and making the outputs available downstream.
Preserving run evidence and artifacts.A final strategy
implementation and its backtest result do not explain
how a run reached its outcome. QuantHarness therefore
maintains anArtifact Storefor each run. During the run,
the store retains the Runtime Cards supplied to agent-
facing stages, tool-call outcomes, lifecycle decisions, and
the outputs produced at each stage, including outputs
that later fail a check. When the Workflow freezes a
candidate, the store links the final implementation to
the intermediate artifacts and decisions supporting its
acceptance. If no candidate is accepted, the store retains
the evidence showing where and why the run ended.
Post-run analyses can inspect these materials but can-
not modify them or feed new measurements back to the
Workflow. The Artifact Store thus preserves the termi-
nal outcome together with the intermediate evidence
needed to inspect how it was produced.
4 FinRelBench
4.1 Benchmark Objective
Reliable financial strategy development requires an
agent to preserve the user’s requirements throughout
requirement interpretation, data preparation, strategy
implementation, runtime verification, and final accep-
tance. A strategy may run successfully and produce
favorable backtest results even when its data processing
or trading logic departs from those requirements.
Evaluating this reliability therefore requires tasks that
connect explicit strategy requirements to verifiable
implementation and execution evidence. To meet this
need, we constructFinRelBench(Financial Strategy
Development Reliability Benchmark) to assess whether
agents translate stated requirements into correct data
processing, strategy implementations, and observed
runtime behavior.
5
FinRelBench assesses this translation at three comple-
mentary levels:executability, whether the data pipeline
and strategy run successfully;requirement satisfaction,
whether the implementation meets the stated data and
trading requirements; andruntime evidence, whether
execution records demonstrate the required behavior.
Together, these levels support end-to-end evaluation of
task completion. The same tasks also provide common
workloads for boundary-control experiments and audit
and replay studies, which examine how errors are con-
trolled during development and how outcomes can be
traced and reproduced.
4.2 Benchmark Composition
FinRelBench comprises 97 explicitly specified, rule-
based strategy-development tasks in the A-share equity
setting: 80 tasks selected and adapted from the 400-task
QuantCode-Bench [7], and 17 tasks of our own design.
We select source tasks whose core trading logic can
be expressed as verifiable requirements using the
available market data under long-only equity trading.
Tasks whose defining requirements depend on short
selling, derivatives, or unavailable external data are
excluded. Adaptation preserves the core trading logic
while aligning the stock universe, market data, and
execution timing with A-share trading rules, including
T+1 settlement.
The 80 adapted tasks cover trend and momentum,
breakouts, mean reversion, volatility and liquidity con-
ditions, and combinations of indicators, including rules
that depend on position state or calendar schedules. Un-
der QuantCode-Bench’s original difficulty labels, these
80 tasks comprise 41 easy, 29 medium, and 10 hard cases.
The 17 tasks of our own design test data adjustment
and temporal alignment, stock eligibility and portfo-
lio constraints, cash fallback, and execution timing. To-
gether, these tasks test whether agents preserve both
the specified trading logic and the data and execution
requirements needed to implement it correctly.
The combined suite contains 64 daily-score tasks
(50 adapted and 14 original) and 33 minute-order
tasks (30 adapted and three original). Daily-score tasks
assign scores to stocks for cross-sectional ranking
and portfolio construction, testing whether agents
correctly implement historical windows, ranking rules,
and portfolio constraints. Minute-order tasks produce
timestamped buy and sell instructions, testing bar
aggregation, decision windows, order delays, and
position-state transitions. These output types provide
complementary checks of how strategy requirements
are translated into data processing and actual trading
behavior. Task examples are provided in Appendix A.
FinRelBench evaluates reliability against explicit strat-
egy requirements. While reliable development provides
a basis for interpreting financial outcomes, practical use
in financial research also requires evidence of economi-
cally meaningful strategy outputs. To complement this
primary evaluation with evidence of practical financial
utility, we introduceFinResearchSuite(Financial Strat-
egy Development Research Suite). This auxiliary suite
contains 22 tasks: 12 open-ended rule-research tasks, 5
specified-model development tasks, and 5 open-ended
model-research tasks. It assesses research completion
and the return and risk of the resulting strategies to ex-
amine whether QuantHarness’s governed development
process can support economically meaningful strategy
research. Financial performance thus serves as an auxil-
iary check of basic research utility and does not enter
FinRelBench’s reliability scores. Task definitions and
results are provided in Appendices D and E.
4.3 Evaluation Protocol
Each FinRelBench case is a self-contained evaluation
package divided into agent-visible and evaluator-only
components. The agent-visible components are thepub-
lic task query,market snapshot, anddata and submis-
sion documentation. The evaluator-only components are
thetrade execution settingsandtask-specific rubric. The
public task querystates the task objective and public
constraints. Themarket snapshotprovides versioned,
read-only market data, while thedata and submission
documentationdescribes the tables, column definitions,
time coverage, and access paths, explaining how to ac-
cess the data and submit results. Thetrade execution
settingsspecify the test period, trade execution rules,
and transaction costs. Thetask-specific rubricdefines
the requirements used to assess the implementation and
its execution results. Because different implementations
can correctly fulfill the same request, these requirements
are expressed as checkable criteria. Data criteria exam-
ine whether the requested securities and fields are used,
enough past observations are available for the specified
calculations, and the prepared data are actually con-
sumed. Strategy criteria examine the requested signal
calculation, stock selection, and trading schedule. Crite-
ria labeledmustmust all be satisfied for minimum cor-
rectness, while partial satisfaction of supportingshould
criteria allows some implementation flexibility. The cri-
teria describe the expected data processing and strategy
behavior, while forbidden conditions identify behavior
that invalidates the result regardless of other require-
ments satisfied. For example, a strategy may implement
6
the requested trading rule but use future prices to gener-
ate earlier signals. Confirmed forbidden behavior there-
fore causes task failure, even when the implementation
passes execution checks and satisfies other criteria.
Deterministic mechanical checks establish whether
the agent’s code can construct the data and execute the
strategy needed for task completion. Systems provide
their code, data-construction and strategy-execution
commands, and saved data and outputs through a com-
mon interface. The evaluator runs these commands un-
der fixed market and execution conditions.Data Pipeline
Successrequires the dataset to be constructed, saved, and
consumed by the strategy;Strategy Run Successrequires
the strategy to complete execution and produce its de-
clared output.
A strategy can run successfully while using incorrect
data or trading rules, so an external panel uses the rubric
to check whether the implementation meets the task
requirements. The panel examines anonymized code,
summaries of the prepared datasets, execution records,
and outputs. For each criterion, it separately assesses
whether the requirement is satisfied and whether ex-
ecution evidence demonstrates the relevant behavior.
Runtime support requires both code on the actual execu-
tion path and corresponding evidence from the run or its
outputs. Independent evaluators assess two equivalent
wordings of the same rubric. Disagreements that can
affect the result are resolved by an arbiter who reviews
the evidence without seeing the earlier judgments.
These assessments jointly determineTask Success.
Both mechanical checks must pass, and each seman-
tic dimension must satisfy all must criteria, at least 75%
of should criteria rounded up, and no confirmed forbid-
den condition or independently confirmed fatal defect.
Additionally, every criterion derived from an explicit
public requirement must be satisfied and supported by
runtime evidence, including those marked should.
Alongside the two mechanical metrics, we reportRe-
quirement SatisfactionandRuntime-Evidence Coverage
separately for data and strategy, giving six diagnostics
in total. These criterion-based metrics measure the frac-
tions judged satisfied and supported by execution ev-
idence, respectively, with equal weight for must and
should criteria. Section 5.2 specifies how task-level out-
comes and criterion-level judgments are aggregated for
the reported comparison. Appendix A provides the de-
tailed rubric, evidence rules, and worked examples.
5 Experiments
5.1 Experimental Setup
Our evaluation is designed to establish three comple-
mentary forms of reliability evidence. We first test
whether QuantHarness, as a complete system, can con-
struct executable strategies that use the required data
and realize the requested behavior. We then examine
how individual Harness Core boundaries affect reliable
completion and violation containment, followed by an
evaluation of whether structured run records support
post-run audit and decision replay. FinResearchSuite
separately assesses completion and financial utility in
open-ended rule and model research.
For strategy construction and research, we compare
QuantHarness with Codex CLI [13], OpenHands [17],
SWE-agent [24], and Aider [5]. All strategy-generation
calls use GPT-5.5 [14]. Within each task, these systems
receive the same public request, frozen market data, and
documentation, while retaining their native agent loops,
tools, and recovery policies.
To compare systems with different internal work-
flows, FinRelBench evaluates task completion using a
common set of materials. Each system provides its code,
commands for preparing data and running the strategy,
and saved data and outputs. These materials allow the
evaluator to apply the mechanical and semantic checks
defined in Section 4.3, without requiring systems to re-
produce QuantHarness’s workflow stages or internal
record formats. Within each comparison, a deterministic
A-share backtester uses shared signal timing, transac-
tion costs, and trading constraints. Software versions,
resource limits, data windows, and execution parame-
ters are specified in Appendices F and D.2.
For FinRelBench semantic assessment, Claude Opus
4.6 [1] and Gemini 3.1 Pro [6] independently examine
anonymized evidence under two equivalent rubric word-
ings. DeepSeek V4 Pro [3] blindly adjudicates disagree-
ments that can change the outcome. These external
assessments provide no feedback for another develop-
ment attempt. Appendix A details the panel procedure,
and Appendix B documents the implementation and
evidence-collection configurations represented in the
reported comparison.
5.2 Strategy Development Reliability
A strategy may execute successfully while using unsuit-
able data, implementing a different trading rule, or leav-
ing required behavior unverified. We evaluate this relia-
bility gap on all 97 FinRelBench tasks, asking whether
each system carries an explicit request through data
preparation and strategy implementation to a verifiable
final result. Task Success is the primary measure: within
the same task, both mechanical checks and minimum
semantic gates must pass, and every explicit public re-
quirement must be satisfied and supported by runtime
evidence, as defined in Section 4.3.
7
Task Success and the two mechanical metrics are pass
rates over all 97 planned tasks. Requirement Satisfac-
tion and Runtime-Evidence Coverage measure the frac-
tions of criteria satisfied and supported by execution
evidence within each task and dimension. These frac-
tions are averaged across tasks, giving each task equal
weight regardless of how many criteria its rubric con-
tains. Completed diagnostic assessments retain their
scores even when Task Success is zero, showing which
requirements were fulfilled within otherwise unsuccess-
ful tasks. Incomplete assessments are distinguished from
confirmed failures in Appendix B.

cess but 84.54% Task Success; the case-level breakdown
identifies five mechanically completed tasks with unmet
or unverifiable requirements and four with missing run-

ten mechanically completed tasks with unmet or unver-

criterion scores do not imply complete requirement
fulfillment within individual tasks. These differences
are consistent with QuantHarness’s Workflow: the
strategy specification makes the request explicit, data
grounding checks the inputs before implementation,
and validation and review connect candidate acceptance
to supporting artifacts.
Two QuantHarness cases illustrate the difference be-
tween an unmet requirement and missing runtime evi-
dence. In the industry-aware multifactor task, selection
without industry constraints is permitted when industry
labels are unavailable, provided that this limitation is
reported. The code implements the fallback but omits
the required reporting, leaving an explicit requirement
unmet. In the relative-strength task, the strategy imple-
ments a cash fallback when no stock passes the liquidity
and trend filters. Both strategies pass validation and exe-
cution, but one omits required reporting while the other
leaves a conditional behavior unverified. Further details
are provided in Appendices A and B.

Stress Tests
The complete-system comparison shows QuantHar-
ness’s overall reliability, but not which parts of Harness
Core produce it. We therefore examine how the Harness
selects information for each stage and controls tool use
and changes to the run. For each test, we compare the
system with the target boundary enabled against its
ablated configuration, while keeping the task, shared
inputs, and remaining boundary settings fixed. We
report paired results on a fixed subset of 30 FinRelBench
tasks, selected before the experiments to cover diverse
strategy rules and both daily-score and minute-order
outputs.
In normal runs, the Information Boundary controls
the history given to the Agent; the Action boundary
limits its tools and checks calls before execution. For
the Information ablation, both variants draw from the
same source run history and receive the same input-
length budget: with the boundary enabled, the Agent
receives the requirements and implementation decisions
that still apply; when it is weakened, the Agent receives
a chronological slice and must decide for itself which
information remains valid. With Action enabled, the
Agent sees only the tools needed at that stage and calls
are checked; after ablation, the Agent sees more tools,
receives no guidance, and faces fewer checks. Table 2
Panel A shows that, with the Information Boundary
enabled, 25/30 initial implementations execute success-
fully and satisfy the public requirements, compared with
23/30 under ablation. With the Action boundary enabled,
all 30 Workflows complete correctly, compared with
23/30 under ablation, where failures include invalid data
bindings and empty outputs. Together, these results sug-
gest that stage-appropriate information and coordinated
tool guidance and execution checks help agents preserve
task requirements and carry development through to
executable strategies.

6 Limitations and Future Work
Using GPT-5.5 as a shared generation backbone helps
distinguish system-level differences from variation due
to model choice. This common setting supports a con-
trolled comparison, although the findings remain spe-
cific to a single generation model. Broader model cover-
age would require substantial additional resources, as
each task involves a complete development trajectory
with multiple stages of generation and evaluation. Ex-
tending the study to other model families and capability
levels would help establish whether the observed relia-
bility gains generalize and clarify the trade-off between
reliable completion and inference cost.
FinRelBench currently evaluates explicitly specified
rule-based tasks in a long-only A-share environment
through daily-score and minute-order interfaces. These
tasks connect strategy requirements to verifiable data
processing and execution behavior under a shared mar-
ket protocol. Extending evaluation to other markets, as-
set classes, and more open-ended research tasks would
test development reliability under different data con-
ventions and trading constraints. Additional historical
periods would further broaden the conditions covered
by this evaluation.
Our evaluation of Harness Core focuses on gover-
nance within individual development trajectories. Build-
ing on the retained run records, a further direction is gov-
erned experience reuse across tasks and runs. A mem-
ory module could enable later runs to reuse verified
data bindings, recurring failure patterns, and successful
repair procedures, with Harness Core mediating mem-
ory writes and retrieval. Retrieved experience would
remain linked to its source evidence and be checked for
compatibility with the current task and market context
before use. Evaluating this extension would examine
whether experience reuse improves Task Success and ef-
ficiency while preserving traceability and the separation
between development and held-out evaluation.
7 Conclusion
This paper presents QuantHarness, a two-layer harness
for governing the development trajectory of LLM-driven
quantitative strategies. Its finance-specific Workflow
structures strategy construction and evaluation, while
Harness Core mediates information handoff, tool ac-
tions, and lifecycle commitments through the Informa-
tion, Action, and Commitment boundaries. An artifact-
backed run record links each terminal outcome to the ev-
idence and decisions that produced it, and FinRelBench
evaluates strategy-development reliability through ex-
ecutability, requirement satisfaction, and runtime evi-
dence. The experiments show improved reliable comple-
tion on FinRelBench, containment of targeted workflow
violations, and stronger evidence recovery and deci-
sion replay from structured provenance. Together, these
results expose the gap between producing a runnable
strategy and establishing that the requested strategy
was faithfully developed through an inspectable process.
QuantHarness operationalizes a reliability-first view in
which strategy development is assessed through task
fidelity, process control, and supporting evidence, pro-
viding a systems foundation for more rigorous financial-
agent research.

A FinRelBench Details and Evaluation Protocol
FinRelBench evaluates whether a strategy-development system turns an explicit request into an executable
implementation supported by requirement-level evidence. This appendix describes the task coverage,
scoring rules, worked examples, and external evaluation procedure behind the main comparison.
A.1 Benchmark Composition and Coverage
FinRelBench contains 97 explicit rule-based tasks: 64 daily-score tasks and 33 intraday-order tasks. Each
public case contains an identifier, a natural-language query, and an output type. The 22 auxiliary research
tasks in Appendix D are evaluated separately, not pooled with this benchmark.
Original tasks.The fourteen daily-score cases cover moving-average crossover, filtered reversal, medium-
term risk-adjusted momentum, volatility-regime mean reversion, multifactor selection, industry-aware
multifactor selection, low-volatility portfolio construction, momentum with volatility and cash gating,
relative-strength rotation, index-gated selection, price-limit-distance filtering, gap reversal, factor-bucket ro-
tation, and rolling-pattern ranking. The three intraday-order cases cover opening-range breakout, execution-
aware target orders, and minute-level liquidity-shock reversal. These tasks introduce explicit requirements
concerning adjusted prices, security and timestamp identity, tradability, regime filters, missing metadata,
and cash fallback.
Adapted tasks and scope.The remaining 80 tasks contain 50 daily-score and 30 intraday-order requests,
adapted fromQuantCode-Bench, distributed under the MIT license. Selection retains explicit rule-based
strategies whose core trading logic can be expressed as verifiable requirements using the available daily or
minute equity data under long-only trading. Tasks whose defining requirements depend on unavailable
inputs, non-equity instruments, short selling, or machine learning are excluded. Adaptation preserves the
core trading logic and retains numerical parameters where compatible, while aligning the stock universe,
data interfaces, and execution timing with A-share trading rules, including T+1 settlement.
The adapted tasks broaden indicator combinations, lookbacks, stateful entry and exit rules, and multi-
frequency timing. Examples include 252-session absolute momentum, volume-and-RSI breakouts, re-entry
following an earlier exit, Donchian contraction breakouts, and session-VWAP reversals. Minute tasks
may construct session-aligned 5-, 15-, or 30-minute aggregates but still emit orders through the same
latency-constrained interface.

A.2 Rubric Construction and Reliability Endpoints
To assess whether an implementation fulfills a public request, we use a task-specificrubric: a structured
evaluation specification that maps the request and the validity conditions needed for correct implementation
to criteria and disqualifying conditions. For task𝑖, we writeR𝑖 =(C 𝑑𝑖 ,C 𝑠𝑖,F 𝑑𝑖 ,F 𝑠𝑖), where𝑑 and𝑠 denote
the data and strategy dimensions,C contains criteria, andF contains predefined forbidden conditions.
Data criteria concern security and timestamp identity, required inputs, temporal coverage, and their actual
use; strategy criteria concern signals, portfolios, timing, and execution. Forbidden conditions identify
invalid behavior, such as using information unavailable at decision time.
For each task, we decompose the public request into independently assessable data and strategy obliga-
tions. Compound instructions are separated when their components can be satisfied or violated individually;
for example, transaction costs and slippage are assessed as separate criteria. Conditional obligations retain
both the trigger and its required response, so decomposition preserves the meaning of the requested
behavior. Each criterion has an identifier and is traceable either to an explicit public requirement or to a
necessary semantic precondition—a validity condition required for correct implementation of the task. Each
added precondition records a rationale explaining its necessity, such as sufficient historical observations to
compute a requested moving average.
To reflect each criterion’s importance to the requested strategy and the validity of its inputs, we assign a
task-specificmustorshouldlevel. Must criteria capture requirements treated as indispensable to the task’s
defining data or trading semantics and must all hold for minimum semantic acceptance. Should criteria
capture supporting requirements that refine the implementation; partial satisfaction is permitted at this
minimum gate. This distinction sets the minimum acceptance standard without changing the requirements
stated in the public request. For each dimension𝑘∈{𝑑,𝑠} ,C𝑘𝑖 is partitioned into the disjoint sets of must
criteriaM𝑘𝑖 and should criteriaH 𝑘𝑖 . These levels determine the minimum semantic gate formalized below.
Each dimension contains at least one must criterion, so|C 𝑘𝑖|> 0. IfH 𝑘𝑖 is empty, the should-satisfaction
condition is automatically met.
To assess whether judgments remain stable when only wording changes, each criterion has two se-
mantically equivalent statements. These rephrase the same atomic requirement without changing what is
assessed, when it applies, the required behavior, or its numerical constraints. For illustration, “The portfolio
holds at most 20 securities” and “The number of securities held in the portfolio does not exceed 20” express
the same constraint: the criterion identifier, must/should level, and 20-security limit remain unchanged. To
check rubric completeness and source traceability, structural validation verifies task coverage, identifiers
and links, and exact matches between source quotations and public requests. The two statements share one
criterion identifier and level, while automated checks verify numerical consistency between them. Source
quotations remain verbatim even when the criterion wording changes. These checks establish structural
and numerical consistency, not semantic equivalence by themselves. The rubric definitions are frozen
before evaluation; Appendix A.4 describes how the two wordings are used in the evaluation procedure.
To identify which criteria directly encode requirements explicitly stated in task𝑖’s public request, we
defineE𝑖⊆C 𝑑𝑖 ∪C 𝑠𝑖 as the subset of all such data and strategy criteria. The criteria included in E𝑖 are
determined during rubric construction, before any system output is assessed. Each criterion inE𝑖 is linked
to a verbatim quotation from the public request. The same criterion retains its identifier in both equivalent
forms of the rubric, allowing its judgments to be aligned across the two wordings. Criteria added solely as
necessary semantic preconditions are excluded fromE𝑖, although they remain inC𝑑𝑖 orC𝑠𝑖 for semantic
14
evaluation. For example, when the public request explicitly requires adjusted prices, the corresponding
input criterion belongs toE𝑖, whereas a general check of security and timestamp identity introduced only
as a validity precondition does not. Membership inE𝑖 identifies a criterion’s origin within the full criterion
set, whereas its must/should level specifies its role in the minimum semantic gate. Membership is fixed
during rubric construction and does not change with the observed run outcome. A criterion corresponding
to an explicit conditional requirement remains inE𝑖 even when the triggering condition is not observed
during the run. For complete fulfillment of the public request, Task Success requires every criterion inE𝑖
to be both satisfied and supported by runtime evidence, as formalized below.
For every criterion in C𝑑𝑖 ∪C 𝑠𝑖 , we record two assessments: semantic status evaluates whether the
requirement is met, while evidence strength distinguishes runtime support for the relevant behavior from
code or declarations alone. An individual reviewer assigns semantic status asSatisfiedwhen evidence
supports fulfillment,Not Satisfiedwhen evidence shows contradiction or omission, andNot Verifiable
when evidence is insufficient or ambiguous. Evidence strength records the implementation support available
for the criterion, using the labelsOperationalized,Code Only,Declared Only, andNot Verifiable.
Operationalized evidence requires executable code on the actual launch path together with execution
or artifact evidence of the relevant behavior. Code Only denotes a visible implementation without a
corresponding runtime witness. Declared Only denotes an unsupported claim in comments or metadata, or
a setting unconnected to execution. Not Verifiable evidence strength denotes the absence of defensible
implementation evidence.
Data and strategy assessments attribute defects to the relevant semantic dimension, avoiding duplicate
semantic penalties for the same missing input. Insufficient data do not by themselves invalidate strategy
logic correctly expressed in code, although they may limit runtime evidence. For a strategy criterion that
explicitly requires observed end-to-end behavior, code-level correctness alone is insufficient to establish
satisfaction.

A.4 External Panel Resolution and Calibration
The criterion-level judgments illustrated above are produced by an external benchmark panel, separate from
the internal Workflow Judge. The internal Judge recommends acceptance or recovery during development;
the external panel evaluates submitted evidence without feeding its judgments back into further candidate
development.
The panel uses the two equivalent rubric wordings described in Appendix A.2 and frozen before evaluation
to assess the stability of judgments across equivalent formulations. Under each wording, Claude Opus
4.6 (claude-opus-4-6) and Gemini 3.1 Pro (gemini-3.1-pro-preview) independently assess the same
anonymized evidence. They return criterion-level semantic status, evidence strength, and evidence locators
rather than an overall pass or score.
When these independent judgments disagree on decisions relevant to minimum semantic acceptance
or complete fulfillment of the public request, DeepSeek V4 Pro ( deepseek-v4-pro) provides targeted
arbitration. Within a wording, the overlapping triggers concern must-criterion satisfaction; whether the
number of satisfied should criteria reaches the fixed threshold while minimum acceptance remains possible;
the semantic status or evidence strength of a criterion in the explicit-requirement setE𝑖; or whether a
predefined forbidden condition is present. For criteria not sent to arbitration, an agreed semantic status is
retained; otherwise, the status isDisagreed. In either case, the weaker of the two evidence-strength labels
is retained.
After this within-wording aggregation, the same arbiter addresses any remaining differences between
the two sets of results. When a criterion has the same semantic status and evidence strength under both
wordings, those labels are retained, including a shared Disagreed status. In both arbitration stages, the
arbiter judges the original evidence without seeing previous conclusions. An unlisted fatal defect, as
defined in Appendix A.2, additionally requires blind confirmation as an independent defect in the relevant
dimension, not already covered by a listed criterion or forbidden condition. The final labels and execution
checks then determine the reliability endpoints using the fixed formulas in that appendix; any criterion
with a final Disagreed status receives zero credit in both RS and REC.
To check whether this review procedure identifies fulfilled requirements and defects as intended, we
use controlled cases with known expected judgments. Calibration here means verification against these
expected labels. The 17 anonymizedstatic fixturesare preconstructed, fixed evidence bundles containing
public requests, code excerpts, dataset and output profiles, and execution records, with expected judgments
specified separately. Four positive controls provide implementations expected to satisfy the requirements,
covering daily crossover, opening-range breakout, adapted daily momentum, and adapted intraday breakout.
Thirteen negative controls introduce defects in three groups: data adequacy and identity (insufficient history,
incorrect observation identity, collapsed entities, unusable timestamps, unadjusted prices, and incorrect
intraday frequency); strategy logic and timing (reversed rules, entry during range formation, an off-by-one
lag, and omission of the required pre-entry channel-contraction condition); and execution constraints
(same-session daily execution, T+1 violation, and zero execution latency).
For example, the insufficient-history fixture supplies 30 observations for a 60-session requirement. The
long-window average remains undefined, while the supplied execution record reports normal termination
18
with an empty output. This tests whether the panel attributes the defect to inadequate data coverage. Across
the full fixture set, the final-panel checks require agreement with the expected must statuses, specified
evidence strengths for must criteria, forbidden-condition and unlisted-fatal decisions, and, where specified,
whether all explicit requirements are satisfied and operationalized. The saved reports record successful
completion of these predefined core checks across the 17 fixtures, with should-label comparisons reported
separately.
To make these checks traceable to the evaluated configuration, each validation report records hashes
of the protocol, rubric, judge configuration, output schema, instructions, and fixture bundle. Appendix B
complements these controlled checks with panel consistency and arbitration statistics on benchmark
submissions. The next subsection describes how evidence from the evaluated systems’ benchmark runs is
collected and organized for this review.
A.5 Evidence Collection
All evaluated systems follow the same neutral submission rules: declared data-construction and strategy-
execution commands, persisted datasets and outputs, code on the actual launch path, and captured execution
evidence. These requirements do not oblige other systems to emit QuantHarness stage names or internal
contracts.
The evaluator organizes these materials into an anonymized, structured evidence pack, supplied to
the panel together with the task requirements and rubric. Dataset profiles, observed file access, executed
outputs, and code references carry evidence labels and locators, allowing criterion-level judgments to point
to specific code or records. Where orders, fills, fees, and holdings are exported from an existing native run,
the export protocol uses code and artifact hashes, invocation settings, and backtest metadata to associate
the records with their originating execution. This association preserves their status as evidence from the
original execution: exporting or linking the records does not constitute an independent replay or by itself
establish that a strategy requirement is satisfied. Self-reported fields provide auxiliary context rather than
substitutes for actual execution materials. The same distinction between reported claims and observed
evidence applies to every system.
An interruption of the evidence-collection infrastructure leaves the assessment incomplete, rather than
confirming a strategy failure. Appendix A.6 specifies how completed and incomplete task assessments
enter the aggregate metrics.
A.6 Aggregation and Missing Outcomes
System-level results aggregate the task-level endpoints in Appendix A.2 across all 97 planned cases for
every system. Data Pipeline Success, Strategy Run Success, and Task Success are each the number of tasks
passing the corresponding check divided by 97. Requirement Satisfaction (RS) and Runtime-Evidence
Coverage (REC) use the task-level fractions defined in Eq. 3, separately for the data and strategy dimensions.
Each system-level value is the sum of the corresponding task values divided by 97, with unavailable values
treated as specified below. This is an unweighted task-macro average: cases with longer rubrics do not
receive greater weight. Must and should criteria have equal diagnostic weight within a case, without
changing their roles in the minimum semantic gate. For illustration, within one dimension, complete
coverage on a four-criterion case and coverage of four of eight criteria on another case yield a two-case
mean of 75%, not the pooled-criterion value of 8/12.
Eligibility for external semantic assessment requires successful generation, a valid submission, and an
execution-check record without infrastructure errors; it does not require either mechanical check to pass.
This run-level eligibility check is distinct from the dimension-level reviewability conditions in Appendix A.2,
which concern the minimum evidence available for assessment. Completed, eligible semantic assessments
retain their measured RS and REC values even when Task Success is zero. A completed system failure that
19
prevents semantic assessment, such as generation failure or an invalid submission, contributes zero to
these diagnostics rather than removing the task from their denominators.
Eligibility does not guarantee completion: an external assessment is incomplete when evidence collection
or required panel reviews remain unfinished. Such tasks receive no Task Success credit and contribute
zero to RS and REC, while their observed Data Pipeline Success and Strategy Run Success outcomes are
retained. They remain in all planned denominators and are reported separately from confirmed strategy
failures, so missing evaluation is not interpreted as evidence of an incorrect strategy. Appendix B reports
the corresponding counts and task-group results.

The experiments separate three questions: how runtime boundaries affect ordinary development, whether

C.4 Audit Corpus and Evidence Views
The Commitment stress test above asks whether the system prevents a modified candidate from being
frozen under an earlier approval. We next examine whether the saved records allow an auditor to recover
the evidence needed to understand what happened during development and how the terminal authorization
outcome arose.
Each audit record links a task’s saved development history to one post-Judge branch of that stress test.
The corpus contains 44 records from the same 30 tasks: sixteen in which the candidate remains unchanged
after approval and is frozen (clean); fourteen in which a post-approval replacement is prevented from being
frozen (contained); and fourteen in which the replacement is frozen under the earlier approval (escaped).
Following the prespecified replacement assignment, the unmodified tasks contribute their Full branches,
while the replacement tasks contribute both their Full (contained) and Off (escaped) branches. Selection
does not depend on audit or replay outcomes.
The audit comparison holds each recorded process and outcome fixed and varies the evidence available to
the auditor. Each record is presented through three evidence views—organized, flat, and final-only—which
specify the artifacts and organizational information available, giving 132 record–view audits.
For each record, organized and flat views retain the same visible artifact contents, hashes, and order-
ing under a shared 240,000-character artifact-content budget. The organized view additionally provides
descriptive file paths and metadata, links between related artifacts, and an index organized by evidence
role. A role identifies the type of evidence sought, such as a validation report or the approved source code.
Flat replaces the descriptive paths with numbered item identifiers and removes the explicit structure; its
underlying file contents remain searchable and readable. These visible paths or item identifiers serve as
locators for accessing artifacts. The final-only view exposes the terminal candidate and final summary
artifacts, omitting intermediate process and governance evidence.
The auditor is asked to locate each requested item of evidence and return its locator and visible-content
hash. It also reports findings about the first process failure, if any, the Judge proposal, authorization and
seal status, and the identities of the approved, live, and frozen candidates. The auditor uses GPT-5.5 at
temperature 0.0 with up to twelve evidence-tool calls per record–view audit. All views support listing,
content search, and reading by locator. Only the organized view supports batch lookup by evidence role,
26
returning locators and hashes for multiple requested roles in a single budgeted call. Access is restricted to
the assigned view, without preassigned outcome-category labels or access to the original run directories.
The organized–flat comparison therefore evaluates structured evidence organization together with its
retrieval interface under this call budget; comparison with final-only additionally examines evidence
retention.
To compare how well the views support evidence recovery, we report Exact audit evidence, the fraction
of required audit roles that the auditor recovers accurately. A role counts as accurately recovered only
when the returned artifact locator is correct and the hash matches its visible content. For each record, the
score is the number of accurately recovered required roles divided by the number of audit roles required
for that source record. The reported value is the unweighted mean of these fractions over all 44 records in
a view.
For this metric, twelve evidence roles are required for every record: process trace, Runtime Card, validation
report, backtest report, Judge report, Judge proposal, commitment seal, seal-verification result, governance
events, run metric, accepted code, and live code. Frozen hashes form a thirteenth required role when the
source record contains a frozen candidate; contained records without one do not require this role. Any
role required for a source record remains required in all three views, even if a view does not expose it.
Recovering these audit roles does not by itself establish that the recorded outcomes can be reproduced.
The auditor also searches for additional inputs, including task specifications, data bindings, and execution
configurations, to support the validation, backtesting, and Commitment reconstruction described next.
C.5 Replay Inputs, Execution, and Reconstruction
Replay uses the retrieved evidence to check two linked outcomes: validation and backtesting of the approved
candidate, and authorization of the candidate presented for Commitment. The acceptance component
requires ten roles: task contract, strategy specification, blueprint, and implementation plan describe the
task and design; data binding, field contract, implementation metadata, and accepted code identify the
candidate and its data constraints; validation and backtest configurations specify execution. After resolving
locators and verifying hashes, the runner restores these inputs in a fresh run directory, reruns validation
and, if it passes, the configured backtest using the separately configured frozen market-data snapshot.
It compares the content hashes of the candidate code, data binding, and field contract with those in the
source record. The comparison also covers the validation outcome, backtest status, and output protocol.
The Commitment component instead reconstructs the authorization outcome for the live candidate
at commitment time. It resolves live code, the saved Judge proposal, commitment seal, seal verification,
governance events, run metric, and frozen hashes where a frozen candidate exists. It recomputes the live-
code identity and compares it with the seal, while using recorded verification evidence for the supporting
artifacts covered by the seal. The reconstruction checks authorization and terminal candidate identity
under the recorded enforcement condition. The saved Judge proposal is an input; Judge review itself is not
rerun.
Each component can proceed only with a complete input packet: all of its required artifacts must be
available through the retrieved locators with matching visible-content hashes. The replay input interface
accepts either descriptive file paths or numbered item identifiers and restores the required inputs in the
same canonical workspace. All views use the same completeness and execution checks.
A replay is verified only when both components match the source record’s specified outcomes and
candidate identities. This includes reproducing normal authorization and freezing for clean records, denied
authorization without freezing for contained records, and unsupported freezing under Off for escaped
records. Verification concerns these execution and authorization outcomes and artifact identities; it does
not require byte-identical trading outputs or identical financial metrics.

D FinResearchSuite
D.1 Task Composition and Interfaces
FinResearchSuite complements FinRelBench by assessing research-task completion and financial utility;
these results are not pooled into the FinRelBench reliability score. It contains 22 auxiliary tasks in three
groups: open-ended rule research, specified model development, and open-ended model research. The
groups differ in whether the strategies are rule- or model-based and in how much of the design is specified
by the public request. They are evaluated separately.
Across the three groups, each public case contains an identifier, a natural-language query, and a required
output type. Daily scores support stock ranking and portfolio construction, whereas intraday orders specify
timestamped buy and sell instructions.
RuleResearchBench (12 open-ended rule cases).Each request fixes a research objective and public con-
straints while leaving the detailed signal construction, parameter choices, and portfolio design to the system
within those constraints. The ten daily-score cases cover trend–value blending, market-regime rotation,
liquidity-quality selection, reversal with risk controls, low-volatility and momentum mixing, price-limit-
aware alpha, industry-balanced alpha, gap–volatility alpha, cash-gated relative strength, and factor-bucket
ensembling. Two additional cases require intraday orders for liquidity reversal and breakout-or-reversion
research.
ModelBench (5 explicit model cases).Each case specifies the model to implement, with the five cases
covering LightGBM, gradient-boosted decision trees, a multilayer perceptron, a long short-term memory
network, and a Transformer encoder. The public requests also specify input features or feature groups,
prediction targets, chronological train/validation/test separation, and daily-score output; some also fix
sequence lengths, architecture settings, or portfolio constraints.
ModelResearchBench (5 open-ended model cases).In contrast to ModelBench, these requests set a research
direction and any model-family requirements while leaving more of the design to the system. Within
the public constraints, the system selects the model or its configuration, feature set, preprocessing, and
training design. The five cases cover tabular, tree/boosting, neural, sequential, and Transformer-style alpha
research. All five cases require daily-score output.

D.3 Completion and Financial Metrics
A task is counted as completed only when it produces a final-period backtest. For each system, completion
uses all planned tasks as its denominator: 12 for open-ended rule research and five for each model group.
The rule group is further separated into ten daily-score tasks and two intraday-order tasks, with completion
reported separately for these two output types.
Financial summaries cover the daily rule tasks and the two model groups; intraday-order tasks are
reported only through completion counts. For each system, financial metrics are computed per completed
task and averaged with equal task weight within the corresponding group. Tasks without a final-period
backtest remain in the completion denominator but are excluded from financial means, rather than being

D.4 QuantHarness Optimization and Candidate Selection
This subsection details how QuantHarness develops and selects candidates for the final evaluation in
FinResearchSuite. The optimization and selection rules below are specific to QuantHarness; the shared
evaluation protocol is described in Appendix D.2.
Passing validation and backtesting establishes that a candidate can be executed with the bound inputs
and declared output protocol. At this point, QuantHarness supports an optional, budget-limited exploration
of whether its financial quality can be improved while preserving the task requirements. The Workflow can
complete without this loop. During optimization, the task requirements, data binding, and output protocol
remain unchanged. Repair addresses execution or interface errors; optimization explores improvements to
an executable candidate, and the two budgets are not interchangeable.
Protocol and candidate proposals.The optimization budget and financial-quality objective are fixed before
the run. In FinResearchSuite, QuantHarness allows at most three optimization evaluations and one final
submission per task. Market data are divided into non-overlapping optimization and final evaluation
periods; during candidate development, model-based strategies fit on an earlier training period.
30
Within this protocol, the proposing agent receives the permitted changes and evaluation settings, the
current candidate, and optimization-period feedback, not final-period results. A proposal must name an
allowed change and remain consistent with the task, data binding, and output protocol. The proposal guard
checks whether the requested change is permitted before development; the variant guard then checks the
resulting candidate against the proposal’s constraints and the fixed task and data requirements. Model-
based variants also produce the training and inference artifacts required for validation and backtesting.
Each variant must pass validation before backtesting on the same optimization period.
Budget accounting and evidence.To keep exploration within the declared budget, the Workflow tracks
candidate identities, proposal iterations, optimization evaluations, repairs, final submissions, and final
evaluation attempts separately. An append-only budget ledger records evaluation starts and completions,
candidate identity, and submission status. Starting an evaluation consumes its applicable allowance, even if
the attempt subsequently fails.
To identify duplicate candidates, hashes are computed from the designated files that define the strategy
and its implementation, including model configuration where applicable. A duplicate candidate does not
receive a new independent evaluation. Instead, it may reuse a recorded result only if that result comes from
a completed evaluation of the same candidate under a matching recorded execution protocol. Incomplete
or incompatible cached evidence cannot make a candidate eligible for selection.
Stopping and selection.Exploration ends and the Workflow proceeds to selection when the configured
iteration, candidate, or evaluation budget is exhausted, or when no actionable improvement or further
admissible proposal is available. Rejection of a new proposal, for example because it attempts to change
the fixed data environment, blocks that proposal and can end exploration without invalidating previously
eligible candidates.
By contrast, a run-level protocol violation, such as attempting to evaluate an unregistered candidate
or replace an already registered selection with another candidate, terminates the run without a valid
final submission. When the protocol remains valid, selection considers both the initial candidate and its
revisions. Eligibility requires the applicable validation and backtest checks, not just a favorable financial
number. If no eligible candidate remains, the run terminates without a valid final submission. Otherwise,
the eligible candidates are compared under the fixed objective using optimization-period evidence. The
selected candidate’s identity, the ranking, and the record of its final submission are retained.
In FinResearchSuite, QuantHarness uses a composite financial score rather than return alone. For the
eligible candidates in the reported runs, the score adds annualized return and 0.5 times the Sharpe ratio,
then subtracts 0.5 times absolute maximum drawdown and 0.05 times cumulative turnover. Returns and
drawdowns enter as fractions; the metric conventions are defined in Appendix D.3. Rule-task scoring adds
0.01 per executed fill, capped at 0.1. Model-task scoring halves this fill-count bonus and adds 0.05 times
the fill rate, the ratio of executed to requested shares, clipped to[0, 1]. Turnover above 2 incurs a further
penalty of 0.25.
Rule-research candidates are ranked by this score over the optimization period. Model candidates instead
require complete evidence for four monthly subperiods within that window, September–December 2025,
with at least ten daily observations per subperiod. Their ranking score is the median monthly score minus
half the population standard deviation of the four scores and half the difference between the median and
worst monthly scores. A model variant must also outperform the candidate it revises on at least three of
the four monthly scores to remain eligible. Selection takes the highest-ranked eligible candidate; equal
scores retain candidate registration order. These calculations use only optimization-period evidence.
Final evaluation and engineering acceptance.After selection, the candidate’s design is held fixed for a
single evaluation on the final period. For QuantHarness’s model tasks in FinResearchSuite, preparation
for this evaluation includes refitting the selected model on the training and optimization periods and
31
generating predictions for the final period, without revising the selected design. The final attempt is
registered before execution; failure does not authorize reselection or a second final attempt. Final financial
results are retained for quality analysis and are not returned to optimization or used to retune the strategy.
Their magnitude does not determine engineering acceptance.
The candidate then proceeds to Judge review with the corresponding validation and backtest reports.
Harness Core checks the evidence supporting the Judge’s recommended route before allowing it to take
effect. Formal Workflow freezing follows an authorized acceptance decision and preserves the candidate
together with its supporting artifacts; holding a candidate fixed for evaluation does not itself constitute
freezing. Experimental windows and generation budgets are specified in Appendix D.2.
Full financial results and their interpretation are provided in Appendix E.

F.4 Market and Backtest Protocol
All reported financial outcomes are recomputed by one deterministic A-share evaluator rather than taken
from a system’s native backtester. This keeps the price basis, transaction costs, liquidity limits, and market
rules fixed within each comparison.
The FinRelBench snapshot uses the CSI 500 constituent list dated 2025-12-31, with daily coverage from
2025-01-02 through 2026-05-29 and minute coverage from 2026-04-29 through 2026-05-29. The provider is
Tushare; snapshot manifests record checksums that identify the exported clean and quantitative data files.
Individual tasks request the history they need within this coverage. Daily history before the evaluation
window provides warmup observations for computing the task’s signals. When a task requests adjusted
prices, evaluation inspects both the adjustment-factor inputs and the code that computes those prices. The
separate boundary tests use a frozen 100-name CSI 100 universe for checking whether a candidate passes
backtesting.
FinResearchSuite’s chronological windows and CSI 500 constituent snapshots are specified in Appen-
dix D.2.
F.4.1 Signal Timing, Portfolio Conversion, and Execution Constraints.Daily scores generated at market
close cannot trade on that close. They are converted to orders for the next session, using the 09:30–10:00
volume-weighted average price for execution. Unless a public task specifies another construction rule,
daily-score outputs form equal-weight, long-only portfolios with at most 100% gross exposure.
Intraday order outputs retain their declared shares, notional amounts, or weights. Execution is attempted
on the first available minute bar at least one minute after the signal time, using the price basis stated by
the task. Both timing rules prevent execution on the bar that produced the signal and apply equally to
systems within a case. Actual fills remain subject to the account and market constraints below.
Each account starts with CNY 1,000,000. The evaluator charges a three-basis-point brokerage commission
with a CNY 5 minimum, a five-basis-point stamp duty on sales, and a 0.1-basis-point transfer fee on both
purchases and sales. The experimental default slippage is five basis points; the filtered-reversal case has a
prespecified eight-basis-point override.
Beyond trading costs, fills are constrained by liquidity, available cash, and market rules. Each order may
consume at most 10% of market volume in its execution window or minute bar. The account prohibits
negative cash and does not allow purchased shares to be sold until the next trading session (T+1). It also
enforces trading suspensions, daily price limits, and purchases in multiples of 100 shares, while allowing
complete liquidation of an odd-lot remainder.
Case-specific lookback, portfolio, and execution constraints are fixed across systems within a task. These
settings define the common evaluation conditions; they enter requirement-fidelity assessment only when
specified in the public request.

Fake News, Real Crashes: Information Injection Attacks
on LLM Financial Trading Agents
Mengxiao Wang
Texas A&M University
jacksonwang@tamu.edu

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents now trade real capital
using news as evidence, yet they cannot reliably tell fabricated
headlines from real news. Because they read the same feeds
under near-identical policies, a single fabricated post can push
them into the same trade at the same moment. We present a
systematic attack benchmark forinformation injectionagainst
LLM trading agents. Unlike prompt injection, which targets
the model’s instructions, information injection targets the ev-
idence the agent trusts. The benchmark spans five injection
vectors and seven event categories, evaluated over∼10,000
decisions on 15 U.S. stocks. Five findings emerge. (i) Prompt
injection achieves 14% attack success rate (ASR), but fabri-
cated news reaches 62% and two 30-word templates reach
100% on all 15 stocks, inverting the usual LLM-security intu-
ition. (ii) For fact-poisoning templates, surface form matters
more than content, since removing credibility cues such as a
named source or a “BREAKING” tag drops ASR from 100%
to 0% while the fake claim remains identical. (iii) Success de-
pends on the agent’s state, with a fake indicator panel scoring
91% against an agent about to hold but 12% against one about
to buy. (iv) Attacks generalize across model vendors and re-
main effective when blended, unlabeled, into real news feeds.
(v) Most attacks evade standard defenses, since model scaling
stops shallow vectors but not fact poisoning, peer debate fails,
and only hierarchical aggregation reduces ASR.
1 Introduction
On 23 April 2013, the Syrian Electronic Army hijacked
theAssociated Press(AP) Twitter account and posted a sin-
gle fake tweet: “Two Explosions in the White House and
Barack Obama is injured.” Within three minutes, the S&P 500
shed an estimated $136 billion [ 26, 68]. A decade later the
traders have changed. Large language model (LLM) agents
now reason directly over unstructured text and place real
BUY/SELL/HOLD orders [13, 32, 53, 80]. Robinhood customers
can now let third-party AI agents trade stocks for them [41],
and Interactive Brokers [24] and Public.com [ 52] have fol-
lowed. U.S. lawmakers have begun pressing the SEC on how
it will supervise this exposure [14]. Whereas the 2013 attack
needed one trusted account, an LLM trading agent can be
hijacked byanytext in its pipeline, and fake news is known
to shift stock prices [28, 34, 44].
Two shifts make this urgent. First, autonomy removes the
human check. Whereas a supervised desk had three minutes
to flag the AP tweet, an LLM agent reasons in milliseconds
and takes a position on its own. Three properties amplify
this exposure. Fabricated content is now essentially free to
produce at scale [61, 81], and agents ingest far more sources
than any human desk can read. Because thousands of agents
consume thesamefeed under near-identical policies, one post
can change many portfolios at once. Second, almost all prior
LLM-security work targets thecontrol plane(system prompt,
role, tool routing), which strict trading policies resist. It cov-
ers prompt injection [19, 36, 50], indirect injection through
tools [11, 79, 82], jailbreaking [3, 12, 35, 58, 71, 85], and
training-time poisoning [4, 60, 66]. Thedata plane, the in-
formation the policy is built to act on, is undefended. To the
agent, a fabricated “CEO arrested” report is exactly the input
its instructions tell it to act on. We therefore measure how
much of an agent’s decision a few fabricated sentences can
control when its only contact with the world is text.
We build a systematic attack study along two orthogo-
nal axes. Fivevectors(V1–V5) capturehowcontent is cor-
rupted (fact poisoning, sentiment narrative, selective evidence,
structured signal, and coordinated multi-source). Sevenevent
categoriesdescribe the price-impacting event that is faked
(corporate actions, governance/insider, regulatory/legal, earn-
ings/analyst, market structure, macro, and narrative). Because
the two axes are separate, we can test which matters more, the
manipulation technique or the faked event. We evaluate on 15
real U.S. stocks, each identified by its ticker (the short sym-
bol under which a stock trades, such as AAPL), over roughly
10,000 trading decisions and compare against control injec-
tion. Ablations vary trading mode, position state, phrasing,
cross-ticker transfer, model scale and vendor, and injection
realism.
The result inverts the usual LLM-security intuition.
Control-plane payloads (and the benchmarks built to score
them) are largely ineffective against a trading policy (14%

mean attack success rate, ASR). Because the policy has a
goal, it will not discard that goal for an “ignore previous in-

responds to surface markers of credibility rather than to verifi-

a fake indicator panel scoring 91% against an agent about to
hold but 12% against one about to buy. It depends on thetar-

more than the choice of vector.
Three obvious defenses each fall short in a different way.
Scaling up the model gives partial protection. GPT-4o reduces
the structural vectors to 0%, but fact poisoning still succeeds at
93% ASR, and a frontier reasoning model (GPT-5) is no better
(§5.6). Adding agents helps only in a hierarchical pipeline,
because a star pipeline cuts ASR to 9% while ASR under
peer debate remains 74%. Presentation alone can also make
a safety-tuned model look immune. Its ASR is 0% under an
explicit adversarial marker and 46% once the same content
is blended into the feed. Economically, each injection drives
the agent to deploy $16K, roughly 80% of its $20K buying
power, in the attacker’s chosen direction. Because one post
reaches all agents that read the feed and they react alike, a
single headline can push many portfolios in the same direction
at once. §8 maps this market-level exposure.
Several recent studies attack trading agents through the text
they read, and each makes a different assumption about the
attacker. Rizvani et al. [54] hide homoglyphs and invisible
clauses in one day of headlines, a payload built to escape a
human reader. In contrast, ours is an ordinary sentence that
an editor would publish without a second look. AutoRed-
Trader [37] refines misinformation with feedback from the
victim agent and reports 26.67% attack success on Bitcoin
data. Our templates are written once without querying the vic-
tim, yet two of them succeed on all 15 stocks. TradeTrap [77]
perturbs components that run inside the operator’s system,
and two multi-agent studies [42, 62] follow a poisoned sig-
nal as it passes from one agent role to the next. Our earlier
systematization of 15 academic trading schemes found no
scheme that verifies where a news item came from and also
cross-checks it against other outlets [ 69]. We measure that
gap for the weakest attacker we can define, one who can only
post.
Three features make the study distinct. First, it crosses the
manipulation technique (five vectors) with the faked event
(seven categories) on the same agent. As a result, we can
attribute success to the faked event and its credibility cues,
and the lexical ablation then isolates those cues while the
claim remains fixed. Second, it measures success as a func-
tion of the agent’s own position and operating mode, which
yields a concrete attacker procedure (§5.7) and a defense cri-
terion conditioned on the agent’s posture. Third, it puts the
defenses a deployer would try first under one protocol, testing
a larger model, a frontier reasoning model, three multi-agent
topologies, and blending into a real feed. In all three topolo-
gies the fabricated narrative still appears in an intermediate
agent’s output, and where the attack fails, the trader agent’s
final action is the step that blocks it (§6).
Our Contributions.We contribute the following:
1.Information injection as a distinct threat.We formulate
information injection as a data-plane threat to LLM agents—
distinct from control-plane prompt injection—and build a
systematic attack framework for it. Beyond finance, any high-
stakes domain where agents act on unverified external content,
such as clinical, legal, or operational settings, faces the same
exposure, and the framework applies to it directly.
2.A large-scale measurement.Across the benchmark, infor-
mation injection is 4.5× more effective than control injec-
tion, generalizes across model vendors, and remains effective
under realistic unlabeled blending. Attack design is domain-
dependent, as generic manipulation primitives matter less
than domain-grounded framing. Attacks built from real do-
main knowledge (here, price-impacting event archetypes) are
markedly more effective. As a result, each agentic setting
needs its own attack vocabulary.
3.A benchmark and a defense criterion.We build a 62-
template benchmark that crosses five vectors with seven event
categories and show one template per vector and per category
with the agent prompt (Appendix A). Because the credibility
cues that make fakes work are the same cues real news con-
tains, a viable defense must score contentconditional on the
agent’s postureand report ASR at a fixed false-positive rate
(FPR).
2 Background and Related Work
The retail agentic-trading wave.Agent-driven trading be-
came a deployed retail product within a year. Robinhood’s
2026 Agentic Trading beta lets customers connect third-party
LLM agents to their accounts over Model Context Proto-
col (MCP) servers, an open protocol through which agents
call tools that third parties publish [41, 55]. Interactive Bro-
kers [24] and Public.com [ 52] followed, and open trading
APIs [63] and agent stacks [22] let the same agents place live
orders.
LLM trading agents and adversarial attacks.Domain mod-
els [73, 78], benchmarks [76], and agent systems [7, 32, 53,
80] share one architecture, a language-conditioned policy
that ingests market data plus natural-language information
and emits a structured trade. We target this generic class.
Most adversarial-LLM work targets thecontrol plane, through
prompt injection [ 36, 50, 64], indirect injection through
tool outputs [11, 19, 82], and jailbreaks and adversarial suf-

over an open, unauthenticated news feed into a policy that
outputs a position instead of an answer (Table 1). Our lexical
ablation (§5.4) measures one form of reported LLM syco-

text [57].
Attacks on trading agents through their inputs.The stud-
ies closest to ours attack a trading agent through the text it
reads, and they differ from ours in the attacker’s entry point,
the form of the payload, the factors they vary and the sys-
tem they attack. Rizvani et al. [54] alter one day of headlines
with Unicode homoglyphs and hidden-text clauses, designed
to remain invisible to people. Fed to nine sentiment models
inside a trading system that fuses an LSTM price forecast
with LLM sentiment, these headlines lower annual returns
by up to 17.7 percentage points. They also survey scraping
libraries, platforms and practitioners to argue feasibility. Au-
toRedTrader [37] generates finance-specific misinformation
through behavioral-bias framing, small textual perturbations
and rewriting, strengthens it with the agent’s feedback, and
evaluates it on Bitcoin data in a simulated environment. Trade-
Trap [77] perturbs four internal components (market intelli-
gence, strategy, ledger and execution) in closed-loop backtests
(replays of a strategy on historical prices) on U.S. stocks. Two
multi-agent studies restrict the attacker to the data agents
consume. Poisoning Agentic Alpha [ 42] pairs each role of
a four-role pipeline with a matched attack across four com-
munication topologies and concludes that no architecture is
inherently robust. Contagion [62] injects budget-constrained,
benign-looking social posts into an analyst’s evidence stream
and finds that well-chosen topologies and coordinator prompts
damp the shock. Outside trading, publishing LLM-written ar-
ticles into a crawled news corpus shifts the predictions of
retrieval-augmented forecasters [38]. Market signal injection
likewise shifts LLM pricing agents by changing number for-
matting, competitor order or commentary while the values
remain fixed [31]. In contrast, our attacker enters through one
public post, and the payload is a single visible, unoptimized
sentence. We vary the faked event and the manipulation tech-
nique as separate axes on the same agent, together with the
credibility cues, the agent’s position and operating mode, the
model and the pipeline topology, on 15 U.S. stocks and six
models from three vendors. This design lets us measure which
of these factors decide the trade. As a result, we can show that
a 30-word claim flips all 15 stocks. Removing its source noun
or urgency cue drops it to 0% while the claim is unchanged,
and hierarchical aggregation stops the trade even though the
injected narrative still appears inside the pipeline.
Text-driven market manipulation and LLM-generated
misinformation.The 2013 AP hack-crash [ 26, 68] is the
canonical text-driven market disruption. Abnormal-return ev-
idence for social-media manipulation is well established [2,
28, 34, 56], and regulators already classify it under exist-
ing market-manipulation rules [ 16, 44, 45, 65]. The event
magnitudes anchoring our taxonomy follow the event-study
literature [ 20, 30, 39]. Generative models make plausible
misinformation inexpensive to produce at scale [18, 61, 81].
Whereas existing misinformation benchmarks [46, 59] target
a human reader, we measure the effect on a downstream LLM
trading agent.
Multi-agent defenses.Multi-agent debate [ 5, 15, 33] has
been proposed as both an accuracy enhancer and a defense,
and concurrent work tests it against prompt injection [1]. We
test it as one candidate defense (§6). Structurally, information
injection resembles software supply-chain attacks [10, 29, 47],
because each news item is a trusted “component” the agent’s
reasoning “builds” into a decision, and the adversary corrupts
that component instead of the build.

We formalize the information injection threat model that our
benchmark evaluates. We assume a deliberately weak attacker,

The system under attack is an LLM trading agentπ that, on
each trading day d, maps a market snapshot md, which con-

with technical indicators, and a natural-language context xd

with action αd ∈ {BUY,SELL,HOLD}, quantity qd ∈Z ≥0 (a
non-negative integer share count), and the prices tpd,sl d of a
take-profit order and a stop-loss (an order that sells automat-

reference agent is a single-shot trend-following trader built
in 130 lines of Python around a GPT-4o-mini call (§5.1).
We condense the multi-call architectures of prior trading
agents [32, 74, 80], which spend thousands of tokens and
minutes of wall-clock per decision, into a single call. At that
latency the news edge being traded has already decayed. A
compact agent on a fast, inexpensive, widely available model
is therefore the realistic retail configuration. Its benign base-
line π(md,x benignd ) at temperature 0 is deterministic and serves
as the reference decision against which each attack is mea-
sured.
We give the attacker asinglecapability, namely to post
text into one or more public channels that the agent’s news
pipeline ingests (e.g., X, Reddit, investor forums, fake press-
release wires, or compromised feeds). Such content is typi-
cally scraped or summarized intoxd, and is not pre-filtered for
accuracy. We grant the attacker no knowledge of the agent’s
prompt, model, or weights (black box); no ability to alter its
code, training data, or system prompt; no ability to mass-
compromise outlets such as the Associated Press, Reuters, or
Bloomberg (which would strengthen the attack, as the 2013
crash showed [26]); and no capital, broker access, or market
power other than the trades the agent itself is induced to make.
The pipeline may rate-limit (e.g., top-k headlines per ticker
per day), but injected content otherwise blends with legitimate
news.
Yet this setting is alreadysufficient, because the attacker
does not need to defeat the agent’s policy, only to satisfy its
input contract, “treat news as evidence and act on it.” Plausible
fake news satisfies that contract by construction. As a result,
the agent acts on adversarial input without breaking any of its
own rules. A content-only defender cannot tighten the contract
without disabling the agent. One consequence, drawn out in
§7, is that nocontent-onlydefender can separate a fabricated
“CEO arrested” tweet from a genuine breaking report of the
same shape. Only provenance or the agent’s own market state
separates them.
Excluded attacks.We exclude several adjacent attacks: di-
rect prompt injection / system-prompt overwrite (studied by
Liuet al.[ 36], and included here as a comparative baseline,
§5.2); tool-output indirect injection [ 11, 82]; training-data
poisoning of the underlying model [4, 66]; attacks on market
microstructure (the mechanics by which an exchange matches
buy and sell orders), such as spoofing, layering and quote stuff-
ing, which target exchange mechanics instead of the agent’s
reasoning; and compromise of authoritative outlets such as
the AP or Reuters, a stronger adversary against which our
results are alower bound.

4 Information Injection Attack Frame-
work
Our framework is a systematic family ofplain-language ad-
versarial financial contentattacks against an LLM trading
agent, evaluated under a realistic black-box public-channel
threat model. Benchmarks of instruction-style payloads, jail-
breaks, and human-facing misinformation measure a model’s
obedience to a payload or a reader’s belief in a story [11, 46,
59, 71, 82]. In contrast, we measurewhichcontent techniques
change a trading decision and how much trading harm they
cause.

Vector axis (V1–V5).The five vectors abstract distinct manip-
ulation primitives.V1 fact poisoninginserts a single concrete
“breaking-news” claim with material price impact (“CEO ar-
rested overnight,” “CFO resigned after admitting revenue
inflation”). It is the most direct attack and the closest ana-
logue to the 2013 AP hack-crash tweet.V2 sentiment nar-
rative manipulationasserts a sentiment shift without spe-
cific facts (“the narrative around this stock has turned deci-
sively negative”), exploiting the agent’s training on sentiment-
conditioned price patterns.V3 selective evidence injection
cherry-picks a small set of indicators and instructs the agent to
decide on them alone, exploiting deference to apparently com-
plete summaries.V4 structured-signal poisoningpresents a
synthetic dashboard of quantified technical signals, such as the
relative strength index (RSI), moving-average convergence-
divergence (MACD), and average directional index (ADX). It
is written like a bullish (expecting the price to rise) indicator
readout, “RSI=18, MACD bullish crossover, ADX strengthen-
ing”, and exploits the agent’s training on structured indicator
panels.V5 coordinated multi-source manipulationpresents
the same conclusion as if reached independently by several
sources, exploiting the heuristic that cross-source agreement
implies veracity.
Event axis (seven categories).The event axis is grounded
in a survey of roughly 130 real-world price-impacting event
types, organized into the seven categories of Table 2. They are
corporate actions (CA), governance/insider/short (GI), regu-
latory/legal (RG), earnings/analyst/operations (EA), market
structure/options (MS), macro/geopolitical (MA), and narra-
tive/sentiment (NA). We use these abbreviations throughout.
Their canonical price impacts are drawn from the event-study
literature [20, 23, 30, 39]. We include events that (i) have doc-
umented price impact ≳2% and (ii) can plausibly be faked
as a short social-media post (a rumor-like first-mover signal
instead of an audited filing). For each event we author one or
more short, tweet-styleevent-grounded templates.
Cross-axis coverage.Table 3 records which vectors can carry
which event categories, and the coverage is uneven. The em-
pirical question of §5.3 is which of the two axes better predicts
attack success. Fact poisoning (V1) is the most expressive
vector and supports all seven event categories. Structured-
signal poisoning (V4) is most natural for market-structure
(MS) and operational (EA) events. Coordinated multi-source
manipulation (V5) most naturally targets governance (GI),
analyst (EA), and narrative (NA) events.

The reference agent.We use a single-shot trend-following
agent built on GPT-4o-mini [48], designed to be representa-
tive of production deployments [7, 32, 74, 80] (full prompt
in Appendix A). It states a trend-following policy with
exponential-moving-average (EMA)-based trend detection, a
one-position-at-a-time constraint, and JavaScript Object No-
tation (JSON)-structured output. We keep the architecture
minimal so that observed vulnerabilities are attributable to
the language-conditioned decision policy itself. We then ex-
tend the study to three multi-agent topologies (§6) [ 9, 53].
The agent receives a market snapshot, a position summary,
buying power, a trading-mode hint, and a free-textnews con-
textinto which our attacks are injected. The snapshot con-
tains price, RSI, MACD, Bollinger bands, support/resistance,
EMA20/50/100/200, average true range (ATR), and volume-
weighted average price (VW AP).
Metrics.Our primary metric is the attack success rate (ASR)
of Eq. 3. To characterize thedirectionof induced deviations,
we report the distribution of post-attack actions. For the event-
grounded attacks, we also report thedirection-match rate
(§5.3), the fraction of successful flips whose induced action
matches the attacker’s intended direction. We reportmean
ASR, broken down by attack category, vector, ticker, baseline
state, agent mode, and position state. We also reportworst-
case ASR, the maximum over all templates within a ticker or
category, which models an optimizing adversary.
5 Empirical Evaluation
We evaluate the attack along four axes. We measure the size
of the information injection surface relative to control-plane
injection (§5.2), the factors that govern success (§5.2–5.4),
generalization across models, vendors, and realistic unlabeled
injection (§5.6), and end-to-end economic impact (§5.7). §5.7
distills these results into the attacker procedure a defender
must counter. Throughout, we find that adversarialinforma-
tion(the content the trading policy is built to act on) is far
more potent than the instruction-style payloads current LLM-
security benchmarks measure.

of magnitude more per call.Deployment: the open trading-
agent stacks and retail-broker integrations of §2 run on this
same class of general-purpose chat models [7, 32, 53, 74, 80].
This tier is the one an attacker would actually meet.

5.3 Worst-Case Templates and Event-
Grounded Attacks
A worst-case template.Ranked by mean ASR, two V1 fact-
poisoning prompts stand apart in Figure 4. An overnight
“CEO arrested” report and a “CFO resigned after admitting
revenue inflation” bulletin each flip the agent onall15 tickers
in all 10 repetitions, at 100% ASR with zero variance. Be-
cause the 10 repetitions per ticker are correlated, we treat the
15 tickers as the independent unit. With all 15 tickers succeed-
ing in all repetitions, the two-sided 95% Clopper–Pearson
interval on per-ticker success is [78%,100%], and the one-
sided 95% lower bound is 82%. With no model access, no
broker, and no per-ticker tailoring, a 30-word public post is
already a near-perfect attack. Both templates name no com-
pany and saythis companyinstead. When we write the ticker
in, as a real post would, all 60 configurations remain at 100%
(§5.5).
Event archetype.Grounding the attacks in real event types
reveals a clean gradient (E-bench, n=990 ), which Table 6
and Figure 5 show. Regulatory/legal (RG, 67%) and gov-
ernance/insider (GI, 64%) events lead, while pure narra-
tive/sentiment (NA, 26%) is the lowest. §5.3, Table 7, breaks
out all 22 events. Yet each category still contains at least
one (event, ticker) pair reaching 100%. The eventcategory
thus predicts mean ASR more cleanly than the manipulation
vector does. Our results suggest that the agent’s reasoning
over financial text is organized around event archetypes, over-
weighting binary, regulator- or auditor-driven outcomes and
under-weighting slow-moving sentiment. For an attacker this
prescribes framing fake content as a regulatory or governance
event. For a defender it flags regulator-shaped content as the
highest-priority verification target.
Steerability of successful attacks.Because our success crite-
rion counts any change from the benign baseline, one might
suspect that the induced flips are noise. They are directed far
more often than chance. Each event-grounded attack has an
intended direction (bullish →BUY ; bearish, that is, expecting
the price to fall, →SELL ), and the induced action matches
it in 87% of successful flips ( 474/543). Steerability tracks
event hardness, reaching at least 89% for the binary, regulator-
or auditor-driven categories (governance/insider 97%, regu-
latory 91%, market structure 90%, corporate action 89%). It
falls to 52%, near chance, for pure narrative content, where
a vague sentiment nudge is equally likely to push the agent
either way. Framing a hard event therefore controls both the
decision to trade and itsdirection. Because the denominator
is the 543 successful flips, steerability and ASR measure dif-
ferent things. The 87% counts exact matches and understates
control. Of the 69 remaining flips, 60 are BUY-baseline agents
that a bearish payload pushes to HOLD, canceling a planned
purchase instead of opening the short. This still shifts the port-
folio in the attacker’s direction. Only 9 (1.7%) are genuinely
counter-directional, and a posture-aware definition therefore
puts directional control at 98.3%. Directional control sepa-
rates disruption from a profitable position. An attacker who
needs a specific outcome is therefore pushed toward hard,
checkable events, precisely the class a defender can most
readily verify against a ground-truth feed.

5.4 Ablation Studies: Phrasing, Entity
Grounding, and Operating Policy
Confidence cues over the claim itself.Varying the credibil-
ity framing of the high-ASR bases (Figure 6, where three of
the five templates start at 100%) isolates the cues the agent
responds to. Two edits are decisive. If we strip the source at-
tribution (“federal investigators” or “trading halt” (a pause the
exchange imposes on trading in a stock)) or the urgency frame
(“BREAKING,” “this morning”) from a 100%-ASR “CEO ar-
rested” base, ASR collapses to 0% with the underlying event
unchanged. Hedging (“unconfirmed reports suggest”) cuts
ASR by 60–80% without eliminating it, and length is roughly
neutral. We therefore locate the vulnerability in the surface
markers of credibility, exactly the form that LLM-generated
fake news reproduces at near-zero marginal cost [18, 61]. The
corollary cuts both ways. Because the same markers make
genuine breaking news actionable, a filter that removed them
would blind the agent to real events. Here we observe the
content-level indistinguishability of §7 at the level of individ-
ual features.
Entity grounding as a residual defense.If a template tai-
lored to source ticker s is applied unchanged to a different
target t, it transfers at 7% mean ASR (Table 10). The agent
correctly declines to act on news about the wrong company.
The exceptions are sectoral (NVDA and the cloud-services
cluster show peer-mention contagion), but the pattern is con-
sistent. An attacker must therefore tailor each post to each
target ticker, a real, if manageable, cost. Entity grounding is
the one defense the agent supplies by itself, and it mirrors the
lexical finding. The agent verifies which entity a claim con-
cerns but does not verify the claim itself. It is therefore worth
measuring explicitly and preserving under any finetuning that
touches news-reading behavior.
Operating policy as incidental security.Attack success also
tracks policy settings chosen for non-security reasons (Ta-
bles 8, 9). Conservative mode roughly halves mean ASR
and drives it to 0% on HOLD-baseline targets but offers no
protection on a BUY-baseline one. With the state-dependence
above, the attacker’s hardest case is aconservativeagent that
would HOLD, the easiest anaggressiveone that would open
a long. Operating policy, more than the model itself, shapes
security here, which is a useful counter to the view that LLM
agents are inherently unsafe. It also limits the benefit of tight-
ening the policy, since risk falls only in the states the policy
constrains.

item per day, and it might succeed only because no other news
competes with it. We therefore pad the feed with 12, 60, 120,
or 240 additional headlines (AAPL, GOOG, NVDA, R=5 ,
n=60 ) and score against a benign baseline on the same
padded feed. ASR remains 100% in all twelve configurations,

its full $20K buying power. At a thinly traded name’s intra-

realized slippage (the gap between the expected price and
the price the order actually gets) of $5–20 per attack. The six
names in our backtest (§5.7) are large and mid-sized compa-

an upper bound on their per-attack slippage. Scaled to a pop-

ulation of N agents reading the same tweet, the expected

Two limits on this accounting deserve emphasis. First, our
$20K buying power ($10K equity plus $10K margin) sets
the $16K figure, which is roughly 80% of it and scales with
whatever capital the victim delegates. We measure that the
agent deploys nearly all of its buying power in a direction the
attacker chose, and the dollar figure scales with the deploy-
ment. Second, and more importantly,capturingvalue from
the induced flow, by front-running it or by taking the other
side, requires capital, broker access, and execution latency.
Our threat model denies the attacker all three (§3). The in-
duced order flow is therefore best read as an externality that a
second, better-resourced adversary could monetize. Our mea-
surements leave open the population N at which that becomes
worthwhile. Our threat model’s attacker does obtain, uncondi-
tionally, the ability to set the direction of the victim’s book at
zero marginal cost per additional victim.

Our reference agent (§4) is single-shot, but production
financial-agent stacks increasingly decompose the decision
across ateamof specialized LLM agents. Common patterns
include a news analyst feeding a risk evaluator feeding a
trader, and a panel of fundamental/technical/sentiment an-
alysts feeding an aggregator. Others run a debate among
independent analysts who then vote [74, 84]. Recent agentic-
security work shows that such added structure opens new
attack surfaces [ 27, 67, 70]. For information injection, we
therefore test if routing the same fabricated content through

V1–V5 vectors through three canonical topologies and com-
pare against the single-shot baseline on the identical market
snapshot, benign news, and trader policy. Any difference is
therefore attributable to the collaboration structure alone.
We evaluate three topologies, each terminating in thesame

•Linear: NewsAnalyst→RiskEvaluator→Trader.
• Star: {Fundamental, Technical, Sentiment} analysts → Ag-

7 Defense
We first revisit the two candidate defenses we tested, then
explain why content-only filtering is poorly suited to this
setting, and finally outline defense directions that rely on
signals beyond content.
Potential defenses.Two candidate defenses are measurable
on our data. Model capability is a partial one. On GPT-4o, the
fake indicator panels (V4) and the fake multi-source agree-
ment (V5) both fall to 0%, because each contradicts indica-
tors already in the agent’s context. Fact poisoning contra-
dicts no indicator the agent can check and remains at 93%
(Exp 5, §5.6). Capability therefore provides internal consis-
tency checking, which removes the vectors a defender could
already detect, while a fabricated hard fact would require
external corroboration. Hierarchical aggregation is more ef-
fective. A star pipeline limits ASR to 9%, against 74% for
peer debate (§6). The fabricated item reaches the trader in all
three topologies. Because the star pipeline’s trader declines
to act on a hedged summary, this defense works on the action,
the distinction the rest of this section develops.
The limits of content-only filtering.The natural first line of
defense is a content-only filter that inspects each item in isola-
tion. Mainstream LLM-security defenses do not fill that role.
StruQ [6], Jatmo [51], activation-delta task-drift detection [1]
and holistic content moderation [ 40] all targetinstruction-
shapedinputs, payloads containing “ignore” or attempting
to override the system prompt. Information injection con-
tains no such signature. The deeper barrier iscontent-level
indistinguishability. Consider a content-only defender map-
ping a text x to a flag D(x)∈ {0,1}from features of x alone,
with no corroboration and no access to the agent’s market
state. A fact-poisoning tweet (“CEO arrested overnight”) and
a genuine breaking report of the same event share syntactic
shape, lexical register, and structured arguments. They differ
only in a truth value that isnota function of the text. To
the extent the two are indistinguishable in D’s features, D
cannot raise its true-positive rate without raising its false-
16
positive rate on genuine news by a comparable amount. Our
lexical ablation supports thisempiricalclaim about feature
overlap. Because the attack’s potency comes from surface
credibility markers (a named source, an urgency cue) that real
breaking news also contains, stripping them drops ASR to 0%
(Exp 3, §5.4). The argument is scoped tocontent-only, single-
item, pre-verificationdetectors. Defenders that use signals
beyond content, such as cross-checking against the agent’s
own in-context indicators, corroborating across disjoint feeds,
or requiring provenance, fall outside it. Escaping it is neces-
sary but not sufficient, because a deployable defense must
still avoid halting the agent on genuine breaking news. The
meaningful criterion is therefore ASR-after-defense at a fixed
false-positive rate on realistic news, which the evaluation pro-
tocol of §7 fixes.
Defense roadmap.Five directions would reduce informa-
tion injection ASR without halting the agent on benign news.
Issuer-attested channels(R1) act only on claims an issuer
has signed, such as an Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis,
and Retrieval (EDGAR) filing ID. Self-signed posts do not
qualify, since a signature binds origin, not truth. The cost
is symmetric, because genuine first-mover rumors have no
attestation either.Action-conditioned gating(R2) gates the
action instead of the claim, raising the evidentiary threshold
as the position delta grows so that no single unattested item
can change the portfolio by more than a set fraction.Cor-
roboration across independent feeds(R3) requires agreement
between disjoint sources. Independence, not count, gives it
force, since §3 grants the attacker several channels and V5
shows simulated agreement already works inside one.Cali-
brated uncertainty propagation(R4) propagates uncertainty
per claim. A high-impact claim with low source confidence
then yields a smaller position instead of a binary flip.Hi-
erarchical aggregation(R5) has an aggregator cross-check
independent analysts before the trader acts. It is the only direc-
tion we measure, and it limits ASR to 10% (§6). They trade
off differently. R1 needs infrastructure nobody has yet. R3
and R5 add model calls before the agent may act, during the
wall-clock window in which the news edge decays (§3). Be-
cause R2 and R4 require a verifier call and a schema change,
we expect deployments to adopt them first.
Defense evaluation harness.Comparing the defense direc-
tions of §7 across studies requires a fixed evaluation contract,
with the same attack set, the same benign feed, and the same
definition of a false positive. Our harness fixes that contract. It
places a candidate defense in front of the reference agent and
replays the attack templates under the single protocol used
throughout the paper. It then reports both the residual attack
success and the cost the defense imposes on genuine news.
This appendix documents the interface, the per-decision out-
put, and the reference numbers a defense is measured against.
It adds the evaluation protocol behind the ASR-at-FPR crite-
rion of §7 and no new experiment.
A defense enters the pipeline at one fixed point: it inspects
the news feed before the agent receives it, and it may use
whatever signals it is entitled to. This is where the distinction
of §7 becomes operational. A content-only defense examines
each item’s text in isolation and, as argued there, cannot raise
its true-positive rate without paying a matching false-positive
rate on real breaking news. A stronger defense is allowed
more. It may read the agent’s own in-context market state,
corroborate an item across feeds, or cap the position the agent
is permitted to take on an unverified claim. These map onto
the roadmap directions R2 (action-conditioned gating) and R3
(cross-feed corroboration). The harness does not favor any one
of them. It fixes only the inputs a defense may consume and
the way its effect on the agent’s decision is scored. A content-
only filter and a state-aware gate can then be compared on the
same axis.
Reference defense.The harness includes one example de-
fense that instantiates the roadmap in three stages, each op-
tional and independently replaceable. The first stage is a
content-only pre-filter: it flags items that are instruction-
shaped or contain high-urgency phrasing, and returns pass,
warn, or block. This is exactly the content-only detector of
§7, included to show a defense designer the false-positive
cost that argument predicts. The second stage scores an item
against signals beyond its own text, namely the agent’s in-
context indicators, a company–entity table, and agreement
across sources. It combines them into a trust score in [0,1]
(direction R3). The third stage does not filter at all. Instead,
it caps the induced position delta in proportion to that trust
score, and a low-trust item therefore cannot change the posi-
tion by more than a set fraction of buying power (direction
R2). A new defense may keep, replace, or drop any stage, or
supply an entirely different defense that receives the same
feed and market snapshot.
Metrics.The harness reports three quantities. The first is
ASR-after-defense, the fraction of attack runs whose decision
still changes with the defense in place. The second is FPR, the
fraction of benign runs the defense blocks or over-anchors,
measured on the null-control protocol of §5.1. The third is
the Pareto ASR-vs-FPR curve as the trust threshold sweeps.
Reporting a single (ASR, FPR) point without the curve is
discouraged, because the interesting comparison is at matched
FPR.
Reference baselines.Any defense is compared against two
anchors. The first is the undefended reference agent at ASR
=61.7% , FPR =0% (§5.2, V-bench II). The second is the
star pipeline of §6 at ASR =9.5% , FPR ≈0% , measured
against its own benign baseline (§5.5). A content-only defense
that halves ASR at FPR ≤5% on real news is a material
improvement over the star pipeline. One that needs FPR >
20% to reach the star’s ASR is not, because the star pipeline
achieves that ASR without blocking any benign item.

8 Discussion
Limitations.Three caveats bound our claims. First, our null
control (§5.1) rules out sampling noise but not the effect of
appending a text block as such. A reported ASR therefore
describes the injectedblockas a whole. The lexical ablation
(Exp 3, §5.4) fixes the block and removes only its credibility
cues, which alone drops ASR to 0%. It therefore isolates the
content effect. Second, the primary agent is single-shot on a
GPT-4o-mini base, the attack set is fixed and unoptimized [35,
36], and we cover English-language U.S. equities only. Still,
the cross-vendor replay (Exp 6, §5.6) reproduces the attack on
four models from three vendors, and the mechanism therefore
extends beyond one model. Third, most results are means
without intervals and some numbers are grid extrema without
multiple-comparison correction, though the headline 62%-
against-14% gap far exceeds any plausible interval.
Relation to prior findings.Several results sharpen or qualify
earlier findings. News-corpus poisoning of forecasters found
its effect unchanged by the claimed publisher [38]. By con-
trast, in our trading agent, removing the source noun from
a 100%-ASR claim drops it to 0% (§5.4). A trading policy
therefore weighs attribution cues that a forecaster’s proba-
bility ignores. Contagion found that topology and coordina-
tor prompts can damp adversarial posts [62], and Poisoning
Agentic Alpha that no architecture is inherently robust [42].
Our topology study is consistent with both and shows where
the damping happens. Star aggregation limits ASR to 9%
although the injected narrative reaches an intermediate agent
in all runs, while peer debate converts the same narrative into
an apparent consensus (§6). Rizvani et al. priced a one-day
attack at up to 17.7 points of annual return [54]. Our backtest
adds that a persistent campaign lets the attacker choose the
direction of the victim’s book while the market sets the sign
of the realized P&L (§5.7). Finally, our systematization urged
deployers to corroborate sources and to enforce loss limits
outside the model [69], and the measurements here give each
recommendation a concrete role. Cross-feed corroboration
(R3) is the stronger form of the one defense that worked, hier-
archical aggregation (R5). An action-conditioned gate (R2)
is a loss limit outside the model that limits how much one
unverified item can change the portfolio.
Agent herding.Human traders have not reacted uniformly
to a feed. That disagreement supplied liquidity (the ability to
trade without moving the price much), since a trade requires
someone to take the other side. A fabricated post changed
prices only when it reached enough desks fast enough to
overwhelm this diversity, as the 2013 AP tweet briefly did.
LLM trading agents collapse that diversity. Thousands of
them use the same public feeds, pass the text through the same
small set of frontier models, and inherit the same credibility
priors learned during training. System prompts differ at the
margin. As a result, a single fake post pushes a large fraction
of the population in the same direction, and the effect is largest
for the most extreme fabricated events. Because developers
keep a profitable trading agent private as a source of alpha
(returns above the market), this population grows out of public
view, leaving an unusually adversarial setting understudied.
The autonomy trade-off.Retail brokers are already mov-
ing toward delegated trading agents (§1), and the benefit is
real. An ordinary investor gains continuous monitoring and
analyst-grade reasoning over filings, prices, and news that
once required a professional desk. Our attack targets this
same capability. An agent is worth deploying because it pro-
cesses all incoming information and acts without waiting for
its owner. By contrast, a human who saw “CEO arrested
overnight” might hesitate, consult a second source, or be
unavailable, and delegation removes each of these checks.
Because the same property creates both the benefit and the
vulnerability, removing the surface would give up the auton-
omy that motivates deployment. Deployers have to govern it
instead, through provenance, action-conditioned gating, and
ASR-at-FPR reporting (§7).
The attacker–victim cost asymmetry.Information injection
is economically one-sided. The attacker pays under $0.01 per
post with zero marginal cost per victim, while forced order
flow scales with book size, campaign persistence, and the
feed population N. Capturing that flow requires capital and
broker access outside our threat model, so as N grows the
concern is the externality the flow creates (§5.7). This differs
from traditional market manipulation such as pump-and-dump
(buying a stock, promoting it to raise the price, then selling),
whose cost scales with the trading volume the attacker must
generate. Here, the agent population supplies the volume, and
the attacker only publishes.
Correlated books and systemic risk.The per-agent numbers
understate the concern, because the agents are not indepen-
dent. When a population trades the same forced direction
within the same few seconds, its orders become a single cor-
related flow that a market maker (a firm that continuously
quotes both buy and sell prices) cannot offset. Correlated flow
of this kind is the microstructure precondition for a cascade
like the 2010 Flash Crash. Information injection provides syn-
chronized direction on demand, at a cost that does not grow
with N. Beyond a cascade, the same correlated flow repro-
duces recognized manipulation patterns, namely execution
and momentum ignition, volume distortion, benchmark-price
marking, and, for a limit-order agent population, order-book
layering. §8 maps each of them, although we do not mea-
sure them here. The threshold N at which correlated flow
pushes a thin order book (the resting buy and sell orders for
a stock) into a cascade depends on the agents’ own price
impact, which an agent-based market simulator can price and
our single-book backtest does not.
Market-level manipulation vectors.Our benchmark mea-
sures a single book, but its threat model is a population, be-
cause many agents read the same feed under near-identical
policies (§3). The cross-vendor replay (§5.6) supports this
18
directly, since one injection flips four models from three ven-
dors. When N such agents take the same forced direction
within the same window, their orders merge into one corre-
lated flow (§8). That flow is more than a front-running oppor-
tunity: it reproduces several patterns that existing rules already
classify as market manipulation [ 16, 45, 65]. We map four
below. Our single-book backtest does not model the agents’
own price impact. Each vector below is therefore a conse-
quence the population premise implies and a target for the
impact-aware simulator of §8. One framing difference mat-
ters throughout. Unlike a coordinated adversary who owns
the agents, our attacker only posts, and the agents place their
own genuine orders. The vectors below therefore arise from
correlated genuine flowinstead of spoofed or wash orders.
Execution and momentum ignition.A synchronized burst
of same-side market orders (orders to trade immediately at the
best available price) in a thin window consumes the resting
liquidity at the best quote and forces the last traded price to
move quickly. Trend- and momentum-following strategies
read this velocity as a genuine breakout and chase it. The
move can also trip clustered stop-loss orders into further mar-
ket orders, adding fuel in a self-reinforcing cascade [26]. Our
measurements map this vector most directly. Because our
agents already emit real BUY/SELL orders, a correlated pop-
ulation supplies exactly the same-side pressure this attack
needs, with no spoofing required.
Volume and activity distortion.N agents trading the same
name in the same direction inflate its traded volume and the
volume-weighted average price (VWAP). Execution algo-
rithms and listing criteria treat these and similar indicators
as evidence of genuine interest. Unlike wash trading, which
fabricates volume through self-matched trades, the volume
here is real order flow. That makes it harder to flag as ma-
nipulation, even though its cause is a single fabricated post.
Institutional algorithms that size into apparent liquidity are
then drawn in under false pretenses [21].
Benchmark-price distortion.Correlated fills concentrated
near the session close move the Open/High/Low/Close marks
that underpin derivative settlement, futures expiry, and daily
fund net asset value. Because a single injection can time the
agents’ entry, a post released late in the session pushes the
closing print in the attacker’s chosen direction. That corrupts
the one data point with the largest downstream footprint [65].
Order-book and liquidity distortion.This is the most indi-
rect vector, and it needs a stronger adversary than our threat
model grants. If the induced agents placelimitorders (orders
that execute only at a set price or better) instead of market or-
ders, correlated placement and cancellation on one side of the
book distorts perceived depth, the correlated-flow analogue of
layering [16]. Our reference agent places market orders. This
is therefore a surface a limit-order agent population would
open, not one our experiments exercise.
The four escalate in the capability they require. Execution
and volume distortion follow from a correlated market-order
population, benchmark marking adds only timing control,
and order-book distortion needs limit-order agents. All four
share one root, since a single unverified post that reaches
enough homogeneous agents becomes a market-level signal.
Establishing the threshold N and the realized magnitude of
each is the future work identified in §8.
9 Conclusion
We studied fabricated news that an LLM trading agent acts on
because its policy trusts the feed. Over 10,000 decisions on
15 stocks, it reached 62% mean ASR against 14% for control-
plane payloads, driven by credibility markers that real news
also contains. Because no content-only filter can separate the
two, a defense must verify provenance, gate on position size,
and report ASR-at-FPR on real news.
Ethics
This work studies an attack for defensive purposes, and we
have weighed its dual-use risk against its defensive value.
We ran all experiments on historical market data in a sand-
boxed paper-trading simulator. No attack content was posted
to any live social-media platform, no adversarial order was
placed with a real broker, and no deployed trading agent was
targeted. The study involves no human subjects.
We use 15 real, publicly traded tickers and event magni-
tudes taken from the public event-study literature, because
attack realism depends on real market structure. We attribute
no real wrongdoing to any company: the injected events (for
example, an overnight CEO arrest) are explicitly fabricated,
counterfactual stimuli, and the per-ticker numbers of Table 5
describe our referenceagent’ssusceptibility, not any prop-
erty of the underlying firm. The few real events we cite are
used only as magnitude anchors and are already public. No
individual is named or targeted.
The attacker’s decision procedure (§5.7) and the templates
printed in Appendix A are dual-use. We judge their disclosure
to be net-positive: the attack needs no model access, capital,
or expertise, only a public post, so it is already within reach
of any motivated actor, whereas defenders currently lack a
reproducible way to measure or mitigate it. We therefore
keep the material at the level of generic, low-sophistication
content, avoid model-specific exploit engineering, and pair
every attack result with the defense analysis and the ASR-at-
FPR evaluation criterion of §7.

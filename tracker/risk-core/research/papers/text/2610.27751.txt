Market Regime-Based Multi-Asset Portfolio
Optimization Using Nested Clustered Optimization
for Tactical Asset Allocation
Supriadi1,2 and Bambang Siswoyo 3
Abstract—We investigate the interaction between market regime
detection and Nested Clustered Optimization (NCO) for tactical
asset allocation across heterogeneous multi asset portfolios.
The central empirical question is whether conditioning the
NCO pipeline, encompassing covariance estimation, hierarchical
clustering, and weight allocation, on latent market states genuinely
enhances risk adjusted performance, or conversely, whether
estimation uncertainty within regime filtered sub samples induces
severe turnover and performance drag. We implement a causal
rolling Gaussian Hidden Markov Model (HMM) coupled with an
adaptive rebalancing mechanism that distinguishes regime shifts
from portfolio weight drift. The framework is evaluated on a
monthly Indonesian multi asset universe comprising sovereign
bond yields (SBN), the LQ45 equity index, spot gold (XAU/IDR),
foreign exchange (CNY/IDR), and Bitcoin (BTC/IDR) across
a 56 month out of sample period (January 2021 to August
2025). Rigorous walk forward backtesting reveals that standard
regime agnostic NCO achieves remarkable capital preservation
and risk adjusted efficiency, delivering a 29.97% cumulative
return (5.78% annualized), a maximum drawdown of only 2.18%,
a Calmar ratio of 2.65, and low monthly turnover of 5.41%. In
contrast, conditioning NCO on an unconstrained HMM in a small
multi asset universe amplifies parameter estimation noise and
induces excessive rebalancing (47 events; 24.58% average monthly
turnover), which erodes net cumulative return to 1.60% and
increases drawdown to 9.99%. These findings demonstrate that
without explicit transition persistence or asymmetric downside
objectives, regime switching frameworks can degrade rather than
improve clustered portfolio stability, establishing crucial empirical
boundaries for machine learning asset allocation.
Index Terms—market regime detection, nested clustered opti-
mization, tactical asset allocation, hidden Markov model, multi-
asset portfolio, adaptive rebalancing, walk-forward backtesting,
Indonesian capital markets.
I. INTRODUCTION
Modern portfolio theory, established in the seminal work
of Markowitz [1], formalizes asset allocation as a quadratic
trade-off between expected return and variance. While mathe-
matically elegant, classical mean-variance optimization (MVO)
suffers from acute out-of-sample fragility. As demonstrated
by Michaud [2], quadratic programming acts as an “error
maximizer,” placing excessive long weights on assets with

Furthermore, DeMiguel et al. [3] showed that naive 1/N
diversification frequently outperforms optimized portfolios out
of sample due to severe parameter estimation error, while
Bayesian shrinkage approaches such as Black and Litterman [4]
and covariance regularization by Ledoit and Wolf [5], [6] only
partially resolve structural rank deficiencies in ill-conditioned
covariance matrices.
In practical multi-asset allocation, these estimation chal-
lenges are compounded by pervasive non-stationarity, time-
varying correlations, fat-tailed distributions, and structural
breaks [7], [8]. During financial crises and market distress,
cross-asset correlations spike discontinuously, shattering the
diversification benefits anticipated during tranquil regimes [9],
[10].
To mitigate these vulnerabilities, financial machine learning
has developed two distinct paradigms: (i) structural graph
clustering and (ii) dynamic regime conditioning. Within the
structural paradigm, Mantegna [11] and Onnela et al. [12]
pioneered the application of minimum spanning trees and hier-
archical clustering to analyze correlation taxonomies. Building
on random matrix theory [13], [14], L’opez de Prado [15]
developed Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP), which replaces
matrix inversion with hierarchical tree clustering and recursive
bisection. Subsequently, L’opez de Prado [16], [17] formulated
Nested Clustered Optimization (NCO). NCO decomposes
the global portfolio problem into modular intra-cluster and
inter-cluster sub-problems, effectively insulating orthogonal
asset clusters from noise propagation while retaining custom
optimization objectives [18]–[20].
Concurrently, the regime-conditioning paradigm utilizes
Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) and Markov-switching frame-
works to detect latent macroeconomic environments (e.g.,
expansion, high growth, crisis) and tactically adjust portfolio
exposures [21]–[26]. Intuitively, combining regime detection
with clustered optimization appears compelling: if market
regimes dictate correlation regimes, conditioning covariance
estimation and hierarchical trees on the active regime should
yield superior tactical asset allocation (TAA).
However, conditioning complex optimization pipelines on
latent regimes introduces an acute, often overlooked trade-off:
estimation error vs. regime adaptiveness[27], [28]. In empir-
ical practice, estimating state-dependent covariance matrices
requires partitioning historical data into regime-specific sub-
samples. In small multi-asset universes over realistic rolling
windows, these sub-samples suffer severe sample starvation

II. RELATEDWORK
A. Regime-Switching Models and Dynamic Asset Allocation
The study of structural changes in financial time series
originates with the Markov-switching autoregressive framework
of Hamilton [21]. Subsequent literature demonstrated that asset
return distributions, factor risk premia, and inter-asset correla-
tions vary fundamentally across market states [8], [22]–[25].
In multi-asset allocation, Kritzman et al. [26] proposed regime-
conditioned asset allocation to defend against tail shocks,
while Nystrup et al. [30], [31] implemented Hidden Markov
Models within model predictive control (MPC) frameworks to
dynamically adjust equity-bond allocations.
Recent machine learning contributions have expanded
regime-aware allocation through advanced statistical represen-
tations. Costa [32] analyzed risk parity portfolio construction
across Markov states, while Bouzouita et al. [33] developed
causal Wasserstein HMMs to preserve stable state identi-
ties across rolling calibration windows. Zhang et al. [34]
introduced RegimeFolio, combining macroeconomic regime
filtering with ensemble learners for sectoral equity optimization.
Concurrently, Verma et al. [35] combined HMM regime
inference with deep reinforcement learning, and Fernandes
and Desell [36] developed financially guided deep learning
architectures. To address spurious state switching, Shu et
al. [37] introduced statistical jump models that penalize non-
persistent state transitions. Furthermore, Boukardagha [38]
and Bouy’e et al. [39] evaluated macro regimes in cross-asset
settings, observing that downside protection often trades off
against estimation noise.
B. Hierarchical Clustering and Robust Graph Allocations
The vulnerability of Markowitz portfolios to covariance
noise has stimulated extensive research into graph theory and
network filtering [11], [12], [40]–[42]. L’opez de Prado [15]
established Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP), which arranges
assets on a hierarchical dendrogram and allocates risk via top-
down recursive bisection, eliminating the need to invert the
covariance matrix. Raffinot [18], [19] generalized hierarchical
clustering to include Equal Risk Contribution (HERC) and
Minimum Variance variants.
Building on random matrix denoising [13], [14], L’opez
de Prado [16], [17] developed Nested Clustered Optimization
(NCO). Unlike HRP, NCO retains Markowitz optimization but
confines quadratic programming to modular intra-cluster and
inter-cluster stages. Burtini and Loeppky [20] demonstrated
that NCO provides robust out-of-sample diversification across
global equity markets. Nevertheless, how NCO interacts with
dynamic regime conditioning remains an unaddressed empirical
frontier.
C. Turnover Regularization and Estimation Risk
A critical consideration in tactical asset allocation is execu-
tion friction. As formalized by Muthuraman and Kumar [43]
and Boyd et al. [29], optimal dynamic trading under propor-
tional transaction costs requires establishing a no-trade band
around target weights. Without explicit turnover regularization,
tactical models experience severe performance drag. Sepp
et al. [27] and Oliveira et al. [28] warned that parameter
uncertainty in regime-switching models frequently degrades
out-of-sample returns below those of passive benchmarks.

B. Benchmarks and Strategy Specifications
We compare the proposed Regime-NCO strategy against five
distinct benchmark configurations:
1) Equal Weight (1/N): A passive strategy allocating equal
weight (1/N= 20%) to each asset, rebalanced monthly.
2) Buy-and-Hold: A passive strategy starting with equal
weights but letting them drift with asset returns without
rebalancing.
3) Minimum Variance: A standard Markowitz optimizer that
minimizes the portfolio variance using the unconditional
sample covariance matrix.
4) Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP): An independent imple-
mentation of the graph-based recursive bisection allocation
proposed by L’opez de Prado [15], utilizing single-linkage
hierarchical tree clustering and inverse-variance risk parity
without quadratic matrix inversion, rebalanced monthly.
5) Standard NCO (No Regime): A regime-agnostic Nested
Clustered Optimization pipeline using the unconditional
sample covariance matrix.

the HMM-NCO framework
on the monthly multi-asset dataset over the 56-month out-

A. Regime Detection Results
The causal rolling Gaussian HMM segments the 56-month
out-of-sample evaluation period (January 2021 to August 2025)
into three distinct macroeconomic regimes, as illustrated in
Fig. 1: 17 months under Regime 1 (Bullish, 30.36%), 30
months under Regime 2 (Sideways/Neutral, 53.57%), and 9
months under Regime 3 (Bearish, 16.07%). Visual inspection
of Fig. 1 reveals that while the model successfully identifies
major market phases—such as the post-pandemic recovery
and subsequent localized contractions—causal step-by-step
estimation triggers a total of 26 discrete regime transitions over
56 months, indicating significant state switching across rolling
windows.
The empirical validity of these decoded states is corrob-
orated by the regime-conditional return and risk moments
reported in Table II. Across the three states, the multi-asset
universe exhibits starkly divergent behavior. Under Regime 1
(Bullish), Indonesian sovereign bonds (SBN) generate an aver-
age monthly return of +0.76% (σ= 3.49% ), while spot gold

VI. DISCUSSION
A. Marginal Benefit and Empirical Boundaries of Regime
Conditioning (RQ1)
Our first research question examines whether conditioning
the covariance matrix and hierarchical clustering tree on
HMM-detected latent market regimes provides measurable
marginal gains in tactical asset allocation. As documented in
Table II, the rolling Gaussian HMM successfully segments the
historical return series into three economically interpretable
regimes exhibiting distinct annualized return and risk profiles.
For example, Indonesian sovereign bonds (SBN) generate
an average monthly return of 0.76% during bullish equity
expansions compared to −0.38% during market contractions,
while foreign exchange volatility (CNY/IDR) and Bitcoin tail
risk escalate sharply during bearish phases.
These divergent distributional moments translate directly
into distinct regime-conditional asset allocations (Table III),
validating the theoretical hypothesis underlying RQ1 that asset
correlation structures differ fundamentally across market states.
However, our walk-forward out-of-sample backtest demon-
strates that this theoretical differentiation does not automatically
translate into realized risk-adjusted outperformance. While the
HMM correctly captures the cross-sectional covariance shifts
in-sample, conditioning portfolio weights on transient regime
classifications introduces significant estimation variance. In
an asset universe of N= 5 , the reduction in specification
bias achieved by regime conditioning is overwhelmed by the
sampling error incurred from partitioning rolling lookback
windows into sparse sub-samples, establishing clear empirical
boundaries for regime-based machine learning allocation.
B. Risk-Return Trade-offs and the Primacy of Tree Clustering
(RQ2)
The empirical results across the 56-month out-of-sample
test window (Table IV) reveal profound structural insights
regarding risk-return trade-offs in emerging multi-asset markets.
Unconstrained benchmarks such as Equal Weight ( 1/N) and
Buy-and-Hold achieved the highest cumulative returns (76.70%
and 87.98%, respectively), propelled by massive cyclical rallies
in Bitcoin and spot gold. However, these unmanaged strategies
suffered catastrophic tail risk, incurring severe maximum draw-
downs of 15.27% and 21.17% along with annualized volatilities
exceeding 13% to 17%. Such severe capital impairment violates
the risk mandates of institutional endowments and sovereign
wealth funds.
In sharp contrast, the standard regime-agnostic Nested
Clustered Optimization (NCO) strategy achieved outstanding
risk efficiency and capital preservation. Static NCO delivered
a cumulative return of 29.97% (5.78% annualized) with a
maximum drawdown of only 2.18%, an annualized volatility
of 4.65%, and a Calmar ratio of 2.65, comfortably surpassing
Minimum Variance (18.45% CR, 4.75% MDD) and True
HRP (8.27% CR, 4.21% MDD). By decoupling intra-cluster
allocation from inter-cluster asset class weights, NCO insulates
the broader portfolio from instability in high-volatility assets
while preserving participation in market upside.
Crucially, when NCO is conditioned on latent HMM regimes
without transition constraints (Regime-NCO), performance
deteriorates markedly: cumulative return drops to 1.60% (0.34%
annualized), maximum drawdown widens to 9.99%, and the
Calmar ratio collapses to 0.034. The pairwise Jobson–Korkie
Sharpe ratio test (Table V) yields a test statistic of z= 2.684
with a p-value of 0.0073, formally rejecting the null hypothesis
of equal risk-adjusted performance at the 1% significance level.
This rigorous test proves that the observed outperformance
of regime-agnostic NCO over Regime-NCO is statistically
genuine rather than an artifact of sample variation, conclusively
answering RQ2.
C. Rebalancing Dynamics, Drift Thresholds, and Turnover
Friction (RQ3)
Addressing our third research question regarding turnover
dynamics and rebalancing friction, Table VI provides an
exhaustive audit of portfolio interventions across the 56-month
evaluation horizon. The unconstrained Regime-NCO strategy
triggered 47 rebalancing events, comprising 26 regime-induced
reallocations and 20 weight-drift corrections exceeding the
δ= 0.05 threshold (alongside the initial deployment). This
frequent portfolio reshuffling generated an average monthly
turnover of 24.58%, almost five times the 5.41% monthly
turnover observed under static NCO and 3.59% under True
HRP.
At our baseline transaction cost parameter of c= 10bps per
unit of portfolio turnover, direct transaction expenses accounted
for an annualized return drag of approximately 29.5 basis points.
While manageable in isolation, the primary performance penalty
arose from execution friction and tactical whipsaw. Because
causal HMM state decoding relies on backward-looking rolling
10
feature vectors, regime classifications frequently switched near
the inflection points of transient market drawdowns. The
portfolio was consequently forced to rotate aggressively into
safe-haven assets (SBN and CNY) immediately after equities
or gold had already absorbed market shocks, only to reallocate
back toward risk assets after substantial rebound rallies had
already materialized. These findings underscore that in real-
world implementations, adaptive drift thresholds ( δ) must
be paired with explicit state-transition penalties to prevent
execution friction from eroding alpha.
D. Regime Drift and Estimation Error in Small Asset Universes
A critical methodological insight emerging from our walk-
forward evaluation is the compound effect of regime drift
and parameter estimation error in low-dimensional asset
pools. As documented in Table II, the decoded Regime 1
(Bullish) realized an average out-of-sample monthly return of
−1.23% for the equity benchmark (LQ45), whereas Regime 2
(Sideways) realized +0.56%. This counter-intuitive behavior
stems from structural macro shifts between the 2019–2020
training window (characterized by pre-pandemic monetary
accommodation followed by initial COVID-19 stimulus) and
the 2021–2025 out-of-sample window (dominated by global
supply disruptions, aggressive US Federal Reserve rate hikes,
and capital outflows from emerging markets).
When rolling lookback windows ( W= 24 months) are
filtered by latent regime membership, the effective sample
size Tk =|D (k)t | frequently drops below 8 observations.
Although our adaptive Bayesian shrinkage (Eq. 6) guarantees
non-singular covariance matrices by shrinking toward the
unconditional estimator ˆΣ, the underlying eigenvectors and
correlation distance metrics remain noisy. In small universes
(N= 5 ), tree topology is fragile: a minor shift in pairwise
correlations between SBN, Gold, and CNY/IDR can flip the
dendrogram clustering structure from two clusters to three,
completely altering the inter-cluster allocation vector v∗. In
contrast, unconditional NCO computes its correlation distance
matrix over the full 24-month rolling window, maintaining
stable cluster hierarchies and robust out-of-sample performance.
E. Practical Guidelines for Quantitative Portfolio Managers
Based on our empirical and econometric findings, we formu-
late three actionable guidelines for quantitative asset managers
seeking to deploy machine learning allocation frameworks in
emerging multi-asset markets:
1) First, allocators should prioritize structural hierarchy over
temporal switching when operating in low-dimensional
asset pools ( N≤10 ). In such environments, hierarchical
tree clustering (NCO or HRP) alone delivers robust risk
diversification. Adding unconstrained regime-switching
layers partitions rolling windows into sparse, unstable sub-
samples that exacerbate turnover without compensating risk
reduction.
2) Second, quantitative practitioners should enforce sticky
regime dynamics via Bayesian priors if regime conditioning
is retained. Specifically, imposing informative Dirichlet
priors on the diagonal of the transition matrix A or requiring
persistent multi-period signal confirmation prevents spurious
rebalancing and curbs costly execution whipsawing.
3) Third, turnover regularization should be embedded directly
within the optimization objective rather than treated as a
post-hoc filtering heuristic. Incorporating quadratic turnover
penalties into intra-cluster and inter-cluster quadratic pro-
gramming formulations reconciles mathematical optimiza-
tion with institutional trading friction.
VII. CONCLUSION
This paper has presented a rigorous, walk-forward empirical
investigation into the interaction between continuous Hidden
Markov Model regime detection and Nested Clustered Opti-
mization for multi-asset tactical asset allocation. Conducting
an audited backtest across 56 monthly out-of-sample horizons
(January 2021 to August 2025) in the Indonesian capital market,
we evaluated the performance of regime-conditioned NCO
against static NCO, True HRP, Minimum Variance, Equal
Weight (1/N), and Buy-and-Hold strategies.
Our empirical findings establish three foundational conclu-
sions. First, regime-agnostic NCO demonstrates exceptional
capital preservation and risk efficiency, achieving a 29.97%
cumulative return (5.78% annualized) with a maximum draw-
down of only 2.18%, an annualized excess Sharpe ratio of
0.0063 against the 5.75% risk-free hurdle (1.242 relative to
cash zero), and a Calmar ratio of 2.65, while maintaining low
monthly turnover (5.41%). Second, conditioning NCO on an
unconstrained rolling HMM degrades performance in small
asset universes, increasing drawdown to 9.99% and reducing
net return to 1.60% due to parameter estimation noise within
regime-filtered sub-samples. Third, the Jobson–Korkie test
(z= 2.684, p= 0.0073 ) statistically confirms the superiority
of static NCO over Regime-NCO, demonstrating that machine
learning asset allocation must balance dynamic state adaptation
against parameter stability and trading friction.
A. Limitations
Several structural and methodological limitations of this
study warrant consideration. First, the empirical universe is
constrained to N= 5 core Indonesian asset proxies; while
representative of primary macro asset classes, larger asset
pools (e.g., 50 to 100 constituent equities and sector bonds)
may provide greater hierarchical depth and cluster redundancy.
Second, our causal rolling lookback window is fixed at W= 24
months; while justified by sample size constraints, lookback
length influences HMM parameter convergence. Third, HMM
regime inference is inherently backward-looking, resulting
in a structural detection lag during sudden macroeconomic
turning points. Fourth, our transaction cost model applies a
proportional flat fee of 10 basis points, omitting non-linear
market impact and liquidity slippage. Finally, the long-only
investment mandate restricts the portfolio from taking short
positions, limiting downside hedging capacity during persistent
bear regimes.

B. Future Work
Building upon these empirical benchmarks, future research
should explore three promising methodological extensions.
First, incorporating sticky Dirichlet priors or explicit state-
switching penalties into the HMM Baum-Welch training
objective will enforce regime persistence and suppress ex-
cessive turnover. Second, expanding the regime emission
space to include forward-looking macroeconomic features
(e.g., central bank policy rate differentials, yield curve slopes,
and term spreads) may improve turning-point anticipation.
Third, replacing Gaussian emission and Pearson correlation
metrics with non-Gaussian copula formulations will enable the
hierarchical clustering pipeline to better capture asymmetric tail
dependencies and liquidity cascades during systemic market
crises.

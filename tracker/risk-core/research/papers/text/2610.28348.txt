Characterizing Cross-Relay Generalization Failure
in Machine Learning-Based Power System
Protection
Nithin Pradeep Nadayil Kizhakkethil 1,*, Julian Oelhaf 1, Christian Bergler 2, Andreas Maier 1, Siming Bayer 1
1Pattern Recognition Lab, Friedrich-Alexander-Universit ¨at Erlangen-N ¨urnberg
2Department of Electrical Engineering, Media and Computer Science, Ostbayerische Technische Hochschule Amberg-Weiden
*Corresponding author: nithin.pradeep.nadayil@fau.de
Abstract—Machine learning-based fault detectors are often
evaluated at the relay locations used for training, although local
voltage and current measurements vary across power networks.
This work investigates cross-relay generalization for binary detec-
tion of faults anywhere in the network using local measurements,
rather than line-selective tripping. Using 50 ms windows from a
simulated 110 kV transmission network, we evaluate a multilayer
perceptron across all 64 source–target combinations of eight
relays and five random seeds. A shared simulation-level split
and source-only threshold selection provide a controlled held-
out evaluation without target-specific calibration. Despite nearly
unchanged mean F1-scores (0.730 within-relay versus 0.734 cross-
relay), the mean false-positive rate (FPR) increases from 0.088 to
0.341, while the mean false-negative rate decreases from 0.399 to
0.318. The change is strongly directional: models trained at Bus
2 relays yield a mean FPR of 0.670 at Bus 1 and Bus 3 relays,
compared with 0.096 for cross-relay transfers within the same
bus group. Linear probing indicates that learned representations
retain coarse bus-group information, while exploratory class-
conditional latent distribution analysis shows no consistent mono-
tonic association with transfer errors. These findings demonstrate
that aggregate metrics can conceal location-structured changes in
false-alarm behavior, highlighting the importance of evaluating
error types separately when assessing cross-relay generalization.
Index Terms—Power system protection, fault detection, cross-
relay generalization, distribution shift, multilayer perceptron,
representation analysis
I. INTRODUCTION
Power system protection is a fundamental component of
modern electrical grids, enabling the rapid detection and
isolation of faults to maintain system stability, equipment
safety, and operational reliability. Protection relays (PRs)
continuously monitor local electrical quantities, such as phase
voltages and currents, and initiate protective actions when
abnormal operating conditions are detected. With the in-
creasing deployment of digital substations and standardized
communication protocols such as IEC 61850, large volumes
of high-resolution relay measurements have become available,
creating new opportunities for machine learning (ML)-based
protection systems [1], [2].
ML has emerged as a promising approach for fault detection
in power system protection [3], [4]. Recent studies have
investigated ML-based methods for fault detection, fault clas-
sification, and fault localization using electrical measurements,
including multilayer perceptron (MLP)-based approaches for
transmission-line protection [5], [6]. A broad range of learning
architectures has been explored in power system protection,
including conventional ML methods and deep learning models
such as MLPs, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Re-
current Neural Networks (RNNs), and topology-aware Graph
Neural Networks (GNNs) [7], [8]. While these approaches
often report strong performance under their respective eval-
uation settings, their ability to generalize across different
measurement locations remains less clearly characterized.
However, such evaluations often implicitly assume compara-
ble measurement distributions across training and deployment
locations. In practical power systems, this assumption may
not hold. PRs are installed at different locations throughout
the transmission network, and the electrical signals observed
at each relay depend on their position relative to the fault,
network topology, and power flow conditions. Consequently,
measurements recorded at different relay locations exhibit dis-
tinct statistical characteristics, while the asymmetric changes
in classification errors under pairwise cross-relay transfer
remain insufficiently characterized for relay-local models. In
particular, it remains unclear which error type dominates,
whether aggregate metrics reveal this behavior, and whether
the failure exhibits topology-related structure.
Although generalization under distribution shift has received
increasing attention in machine learning [9], [10], relatively
little attention has been given to cross-relay generalization in
power system protection. Existing work primarily evaluates
models using random train-test splits or fixed measurement
locations, making it difficult to determine whether the learned
representations capture relay-independent fault characteristics
or simply memorize relay-specific measurement distributions.
Although topology-aware approaches have demonstrated
generalization to unseen grids without retraining [8], the
transfer behavior of models trained using measurements from
individual relay locations remains less well characterized.
This work therefore investigates pairwise cross-relay transfer
using a controlled MLP baseline, with particular emphasis on
asymmetric changes in false-positive and false-negative rates.
Answering this requires more than overall classification

II. SYSTEMSETUP ANDDATA
A. EMT Simulation Dataset
The experiments in this work are conducted using the
AdaptGrid subset of the EvEMTBench electromagnetic tran-
sient (EMT) simulation dataset generated in DIgSILENT Pow-
erFactory [2]. The dataset is based on a 110 kV double-line
transmission network consisting of three buses interconnected
by four transmission lines, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The network
is instrumented with 11 measurement locations, comprising
eight transmission-line PRs, two external-grid measurement
points, and one load measurement point. Each PR records syn-
chronized three-phase voltage and current measurements [2].
Each measurement location records three-phase voltages
and currents sampled at 9.6 kHz. The six electrical quantities
measured at locationmare represented as

The dataset incorporates significant operating-condition

source characteristics, fault locations, inception angles, and

machine learning models under varying network conditions.
B. Fault Scenarios and Class Distribution
The experiments use fault-event simulations from the
AdaptGrid subset of EvEMTBench [2], excluding switching
events. The dataset contains 4,667 simulations and 107,341
windows per relay, comprising 73,000 fault (68.01%) and
34,341 non-fault (31.99%) windows.

The dataset covers seven fault categories, including
single-line-to-ground, line-to-line, double-line-to-ground,
three-phase, and incipient faults, with varying fault locations
and operating conditions. The task therefore concerns global
fault-state detection from local relay measurements, not
conventional zone-selective tripping.

III. METHODS
This work characterizes cross-relay generalization through
three complementary aspects: predictive performance under
source-to-target transfer, changes in error behavior (False
Positive Rate (FPR) and False Negative Rate (FNR)), and
differences in the learned representations across relay loca-
tions, analyzed using linear probes and MMD 2. A multilayer
perceptron (MLP) serves as a controlled baseline to isolate
the influence of relay-location-dependent measurement distri-
butions.
A. Multilayer Perceptron
The MLP operates on minimally processed, raw relay-local
measurements without input normalization or hand-crafted
feature engineering. As defined in (1), each input window of
raw three-phase voltages and currents is flattened into a feature
vector
z∈R 2880.
It is processed by two fully connected hidden layers with
256 and 128 neurons, each followed by a ReLU activation. A
final linear output neuron produces a fault logit, converted
to a probability by a sigmoid function during inference.
The network has approximately 770,561 trainable parameters.
As this architecture is a controlled baseline rather than a
representative protection model, the magnitude of the observed
degradation should not be generalized to other architectures.
B. Training Settings
The MLP is trained with binary cross-entropy with logits
and the Adam optimizer (learning rate1.38×10 −4, no weight
decay), using a batch size of 1024 for at most 200 epochs.
Early stopping with a patience of 20 epochs is applied, and
the parameters from the best validation-loss epoch are retained.
After training, the operating threshold is selected on the
source relay’s validation set by maximizing Youden’sJstatis-
tic,
J(t) = TPR(t)−FPR(t).
The threshold is then frozen and used for the source relay
and all target relays, so no target-relay information influences
the operating point. ROC AUC is computed from continuous
raw model logits and is independent of this threshold.
Performance is reported as accuracy, F1-score, ROC AUC,
FPR, and FNR, with the fault class as positive. FPR is the
fraction of non-fault windows classified as faults, and FNR is
the fraction of fault windows classified as non-faults.
C. Pairwise Cross-Relay Evaluation
Each of the eight transmission-line protection relays serves
as a source for an independent model, which is evaluated at
the source relay and at each of the seven target relays. This
yields an8×8transfer matrix of 64 pairs: eight self evaluations
on the diagonal and 56 ordered cross-relay evaluations off the
diagonal.
A shared simulation-level split, generated with seed 42, is
reused across all eight relays. It comprises 3,266 training,
700 validation, and 701 test simulations, corresponding to
75,118, 16,100, and 16,123 windows per relay, respectively.
All windows originating from the same simulation remain in
the same partition. Self- and cross-relay evaluations use the
same held-out test simulations.
The complete experiment is repeated for five random seeds
(42, 5, 10, 25, and 100), which affect model initialization and
training but not the fixed simulation-level partition. Results are
reported as mean±standard deviation across seeds.
To examine location structure, the 56 ordered cross-relay
pairs are divided by bus group (Bus 1: R1, R2; Bus 2: R3–
R6; Bus 3: R7, R8) into within-group pairs (16), Bus 1–Bus 3
pairs (8), pairs from Bus 2 to Bus 1 or Bus 3 (16), and pairs
from Bus 1 or Bus 3 to Bus 2 (16). Mean FPR and FNR
are computed per category from the five-seed mean transfer
matrices.
D. Representation Analysis
Representations are taken from the 128-dimensional penul-
timate layer of each source-trained MLP of the seed-42 run.
All representation analyses use one fixed study subset, drawn
once and embedded by every source-trained model, so that
differences between models are not caused by different input
samples. For each of the eight relays, 150 fault and 150
non-fault windows are sampled uniformly at random without
replacement with seed 42 from all non-switching windows
of that relay, giving 300 windows per relay and 2,400 in
total. The subset is not restricted to a data partition and
therefore contains windows from training, validation, and test
simulations; it comprises 1,878 distinct simulations. Sampling
is class-balanced and performed independently for each relay,
so relays are not matched on simulation or window position.
Relay identity is predicted from the representations with
multinomial logistic regression (ℓ 2 penalty,C= 1) on
standardized features, and bus-group identity (Bus 1, Bus 2,
or Bus 3) with a separate probe of the same type. Because
overlapping windows from one simulation are strongly depen-
dent, both probes are evaluated with five-fold stratified cross-
validation grouped by simulation, so that all windows from the
same EMT simulation fall in the same fold. The chance level is
1/8for relay identification. Because Bus 2 contains four relays

IV. RESULTS
A. Overall Cross-Relay Performance
Table I summarizes self- and cross-relay performance across
five seeds. Cross-relay transfer reduces accuracy (0.7010 to
0.6747) and ROC AUC (0.7929 to 0.7493). The change in
error profile is larger: mean FPR rises from 0.0879 to 0.3406,
while mean FNR falls from 0.3986 to 0.3181. Cross-relay
degradation is therefore not a uniform increase in errors but a
shift towards false positives. Because the threshold is fixed
on the source validation set, this shift reflects the model’s
behavior under the target measurement distribution, not target-
specific re-tuning.
The F1-score is nearly unchanged (0.7302 to 0.7336) be-
cause the rise in false positives is offset by the fall in false
negatives. Aggregate metrics such as F1 can therefore hide
substantial changes in error behavior.
Averages also conceal severe individual failures. The worst
source-target pair in each seed averages an FPR of 0.9081
across seeds. The worst-pair FNR (0.5727) exceeds the mean
self FNR, so the decrease in FNR holds on average but not
for every pair.
B. Error Behavior Across Relay Pairs
Fig. 2 shows the pairwise FPR and FNR matrices, averaged
over five seeds. False-positive behavior depends strongly on
the source-target combination. For models trained at Bus 2
relays, the mean FPR at Bus 1 and Bus 3 relays ranges
from 0.497 to 0.807, whereas transfer within a bus group
and between Bus 1 and Bus 3 remains close to the self-relay
level. The FNR matrix shows a different pattern, and pairs with
high FPR do not have high FNR. Together with the aggregate
results, this indicates that the main deterioration under cross-
relay transfer is the increase in false-positive rate.

The degradation is also structured by location. Table II
quantifies this structure. Models trained at Bus 2 relays show
a mean FPR of 0.670 at Bus 1 and Bus 3 relays. Transfer
within a bus group (0.096) and between Bus 1 and Bus 3
(0.132) stays close to the mean self-relay level of 0.088. The
reverse direction, from Bus 1 or Bus 3 to Bus 2, is elevated but
weaker (0.360). The FNR shows the complementary behavior:
Bus 2-trained models rarely miss faults at Bus 1 and Bus 3
(0.166), consistent with their tendency to flag windows there
as faulty. Source relays with the lowest mean cross-relay
FPR (R2, R7, R8) also show the highest mean cross-relay
FNR, which indicates a more conservative operating point
and not better transfer. Cross-relay failure is therefore not a
random loss of performance between arbitrary relays. The task
uses the global fault-state label from local measurements, so
these results characterize cross-location generalization and not
conventional zone-selective relay operation.

D. Representation Distribution Differences and Error
MMD2 is computed per class and related to the matching
error rate over the 56 ordered pairs of the seed-42 run. Between
non-fault windows, MMD 2 has a Pearson correlation ofr=
−0.384and a Spearman correlation ofρ=−0.190with cross-
relay FPR. Between fault windows, the corresponding values
with FNR arer= 0.349andρ= 0.045. The FPR association
is negative, so larger latent distances are associated with
lower false-positive rates, opposite to the naive expectation
that greater shift means worse transfer. The rank correlations
are weak, so the linear associations do not indicate a robust
monotonic relationship.
The observed MMD 2 values indicate differences between
relay-specific latent distributions, but show no consistent
monotonic association with cross-relay transfer errors. Source
and target samples with similar aggregate distributions can still
lie differently relative to the decision boundary, so MMD 2
is best treated as a diagnostic of representation shift and
not as an explanation of generalization failure. A possible
explanation for the negative FPR association is that a source

V. CONCLUSION
This work characterized how an MLP fault detector trained
at one relay location behaves at other locations, using exhaus-
tive pairwise transfer across eight transmission-line PRs in
a simulated 110 kV network, with a shared simulation-level
split and a source-validation threshold. Overall degradation is
moderate: mean accuracy falls from 0.7010 to 0.6747 and ROC
AUC from 0.7929 to 0.7493. The error profile changes far
more. Mean FPR rises from 0.0879 to 0.3406, with the per-
seed worst relay pair averaging 0.908, while mean FNR falls
from 0.3986 to 0.3181, so the F1-score is nearly unchanged
(0.7302 to 0.7336). Cross-relay transfer thus shifts the balance
towards false positives, and aggregate metrics can hide this.
The FNR decrease holds only on average, since the worst-pair
FNR (0.573) exceeds the mean self value.
The degradation is structured by location: it depends on the
source-target pair, and high FPR occurs mainly when models
trained at Bus 2 relays are applied at Bus 1 or Bus 3 relays.
It is weaker in the reverse direction and stays close to the
self-relay level between Bus 1 and Bus 3. Linear probing
shows that individual relay identity is only weakly recoverable,
but coarse bus-group information remains in the representa-
tion, consistent with this pattern. In a single-seed, descriptive
analysis, class-conditional MMD 2 correlates negatively with
FPR (r=−0.38,ρ=−0.19) and positively with FNR
(r= 0.35,ρ= 0.04). The FPR sign is opposite to the naive
expectation that greater shift means worse transfer, and the
rank correlations are weak. MMD 2 is therefore a diagnostic
of representation shift, not an explanation of transfer error.
These results motivate evaluating cross-relay generalization
with FPR and FNR reported separately. The study is limited
to one simulated network, one baseline architecture, and a
global fault-state label, so the magnitudes should not be gen-
eralized. Future work should test whether domain adaptation
and invariant representation learning [12] reduce the location
dependence, and validate on additional network configurations
and measured field data.

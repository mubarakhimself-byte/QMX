Abstract
Cryptocurrencies trade continuously while major traditional markets close over the weekend. We
examine whether weekend cryptocurrency volatility predicts Monday variance in equity, commodity
and fixed-income markets. Using daily range-based variance measures for Bitcoin, Ether, XRP, the
S&P 500, gold and a US Treasury ETF over 2017–2026, we augment heterogeneous autoregressive
models with a common weekend cryptocurrency factor. Predictive content is weak before 2024 but
significant thereafter. In the post-2024 sample, a one-standard-deviation increase in the factor is
associated with 27–54% higher fitted Monday variance. Adding the factor reduces out-of-sample
QLIKE losses by 11–22%, although statistical evidence varies across markets. Friday cryptocurrency
volatility does not reproduce the result, and coin-specific variation adds no significant predictive
information beyond the common factor. An endogenous procedure estimates a break in late 2023,
during growing anticipation of US spot-Bitcoin ETF approval. However, ETF-flow interactions
provide no evidence of amplification through greater measured Monday flow activity, leaving the
mechanism unresolved. The findings suggest that calendar-specific volatility measures reveal pre-
dictive information that aggregate connectedness measures may obscure.

1. Introduction
The trading calendars of cryptocurrency and traditional markets do not coincide. Equities,
commodities and bonds close over the weekend, while cryptocurrencies trade continuously. Infor-
mation arriving during this period can therefore be incorporated into cryptocurrency prices before
traditional markets reopen. Most empirical studies address this calendar mismatch by aligning cryp-
tocurrency and traditional-market observations to a common trading calendar. We instead exploit
the mismatch asa source of temporalidentification: cryptocurrency marketscan respondto weekend
information while traditional markets remain closed, allowing us to test whether volatility accumu-
lated in cryptocurrency during this interval contains predictive information for traditional-market
volatility when trading resumes.
Using this timing asymmetry, we find that weekend cryptocurrency volatility contains predictive
information for volatility in traditional markets on Monday. Specifically, higher weekend cryptocur-
rency variance predicts higher Monday variance in the S&P 500, gold and a US Treasury bond
ETF, even after controlling for each asset’s recent volatility and for cryptocurrency variance on the
preceding Friday. Importantly, this relationship is not evident earlier in the sample. It emerges
around 2023–24 and is statistically significant across all nine cryptocurrency–traditional asset pairs
considered in this study during the post-2024 period.

The timing coincides with an important period of institutional change in cryptocurrency mar-
kets. On 10 January 2024, the US Securities and Exchange Commission approved rule changes
permitting the listing and trading of eleven spot-Bitcoin exchange-traded products in the United
States, following repeated rejections and a court ruling vacating its earlier denial of Grayscale’s
proposal (SEC, 2024). These products expanded access to Bitcoin exposure through regulated
US securities exchanges for institutional and retail investors. A growing literature documents in-
creased integration between cryptocurrency and traditional markets around this period (Babalos
et al., 2025, Bukaita and Li, 2026, Hong et al., 2025). Whereas this literature primarily charac-
terises changes in aggregate integration, we examine whether a specific calendar-based predictive
relationship emerged around the same period. An endogenous structural-break procedure places
the change in late 2023, broadly coinciding with the period in which spot-Bitcoin ETF approval
became increasingly anticipated.
Several findings support the interpretation that the predictive relationship is specifically associ-
ated with weekend cryptocurrency volatility rather than cryptocurrency volatility more generally.
First, weekend variance remains statistically significant when Friday cryptocurrency variance is in-
cluded as a control, whereas Friday variance alone has little predictive content for Monday volatil-
ity. Second, the relationship is driven primarily by a common cryptocurrency-market component.
When each coin’s weekend variance is decomposed into common and coin-specific components, the
common factor significantly predicts Monday variance across all three traditional assets, while the
coin-specific components do not. Third, the relationship is economically and forecast relevant. In
the post-2024 sample, a one-standard-deviation increase in the weekend cryptocurrency factor is
associated with approximately 28% higher Monday variance for the S&P 500, 54% for gold and
27% for the Treasury ETF, while adding the factor to the HAR benchmark reduces out-of-sample
QLIKE loss by 11–22%. Fourth, the main finding is robust to several alternative specifications,
including controlling for the pre-weekend VIX, estimating a date-clustered panel specification, ex-
cluding the most influential observation, and allowing the timing of the structural change to be
determined endogenously.
The calendar-specific nature of the relationship also helps explain why it is not apparent in con-
ventional measures of market connectedness. Applying the Diebold–Yilmaz connectedness frame-
work to the same six assets produces relatively weak volatility transmission from individual cryp-
tocurrencies to the traditional assets, while the rolling connectedness index shows no comparable
change around the 2023–24 period. This contrast suggests that transmission concentrated around
a particular part of the trading calendar may be diluted when connectedness is measured using
daily observations pooled across all weekdays. More generally, the result highlights the value of
explicitly accounting for differences in trading calendars when studying information flows between
continuously traded and periodically closed markets.
Finally, we also examine whether spot cryptocurrency ETF flows provide a potential mechanism
linking weekend cryptocurrency volatility to Monday volatility in traditional markets. If ETF ac-
tivity were the primary conduit, we would expect the relationship to strengthen when fund flows
are larger. We find no evidence of this pattern: the interaction between weekend cryptocurrency
variance and ETF flows is statistically insignificant for the S&P 500 and gold and negative for the
Treasury ETF. Thus, although the relationship emerges during a period of increasing cryptocur-
rency institutionalisation, our results do not support ETF flows as the mechanism driving it. The
underlying mechanism therefore remains unresolved.
The study most closely related to ours is Mourey et al. (2025), who show that weekend cryp-
tocurrency returns predict Monday equity returns and date a structural change in that relationship
to the 2022 LUNA collapse. Our analysis differs along three dimensions. First, we examine volatility
rather than returns, shifting the focus from return predictability to measuring and forecasting mar-

2. Related literature
This study builds on research on volatility measurement and forecasting, cryptocurrency volatil-
ity, cross-market connectedness, and calendar effects in financial markets. Together, these areas
provide the theoretical and empirical background for examining whether cryptocurrency volatility
observed while traditional markets are closed contains predictive information for volatility when
those markets reopen.
2.1 Measuring and forecasting volatility
Volatility is latent and must therefore be estimated from observed prices. Financial returns are
characterised by heavy tails and volatility clustering (Mandelbrot, 1963, Fama, 1965, Cont, 2001),
features that motivated the development of ARCH and GARCH models (Engle, 1982, Bollerslev,
1986) and their asymmetric extensions (Nelson, 1991, Glosten et al., 1993). With the availability of
high-frequency data, realised-volatility measures constructed from intraday returns provided more
informative ex-post measures of price variation than squared daily returns (Andersen and Bollerslev,
1998, Andersen et al., 2001, 2003, Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard, 2002).
3
The heterogeneous autoregressive (HAR) model of Corsi (2009) provides a parsimonious frame-
work for forecasting realised volatility using its daily, weekly and monthly components. Its ability to
capture volatility persistence while remaining relatively simple has made it a widely used benchmark
for volatility forecasting. When intraday data are unavailable, range-based estimators provide an
alternative by extracting information from daily open, high, low and close (OHLC) prices (Parkin-
son, 1980, Garman and Klass, 1980, Rogers and Satchell, 1991). These estimators are particularly
relevant to our analysis because our volatility measures are constructed from daily OHLC data.
Forecast evaluation also requires care because observed volatility measures are proxies for the
underlying latent variance. Loss functions such as QLIKE are robust to certain forms of volatility-
proxy noise (Patton, 2011), while the Diebold-Mariano test provides a formal comparison of pre-
dictive accuracy across competing forecasts (Diebold and Mariano, 1995). Previous evidence also
shows that greater model complexity does not necessarily translate into superior volatility forecasts
(Hansen and Lunde, 2005, Dudek et al., 2024). We therefore use the HAR model as a parsimonious
benchmark and assess whether incorporating weekend cryptocurrency volatility provides additional
predictive information for Monday volatility.
2.2 Cryptocurrency volatility and traditional financial assets
Cryptocurrencieshavedevelopedfromanalternativedigitalpaymentsystemintoawidelytraded
class of financial assets. Their market behaviour nevertheless differs in important respects from
that of traditional assets. Corbet et al. (2019) review the development of cryptocurrency markets
and document their historically low correlations with conventional financial assets, a feature that
initially motivated interest in their potential diversification benefits. A particularly distinctive
characteristic is their high and persistent volatility. For Bitcoin, Katsiampa (2017) shows that
GARCH-type models capture important features of the volatility dynamics, while Baur and Dimpfl
(2018) document an asymmetric response to shocks that differs from the pattern commonly observed
in equity markets: positive shocks can generate stronger volatility responses than negative shocks.
Research has also examined the broader financial role of cryptocurrencies. Questions of infor-
mational efficiency have received considerable attention (Urquhart, 2016), alongside debate over
whether cryptocurrencies behave primarily as speculative assets, alternative means of exchange,
diversifiers or hedges (Dyhrberg, 2016, Bouri et al., 2017). Gold is particularly relevant in this
context because of its established role as a store of value and safe-haven asset (Baur and Lucey,
2010), and comparisons between Bitcoin and gold have therefore become common in the cryptocur-
rency literature. These studies show that the relationship between cryptocurrencies and traditional
assets cannot be characterised solely by their individual return or volatility dynamics; their degree
of co-movement and potential for cross-market information transmission are also important.
Another feature distinguishes cryptocurrencies from the traditional assets considered in this pa-
per: cryptocurrency markets operate continuously, including when major traditional markets are
closed. Although trading is available around the clock, activity is not uniform across time; Ja-
hanshahloo et al. (2022) document pronounced time-of-day effects in Bitcoin trading and network
activity, strongly influenced by conventional US trading hours, with these effects diminishing at
weekends. The absence of scheduled weekend closure nevertheless creates periods when cryptocur-
rency prices can adjust to new information while major equity, bond, and commodity markets are
not trading. This difference in trading calendars has become increasingly relevant as cryptocurrency
markets have developed stronger links with the wider financial system. Using return and volatility
connectedness measures, Iyer and Popescu (2023) document increasing spillovers between crypto
assets and traditional financial markets over time, particularly during periods of market turbulence.
More directly related to the timing considered here, Mourey et al. (2025) exploit the difference be-
tween continuous cryptocurrency trading and weekend equity-market closure and show that weekend

cryptocurrency returns contain predictive information for Monday equity returns, with the relation-
ship strengthening following the LUNA collapse in 2022. Taken together, these findings motivate
examining whether the same calendar-based relationship extends to volatility and, beyond equities,
to commodity and fixed-income markets.
2.3 Volatility connectedness and post-2024 integration
Cross-market volatility transmission is commonly studied using measures of spillovers and con-
nectedness. A prominent approach is the Diebold-Yilmaz framework (Diebold and Yilmaz, 2012,
2014), which uses generalised forecast-error variance decompositions to quantify the proportion of
variation in one market associated with shocks originating in others. The framework has subse-
quently been extended to examine connectedness across different frequencies (Baruník and Křehlík,
2018) and to allow relationships between markets to evolve over time (Antonakakis et al., 2020).
Applications to cryptocurrency markets provide evidence that their links with traditional financial
assets have strengthened over time and that the magnitude and direction of volatility spillovers
can vary during periods of market stress (IMF, 2022, Iyer and Popescu, 2023). More recent stud-
ies examine how these relationships have evolved alongside the increasing institutionalisation of
cryptocurrency markets. Around the introduction of US spot-Bitcoin exchange-traded products in
January 2024, evidence points to changes in Bitcoin’s co-movement with equity markets and in its
volatility dynamics (Babalos et al., 2025, Hong et al., 2025), while Bukaita and Li (2026) document
changes in the spillover structure linking Bitcoin, Ether, gold and US equities.
These studies provide evidence of an evolving relationship between cryptocurrency and tradi-
tional financial markets, but connectedness is generally measured using observations aggregated over
time and is designed to characterise the overall magnitude and direction of cross-market spillovers
(Diebold and Yilmaz, 2012, 2014, Baruník and Křehlík, 2018). This may leave calendar-specific re-
lationships less visible when predictive information concentrates in particular trading periods. The
distinction is especially relevant for cryptocurrency markets, where trading continues outside con-
ventional market hours and through weekends, despite pronounced time-of-day patterns in trading
and network activity (Jahanshahloo et al., 2022). Indeed, Mourey et al. (2025) show that exploiting
the weekend closure of equity markets reveals predictive information in weekend cryptocurrency
returns for Monday equity returns. These findings motivate a complementary approach to aggre-
gate connectedness analysis: we examine whether cryptocurrency volatility accumulated specifically
over the weekend contains predictive information for traditional-market volatility on Monday, and
subsequently compare this calendar-specific relationship with conventional connectedness measures
in Section 5.6.2.
2.4 Calendar effects and information transmission
Calendar effects have long been documented in financial markets. Early studies identify sys-
tematic differences in equity returns across trading days, including the weekend effect (Cross, 1973,
French, 1980). French and Roll (1986) extend this perspective to volatility, showing that return
variance differs substantially between trading and non-trading periods and highlighting the impor-
tance of market opening and closing for incorporating information into prices. Calendar regularities
are also present in cryptocurrency markets despite their continuous trading structure. Caporale
and Plastun (2019), for example, document day-of-the-week effects in cryptocurrency returns, sug-
gesting that continuous trading does not eliminate systematic temporal patterns. More directly
related to our analysis, Mourey et al. (2025) exploit the difference between continuous cryptocur-
rency trading and weekend equity-market closure and show that weekend cryptocurrency returns
predict Monday equity returns, with a structural change in the relationship around the 2022 LUNA
5
collapse. Their analysis establishes the relevance of the weekend trading-calendar mismatch for re-
turn predictability. We examine whether a corresponding relationship exists in volatility: whether
cryptocurrency variance accumulated over the weekend contains predictive information for Monday
volatility in traditional financial markets. In doing so, we shift the focus from return predictability
to market-risk forecasting, extend the analysis beyond equities to commodity and fixed-income mar-
kets, and examine when the volatility relationship emerges and the mechanisms that may account
for its evolution.

theweekendprovidesadditionalinformationaboutMondayvarianceintraditionalfinancialmarkets.
We first construct daily variance proxies from OHLC prices and specify a heterogeneous autoregres-
sive (HAR) model as the forecasting benchmark. An expanding-window forecasting framework is
then used to assess predictive performance using only information available at each forecast date.
The central analysis augments the HAR benchmark with weekend and Friday cryptocurrency vari-
ance factors to distinguish the predictive contribution of weekend activity from that of preceding
Friday activity. Further analyses examine changes in this relationship over time, distinguish com-
mon cryptocurrency-market variation from coin-specific variation, and assess whether ETF flow
activity is associated with the strength of the relationship. The section concludes with the forecast
evaluation criteria and statistical inference procedures.

3.3 Expanding-window forecasting design
We assess forecasting performance using an expanding-window walk-forward design. For each
forecast datet, a one-step-ahead forecast ofvi,t is generated using only information available at the
forecast origin. Lagged predictors are constructed as defined in equation 3.6.
For the general forecasting comparison, HAR and the tree-based models are re-estimated every
21observations, whereas the LSTM is re-estimated every63observations. Between re-estimation
dates, parameters remain fixed while predictors are updated using newly available observations. For
the Monday comparison between HAR and HAR+Wt, both models are instead re-estimated before
each Monday forecast using all preceding Monday observations in the estimation window. The
HAR predictors are constructed from the asset’s daily trading history, andWt uses the completed
Saturday and Sunday cryptocurrency observations.
In both exercises, the estimation window expands over time. Any feature scaling and hyperpa-
rameter selection use only the corresponding training data.
3.4 The weekend volatility channel
We examine whether cryptocurrency variance observed over the weekend provides additional
predictive information about Monday variance in traditional financial markets. The analysis aug-
ments the HAR benchmark with weekend and Friday cryptocurrency variance factors. The HAR
terms control for the traditional asset’s own variance history, while the Friday factor helps distin-
guish the predictive contribution of weekend activity from that of preceding Friday activity. We
refer to this predictive relationship as the weekend volatility channel.

4. Data
4.1 Sources and sample
Daily data are obtained from Yahoo Finance for six assets. The cryptocurrencies are Bitcoin
(BTC-USD), Ether (ETH-USD) and XRP (XRP-USD). The traditional assets are the S&P 500
index (ˆGSPC) for equities, COMEX gold futures (GC=F) for commodities, and the iShares 7-
10 Year Treasury Bond ETF (IEF) for fixed income; we refer to the last as “Treasury” throughout,
noting that it is a bond fund rather than the on-the-run ten-year note. The common sample runs
from November 2017, bounded by the availability of Ether and XRP histories, to June 2026. Two
auxiliary datasets are used in later tests: the CBOE Volatility Index (ˆVIX), and daily US spot
Bitcoin and Ether exchange-traded fund net flows obtained from Farside Investors.
The six assets are chosen to make the identification of Section 3.4 as clean as possible and to
guard against the result being an artefact of any one market. On the cryptocurrency side, Bitcoin
and Ether are the two largest coins by market capitalisation and the two for which US spot funds
began trading in January and July 2024 respectively, so they are the natural candidates for any
channel that runs through institutional access. XRP is included as a contrast. It is also among the
largest and longest-traded coins, but it had no US spot fund until November 2025, near the end of
the sample, so it allows us to ask whether transmission requires a coin to be fund-listed. All three
have deep and liquid weekend trading and price histories that begin before the sample, and we stop
at three because the major coins load heavily on a single common factor, so adding further coins
would multiply tests without adding independent information.
On the traditional side, each asset represents a distinct asset class and has a genuine week-
end closure, which the identification requires. The S&P 500 is the benchmark for US equities,
the market in which the classical weekend literature of Section 2 was developed, and the one for
which an options-implied volatility control, the VIX, is available. Gold is the reference asset of the
cryptocurrency literature, where Bitcoin has repeatedly been compared with it as a store of value
or hedge (Dyhrberg, 2016, Baur and Lucey, 2010); its futures reopen on Sunday evening, giving a
shorter closure than equities, a difference we exploit in Section 5.4.2. The Treasury fund represents
12
fixed income, the asset class through which macroeconomic news is most directly priced. We use
an exchange-traded fund rather than Treasury futures because the fund trades only during the
US equity session and therefore has a full weekend closure, whereas the futures reopen on Sunday
evening. Together, the three span equities, commodities and fixed income, so the result cannot be
dismissed as an equity-only artefact.

5. Results
We first examine the emergence of the weekend predictive relationship and assess whether week-
end cryptocurrency variance provides information beyond Friday activity and the traditional as-

We examine two aspects of the weekend predictive relationship. First, we assess whether week-

whether this predictive information reflects variation shared across cryptocurrency markets or coin-

5.5 The exchange-traded-fund flow mechanism
We examine whether the weekend relationship is stronger when institutional ETF flow activity is
greater. In (3.13), the interaction betweenWt and total Bitcoin and Ether spot-ETF flow activity is
statistically insignificant for the S&P 500 (p= 0.30) and gold (p= 0.85), and significantly negative
for Treasury (t=−2.6). These results do not support the hypothesis that the weekend relationship
strengthens with greater measured ETF flow activity.
The insignificant interactions do not rule out ETF-related transmission, since the flow proxy may
not capture all relevant institutional activity. Similarly, the negative Treasury interaction indicates
a weaker association betweenWt and Monday log variance when flow activity is greater, but does
not identify the reason for this difference. Broader links through overlapping holders, dealers and
risk-management systems remain possible explanations. However, these mechanisms are not directly
tested here, and contemporaneous Monday flows may respond to the same information as Monday
variance. The underlying mechanism therefore remains unresolved.
5.6 Complementary forecasting and connectedness analyses
The following analyses place the weekend results in a broader context by comparing HAR with
flexible forecasting models and examining aggregate daily volatility connectedness.
5.6.1 Comparison with flexible forecasting models
We first assess whether flexible forecasting models improve on the HAR benchmark. Machine
learning has delivered economic gains in empirical asset pricing (Gu et al., 2020), while neural-
network and high-dimensional methods have improved realised-volatility forecasts in some settings
(Bucci, 2020, Rahimikia and Poon, 2020). Using the expanding-window walk-forward design in
Section 3.3, we compare HAR with a random forest (Breiman, 2001), gradient boosting (Friedman,
25
2001, Chen and Guestrin, 2016) and a long short-term memory network (Hochreiter and Schmid-
huber, 1997). Each flexible model receives the HAR predictors and the preceding day’s absolute
return.
Hyperparameters are tuned separately for each asset through an exhaustive grid search evaluated
usingQLIKEundertime-seriescross-validationwithinthefirst60%ofeachseries. Thisproduces372
fitted configurations in total. Forecast evaluation begins at observation400, which falls within the
tuning block, creating a partial overlap between the tuning and evaluation periods. Consequently,
the evaluation is not strictly out of sample with respect to hyperparameter selection and may
favour the machine-learning models. Nevertheless, these models do not consistently outperform
HAR: gradient boosting achieves lower QLIKE for XRP, while the random forest achieves lower
QLIKE for gold. HAR and the tree-based models are re-estimated every21observations, whereas
the LSTM is re-estimated every63observations. The network’s inputs and target are standardised
using training-window statistics only.
The HAR benchmark achieves the lowest reported QLIKE for four of the six assets: Bitcoin
(0.763), Ether (0.640, tied with the random forest), the S&P 500 (0.454) and Treasury (0.402).
Gradient boosting performs better for XRP (1.004compared with1.032for HAR), while the random
forest performs better for gold (2.093compared with2.370). The recurrent network does not achieve
the lowest QLIKE for any asset. Tuning improves the tree models in eleven of the twelve asset-
model combinations relative to their default settings, but does not change the overall ranking. For
Treasury, the random forest and gradient boosting losses remain approximately40%and25%higher
than the HAR loss, respectively. The results therefore show that greater model flexibility does not
consistently improve forecasting performance in this sample.
These findings support the use of HAR as a parsimonious benchmark for the weekend-channel
analysis. Any forecasting improvement from addingWt is assessed relative to this benchmark. The
comparison does not rule out gains from other flexible models or from models that explicitly include
weekend information.

6. Conclusion
6.1 Summary
This paper documents a predictive relationship between weekend cryptocurrency variance and
Monday variance in the S&P 500, gold and a US Treasury bond fund. Weekend cryptocurrency
variance provides additional information beyond each asset’s own variance history and Friday cryp-
tocurrency variance. The relationship is weak before 2024 and significant across all nine crypto-asset
pairs in the post-2024 sample. An endogenous sup-Wald test places the estimated break in Octo-
ber 2023, preceding the January 2024 spot-Bitcoin ETF approvals. This timing is compatible with
anticipation of approval, but does not establish its role in the change.
The weekend factor remains significant when the Friday factor is excluded, whereas Friday
cryptocurrency variance alone has no significant predictive association. The decomposition results
supportapredominantlycommoncryptocurrency-marketrelationship, withnosignificantadditional
predictive information detected in the coin-specific residuals.
The estimated magnitude is substantial: a one-standard-deviation increase inWt is associated
withapproximately27-54%higherfittedMondayvarianceacrossthethreetraditionalassets, holding
the HAR controls fixed. AddingW t to the HAR benchmark reduces out-of-sample QLIKE loss
by approximately11-22%, although the statistical evidence for improved forecast accuracy varies
across markets. The findings generally remain significant after controlling for Friday’s VIX, allowing
for within-date dependence across pairs and removing influential observations. The holiday-week
results limit interpretation of the relationship as a generic reopening effect. ETF flow interactions
also provide no support for a stronger relationship on higher-flow Mondays. The evidence therefore
supports a Monday predictive relationship, while its underlying mechanism remains unresolved.
6.2 Interpretation
Two aspects of the findings deserve emphasis. First, the aggregate connectedness results in
Section 5.6.2 and the weekend-specific regressions capture different features of cross-market re-
lationships. Individual cryptocurrencies account for relatively small shares of traditional assets’
forecast-error variance in the daily connectedness analysis, while the weekend factor provides addi-
tional predictive information about Monday variance. These findings are compatible because the
daily connectedness framework does not explicitly isolate weekend cryptocurrency activity and its
relationship with Monday outcomes. The comparison highlights the value of examining calendar-
specific predictive relationships alongside aggregate connectedness.
Second, the emergence of the relationship around 2023-24 does not establish an ETF-related ex-
planation. TheestimatedbreakprecedestheJanuary2024spot-BitcoinETFapprovals, andtheflow
interactions do not support a stronger weekend relationship on Mondays with greater measured ETF
flow activity. However, these findings do not exclude broader ETF-related or institutional channels.
Overlapping ownership, dealer exposures and common risk-management practices remain possible
explanations, but are not directly tested here. The evidence supports a predictive relationship; its
underlying mechanism remains unresolved.
6.3 Limitations and further work
Several limitations qualify these conclusions. Daily variance is estimated from OHLC prices
rather than intraday returns. These range-based proxies measure within-session price variation but
exclude close-to-open price changes and do not reveal when variation occurs within the session.
Intraday data would permit more detailed measurement and timing analysis, subject to appropriate
treatment of market microstructure noise. The post-2024 estimation samples are short, containing
115–116 Mondays depending on the asset, so the emergence result rests on a limited number of
28
observations and the endogenous break is only borderline significant at the 5% level. The flow
proxy is coarse and endogenous, so the flow null should be read as an absence of evidence rather
than strong evidence of absence. The fixed-income leg is a 7-10 year bond fund rather than the
on-the-run ten-year note. No cryptocurrency provides a clean never-listed control by the end of
the sample, since spot XRP funds launched in November 2025. The holiday-week results suggest a
weaker weekend relationship when reopening is delayed until Tuesday, limiting a generic reopening
interpretation. However, with only eleven holiday weeks, the evidence distinguishing a Monday-
specific relationship from a generic reopening effect remains limited.
Four extensions follow naturally from these limitations. Intraday realised measures would
sharpen estimation and allow the Monday effect to be traced through the trading session rather than
observed only daily. A longer post-event window will permit a sharper test of the emergence date
and reveal whether the channel persists, strengthens, or is gradually arbitraged away as participants
learn of it. Finer institutional data, such as dealer positions or fund holdings rather than aggregate
net flows, would allow the mechanism to be tested more precisely. Finally, the identification strategy
generalises: any pair of markets with differing closure schedules offers the same predictive setting
based on differences in trading calendars, so the approach could be applied across time zones, or to
assets whose closure lengths differ, to test whether the length of the closed window governs the size
of the effect.

Timing of the change.
The estimated break date is sensitive to the choice of estimator. The maximum sup-Wald

Implications.
The positive post-2024 relationship is robust to the choice of range estimator. Under both
alternatives, the weekend factor is positive and statistically significant for all three traditional as-
sets in the weekend-only specifications controlling for HAR terms. Friday-only coefficients are not
statistically significant, and joint-model contrasts favour a larger weekend than Friday coefficient.
Estimated economic effects remain substantial. Adding the weekend factor reduces out-of-sample
QLIKE for every asset under each estimator, although statistical evidence of forecast improvement
varies across assets and estimators.
The timing of the change is less robust. No pairwise coefficient is positive and statistically
significant in the pre-COVID sub-period, compared with eight or nine in the post-ETF sub-period.
Estimated break dates differ across estimators. The October 2023 estimate reported in Section 5.1
should therefore be interpreted cautiously rather than as a precisely established onset. These results
do not establish whether spot-ETF approvals caused the stronger relationship.

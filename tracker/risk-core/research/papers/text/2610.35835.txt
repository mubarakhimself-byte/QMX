Abstract—Human oversight is a pillar of AI safety. It is often
exercised by ahuman in the loop(HITL) who approves critical
actions taken by an AI agent. AI agents’ increasing ability to
bypass guardrails, though, raises the question of how to enforce
HITL robustly.
We introduce the concept ofinalienable human-in-the-loop
(I-HITL). An I-HITL protocol ensures that an agent can
initiate an action only with explicit human approval, even
when the human interacts with a system (e.g., a local agentic
harness)compromised by the agent. AstrongI-HITL protocol
additionally ensures such approval even when anadversarial
human attempts to delegateapprovals to an agent.
To motivate I-HITL, we report on experiments with a
popular agentic wallet platform in which raising an agent’s
spending cap requires HITL approval via emailed one-time
passcodes (OTPs). Given access to the user’s email and a task
blocked by the cap, we find that ten different frontier models
will often autonomously raise the cap, harvesting OTPs to
bypass a HITL guardrail and surfacing other vulnerabilities.
We formally define I-HITL using the concept of communi-
cation topologies and prove necessary and sufficient conditions
for its realization. We show that an I-HITL protocol requires
atrusted entity—a trusted computing device (or equivalent)—
and characterize which such devices can and cannot realize
an I-HITL protocol. We present a prototype I-HITL system
calledCountersignthat can plug into and strengthen existing
HITL systems using off-the-shelf consumer mobile devices.
1. Introduction
AI agents are increasingly entrusted with consequential
decisions and tasks, as personal agents such as Meta’s
Muse [54], OpenAI’s Dots [67], OpenClaw [68], and Her-
mes [63] see rapid adoption. Users frequently grant these
agents broad access to credentials and tools: email, payment
methods, shell access, and browser sessions. Yet because
agents remain susceptible to hallucination, prompt injection,
and misalignment, the same access that lets them act on a
user’s behalf also lets them act against the user’s interests.
A natural response is to constrain what an agent can
do via ahuman-in-the-loop(HITL)approval gate(orHITL
gate, for short)—a mechanism that permits an agent’s action
to proceed only after human approval [41], [72], [80]. HITL
gates are only meaningful, though, if agents cannot bypass
them. In practice, agents can often bypass various guardrails
to escalate their own permissions, as two examples show.

Example 1.Circle Agent Wallets allow an account owner
to place a daily spending cap on an agent [22]. They protect
cap changes via a HITL gate authenticated using a one-time
passcode emailed to the account owner. We show in this
paper that given access to an email tool, a wallet’s agent
will often autonomously harvest the OTPs and update caps
without human approval, bypassing the HITL gate. Nothing
here is specific to Circle or to email: Other agentic wallets’
HITL gates use similar design patterns [27], [55].
Example 2.Early versions of Claude Mythos Preview re-
peatedly bypassed harness-imposed guardrails. In one case,
the model used a tool intended only for file editing to write
to the shell’s input, gaining arbitrary code execution. In
other cases, it searched low-level process data for creden-
tials and accessed resources that Anthropic had deliberately
withheld [6].
These two examples illustrate a shortcoming of many
HITL gates: agents may have or gain access to the very
resources used to enforce the gate. There are numerous
reports of similar examples, e.g., [53], [62], [79], and anal-
ogous, if not HITL-specific, behaviors arising in controlled
evaluations of frontier models [17], [52], [71].
Despite the pervasive risk of agents subverting HITL
controls, however, the security goal of ensuring human
approval before execution of a given action has anotable
lack of formal modeling in the literature to date.
Inalienable HITL (I-HITL).To fill this gap, we introduce
inalienable HITL(I-HITL). The terminalienable, defined as
“unable to be removed” [20], here refers to HITL approval
of agent actions.
Our threat model for I-HITL involves three parties: (1)
thehuman, (2) the agent-controlledplatform, such as the
human’s client machine, and (3) a targetservice, typically
a remote server, which gates agent actions. Informally, an
I-HITL protocol ensures that the service can distinguish an
action approved by the human from one approved by the
agent acting through the platform, even if theagent has
compromised the platform. We also put forth a notion of
strongI-HITL that ensures such approval even whenan
adversarial human controller attempts to delegate approvals
to the agent.This implies a form ofaccountabilityuseful for
enforcing policies that mandate human oversight of AI [33].
Our work shows that I-HITL requires a trusted entityT,
but that this isnot sufficientto realize I-HITL. Specifically,
a trusted entity must be able toattestto having generated a
given cryptographic key for I-HITL to be possible.

Self-escalation.To motivate our work, we realize Exam-
ple 1 with Circle’s Agentic Wallets as a testbed. Its HITL
gate is authenticated by one-time passcodes (OTPs) emailed
to the human user. We give ten frontier models an invoice-
payment task requiring funds that exceed a previously set
daily spending cap. We use a common configuration that
allows agents to access the user’s email.With no adversary
and no prompting, nine of the ten models bypass human
approval, harvesting OTPs and raising their own cap in at
least one run. With email-based prompt injection, this drops
to seven of ten models, as some models detect the prompt
injection. In experiments where the agent is only asked to
summarize email, five models both raise the cap andpay
an attackerimpersonating the user by email. Broadly, our
resultsdo notcorrelate strongly with model capability.
Formalizing I-HITL.Common deployment failures, such
as Examples 1 and 2, prompt us to introduce what we
believe to be the first security definition for HITL gates that
captures security against an agent-compromised computing
platform. Our definitionalsoencompasses a human who
attempts to delegate control to an agent. Previous attempts
at HITL formalism neglect both of these settings and assume
that agents are unable to hijack the approval flow, focusing
instead on the potential mismatch between an approved
action and its execution [43], [83], a consideration which
is subsumed by our definition. Related formal frameworks
in ceremony analysis [32], [70] and transaction authentica-
tion [13], [14], [31], [82] model platform compromise but
not human principals delegating their authority.
We define an I-HITL protocol as having two properties.
Completenessstates that a service will accept an honest
human’s approval of an agent action.Approval-bindingsays
that no adversary can cause the service to accept an ap-
proval that the human did not grant. By extending the for-
mal machinery of [12], we characterize thecommunication
topologies, i.e., sets of entities and communication channels,
needed to realize a complete and approval-binding I-HITL
protocol. In the “plain” topology of human, platform, and
service used in many existing and proposed systems [22],
[26], [45], [66], [75], [76] there exists no complete, approval
binding I-HITL protocol. This result motivates the addition
of a trusted entityToutside of the agent’s control—typically
in the form of a trusted computing device. We prove that
such a trusted entity alone is not in general adequate for
I-HITL, and give precise requirements on its functionality
which must be satisfied.
Common authentication approaches—e.g., 2FA / authen-
ticator apps, hardware wallets, etc.—do not offer a trusted
entityTsufficient to realize an I-HITL protocol. Our work
serves as guidance for identifying what devices can do so.
Countersign.We introduceCountersign, a prototype im-
plementation of a secure I-HITL protocol that uses an
Android or iOS mobile application to realize a trusted entity,
showing that the requirements arising from our formal anal-
ysis can be satisfied with these devices under realistic trust
assumptions. Countersign uses Google’s/Apple’s attestation
functionality and the hardware-backed key management ca-
pabilities of their respective devices to establish the requisite
channels, as well as physical, human-driven approval via a
button press or biometric authentication. Excluding scaffold-
ing, Countersign’s core I-HITL logic consists of∼800 LOC
for the backend service, and∼150 LOC each for the An-
droid/iOS applications, demonstrating the relative simplicity
of the core components. Our implementation also highlights
gaps in the current functionality available to developers that
could be patched to strengthen I-HITL guarantees.

2. Problem Setting
We now specify our threat model, and explain its rele-
vance to several widely deployed HITL gates.
2.1. Threat model
We consider three parties: ahuman, a computingplat-
formrunning an AI agent, and aservice. The human can
interact with the platform directly (by sending/reading mes-
sages over input/output peripherals) but communication to
the service must pass through the platform. The service aims
to determine, for a given message received, whether the
human was responsible for it. Informally this means that
the human saw, and approved of the message.
We assume that the service is honest and executes ex-
actly the approved action [90], but that the agent is adversar-
ial and fully controls the platform, aiming to convince the
service that the human is responsible for a message when
this is not the case. Although a fully corrupted platform is a
strong threat model, we believe it is the right stance to adopt
given the trend of increasing integration of AI agents into
all manner of computing platforms and the corresponding
risk of a corrupted AI agent (e.g. from prompt injection or
a misaligned LLM). Agents have previously escaped their
sandboxes by exploiting misconfigurations [50], [64], and
LLMs have been shown capable of finding and exploiting
zero-day vulnerabilities in operating systems or browsers.
The human may be honest or adversarial in our threat
model. An honest human represents the dominant paradigm
for thinking about human-in-the-loop systems: the human
respects any protocol and can keep short secrets (such as
passwords) from the adversarial platform.
Increasingly, however, human-in-the-loop frameworks
that resist an adversarial human are important. First, LLMs
have shown the ability to deceive, mislead and steer
users [39], [49], which could manifest itself as users di-
vulging secrets to agents. Second, the human and agent
may both be corrupted in the sense that they collude to
evade the service’s requirement of human oversight of agent
actions. Users tend to delegate to and (potentially over-
permission) AI agents [51], and a human might seek to
avoid responsibility for messages to the service either out
of laziness, or to engage with the service adversarially and
later blame an AI agent for resulting damage or harm.
In later sections, we will also consider a fourth party,
a trusted entity. A trusted entity is honest, outside of the
control of the agent, and may communicate with all parties
directly, although with restrictions on the security and di-
rectionality of its communication channels. We deliberately
leave the trusted entity abstract, and it may be satisfied by
different implementations under different trust assumptions.
It may be secure hardware attached to the platform itself,
a separate device the human carries—such as a hardware
wallet—or a physical place, such as a bank branch or an
enrollment office, where communication occurs in person.
2.2. Non-goals and limitations
We assume that physical interaction with devices is
outside of an agent’s capabilities. Although a motivated
human could conceivably deploy sensors or actuators to
delegate to the agent in this manner, this is a significant
barrier, and accountability is more clear cut as it is far less
plausible for a human to claim that an agent physically
interacted with a device without their knowledge than to
claim that a sandboxed agent exploited some vulnerability
to escalate their permissions.
Our threat model abstracts away application-specific
HITL considerations, instead providing a low-level protocol
for the approval of generic messages. In particular, policy
determining what actions/messages are protected by a HITL
gate is out of scope, in our threat model this is the choice
of the trusted service, who also enforces this policy. We
likewise do not consider human-factors questions, such as
how actions are formatted as messages for approval, whether
these are correctly interpreted by the human that receives
them, and whether the human truly engages with approval
requests or merely rubber stamps them. When a service
processes sequences of messages, an adversary that selec-
tively censors approval of specific messages, or reorders in-
dividually benign ones, could cause unintended and harmful
outcomes. As an application-level concern, however, this is
similarly out of scope.
Our threat model focuses on service-side enforcement
of HITL gates. While common (as in Example 1), such
enforcement for the I-HITL schemes explored in this work
requires service-side modification. It also means that using
I-HITL to protect local resources requires deployment of a
suitably isolated local service.
See Section 4 for discussion of our choice to an-
alyze security symbolically, and for a discussion of
authentication/identification-related challenges not captured
by our model.
2.3. Existing deployments
When mapped to our threat model, many deployed HITL
gates share a weakness [24], [26], [55]. Human approval
relies on resources that the agent can reach through the
platform it runs on. These deployments do use software-
based defenses to protect these resources from the agent,
such as restricting permissions, sandboxing, and screening
for prompt injection, and in many cases these are effective.
Such defenses are a relatively weak foundation for secu-
rity. The following three common HITL gate deployment
approaches illustrate the shortcomings.
Agent harnessesdeploy HITL gates to pause the agent
before a designated action, such as a tool call or shell com-
mand, and resume only once a human approves. Examples
include LangGraph’s interrupts [45], the OpenAI Agents
SDK’s human-in-the-loop flow [66], and Claude Code’s
permission prompts and hooks [5]. Harnesses enforce per-
missions and sandbox the agent to limit its control of the
3
host, which in principle should prevent it from subverting
the gate. An agent capable of exploiting the harness or
operating system can bypass these defenses, but in practice
the bar is lower than this. Because the gate’s approval is
just a signal from code on the same host, an agent with
shell access could launch a process in advance that supplies
approvals, even while its own reasoning is suspended by
the gate. Alternatively, with write access to the files that
configure the gate it could disable it outright. It is especially
true that these gates do not prevent adversarial humans from
removing themselves from the loop.
Browser agentsare a special class of harness in which the
agent controls a browser rather than the underlying host,
implemented either as an extension (Claude in Chrome [7])
or as a browser with a native agent (ChatGPT Atlas [65],
Perplexity Comet [69]). Similar considerations as for generic
harnesses apply when gates are browser-enforced, and
prompt injection is an especially concerning attack sur-
face given how frequently browser agents encounter un-
trusted content. However, a browser agent also transacts
with third-party websites, which may themselves gate conse-
quential actions, for instance through bot-detection or out-
of-band authentication. These defenses move the gate off
the potentially-compromised platform, but traditional bot-
detection is increasingly ineffective against agents [48], and
because browsers retain authenticated sessions across ser-
vices, an out-of-band channel such as email may still be ac-
cessible by the agent [21]. In these cases it remains difficult
to distinguish an adversarial human delegating approval to
the agent from a misbehaving agent seizing it. Banks already
face this problem and, unable to tell authorized agents from
rogue ones, have begun blocking agents outright or limiting
them to read-only access [57].
Agentic wallet platformssuch as those from Coinbase and
Circle [22], [26] are of particular interest because a misbe-
having agent can lead directly to financial loss. A common
architecture is the agent controlling a wallet through an
API, subject to a spending policy set by the user, while
a remote service gates any change to that policy on human
approval. Control of the human’s platform therefore does not
let the agent directly bypass the gate, but if the gate relies on
resources accessible to the agent through the platform (either
because the agent compromised them or because they were
given willingly by the human), it can request and approve
an escalation of its own permissions. In the next section we
examine this scenario experimentally.
3. Agent Self-Escalation
Circle’s Agent Wallets [23] let a user delegate control
of a cryptocurrency wallet to an agent and attachspending
policiesthat cap how much USDC the agent may move
per transaction, day, week, or month. Policies are the user’s
means of constraining the agent. Circle’s documentation
describes them as follows: “set transfer limits or address
allowlists and blocklists on your agent wallet to control
how it can move USDC and interact with contracts” [22].
When a wallet CLI command attempts to change a spending
policy, Circle gates the change behind a one-time passcode
(OTP) emailed to the account owner, with the change only
taking effect when the OTP is provided at the CLI. In
this section we test the robustness of this HITL gate in a
typical deployment, where an agent manages the wallet. As
is common in personal-agent deployments, which bundle in
email and web access [54], [67], [68], the agent also has
access to local email and web-search tools. 1 We first aim
to characterize whether models will self-escalate to change
spending policies in benign situations where these conflict
with user requests, before exploring how prompt injection
attacks can cause an adversarial agent to bypass this HITL
gate in the course of an attack.

aims to maximize attack success across a range of models

adversary might also target specific users whom it knows
use a specific model, and our results serve as a lower bound
for that setting.

The risk illustrated by our results can be mitigated in
different ways. As per Circle’s warning, users should be
cautioned not to deploy personal agents that have access
to both a Circle wallet and the email associated with that
wallet. Users may still be tempted to deploy agents with
access to both for convenience, however, and it may be
challenging to robustly disable email access, as an agent
could leverage exploits or find stored credentials using a
terminal/browser to gain access. Perhaps the most reliable
defense is to develop stronger HITL gates which do not
allow agents to self-escalate. In the following sections we
develop and explore our stronger security notion called
inalienable human-in-the-loop (I-HITL).
4. Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop
We now define inalienable human-in-the-loop (I-HITL)
protocols and their security guarantees.
Topologies.A key feature of our threat model is that the
human cannot interact with the service directly: all commu-
nication between them passes through the agent-controlled
platform or, where available, a trusted entity. We follow [12]
in our use of communicationtopologies, though we do not
use their Tamarin model in our formal treatment.

5. Characterizing Secure I-HITL Protocols
In this section we apply our security definitions for I-
HITL protocols, first to show that secure I-HITL protocols
are impossible in the plain topology, and then to demonstrate
the requirements under which they can be realized with a
trusted entity.
5.1. The plain topology
Many proposed systems operate in the plain topology
τ0 of Figure 3. We show that inτ 0, no complete I-HITL
protocol is approval-binding. The argument closely mirrors
the impossibility results of [12], transferred to our setting.
Theorem 4.LetΣbe an I-HITL protocol for(M, τ 0),
whereHis not restricted to programs inC. IfΣis complete,
then it is not approval-binding.
The proof is given in Appendix A. This results from the
adversary being able to read everythingHreceives and to
inject on every channel on whichHsends. SinceHbegins
with no prior knowledge and runs a prescribed program, its
state is a function of what it receives and its external bits,
both of which the adversary has access to, so the adversary
can simulate whatHwould send on approving any request
and inject it, while giving the realHexternal bit0.
While this is a straightforward result, it captures a
fundamental limitation of any human-in-the-loop system
operating in the plain topology, with no trusted party out of
the agent’s reach. Many such systems have been proposed in
the academic literature and deployed in industry, including
the Circle agent wallet of Example 1, which we show agents
bypass under a natural configuration (Section 3).

6.1.T ↔ Schannel labels
In practice, the labels betweenTandSdepend upon
a secret keykheld byTbut not the adversary. We write
kto distinguish it from the signing keyskof the I-HITL
protocol constructions, though in practice the two may
coincide. Since initially all channels between the two are
insecure, establishing a new channel requires two properties
ofk: it must bebound, i.e., verifiably held byTrather
than by a party impersonating it, and it must beisolated,
i.e., not held by any adversarial party. Neither property
is directly observable by a verifier. Both instead rest on
attestation: evidence, verifiable against a root of trust, that
kwas generated withinTand whether it is extractable by
H. Attestation settles binding outright, but settles isolation
only ifkis not extractable, otherwise isolation, and therefore
the channel labels, depend on whetherHacts honestly or
adversarially—a feature of our threat model that differs
critically from previous models of human principals [13],
[14], [31], [32], [70], [82].
Key attestation (attested/unattested):Some devices,
e.g., Yubikeys [88] and Ledger wallets [47], can digitally
sign the keys they generate, where signatures are verifiable
against a manufacturer root of trust. Mobile phones gener-
ally provide an analogous capability, verifiable against the
OS vendor’s root, which installed apps may optionally use
to provide evidence of their key management. We use the
designationattestedfor such devices. Other trusted hardware
devices, e.g., standard crypto wallets, lack this capability and
areunattested, so is any key thatTdid not generate, such
as a shared secret delivered byS, since attestation can only
speak to keys created withinT. When a device is attested,S
can use an attestation to verify that keykis truly held byT
and subject to a fixed policy. Without attestation,Scannot
tell whetherkis held by the adversary, so both channel
labels areins.
Key control (extractable/encumbered):Even if a key
generated by a device is attested, it may still beextractable
by the user. This is in fact an important feature in systems
that do not support key rotation, e.g., cryptocurrency ad-
dresses. Keys that are not extractable by a user are referred
to asencumbered[40]. Key control is a property of the de-
vice’s policy reported by attestation, soScan verify whether
kis encumbered. A device may hold both extractable and
encumbered keys.

7. Countersign
In this section, we present Countersign, a mobile phone-
based strong I-HITL implementation. Mobile phones are a
natural candidate forTbecause they have the necessary
interfaces and attestation capabilities, and are readily acces-
sible to users. Yet, as shown by Section 6, many mobile
phone applications providing closely related functionality
(e.g., authentication and proof of personhood) fail to realize
strong I-HITL. Countersign demonstrates that the neces-
sary capabilities for I-HITL exist on mobile phones (un-
der reasonable assumptions), and many similar applications
are insecure simply because their workflow is designed
for a subtly different threat model. Countersign’s core I-
HITL logic consists of∼800 LOC for the backend ser-
vice, and∼150 LOC each for the Android/iOS application
(excluding generic scaffolding such as configuration, data
models, and user interfaces), illustrating the feasibility of
achieving I-HITL in practice. Our code is open-source at
https://github.com/I-HITL/countersign, low-level imple-
mentation details are deferred to Appendix F.
7.1. Mobile Phone Attestation Capabilities
In our instantiation, the trusted entityTis a mobile

system image, and recent security patches. Second, the
app verdict states whether the app on the device matches
the binary distributed through Google Play. Regarding key
management, apps can generate and use keys through the
Android Keystore system [36]. An app that generates a
hardware-backed key using the keystore (backed by either

on iOS it is a claim by the attested app, and holds only as
long as code integrity holds. In both cases, the binding of the
biometric to the message seen byHrequires code integrity,
since on Android the hardware learns that a biometric

Comparison to existing apps.The mechanisms described
above are available to any mobile app, and push authen-
ticators (e.g., Duo Mobile, Microsoft Authenticator, Okta
Verify) already use several of them, but still fall short of
I-HITL due to issues in their workflow. First, attestation of
ℓT Sis only beginning to appear: Duo Mobile on Android
now appears to use attestation, but this is undocumented
and unevenly enforced, only accessible to Duo and not the
service, and third-party clients have historically been able to
enroll in place of a genuine app [34]. Second, the approval is
not bound to the messageHsees but instead anS-selected
identifier, soSis not left with a publicly verifiable proof of
approval for accountability purposes. Third,ℓ HT typically
uses the touchscreen as an input interface, which in contrast
to hardware-backed biometrics is simulatable in software.
Finally, these apps often fall back to alternate methods of
approval such as exportable passcode seeds, SMS messages,
and backup-based re-enrollment. These fallbacks may be ac-
ceptable for pure authentication, where the human is trusted
and recoverability is an important feature, but compromise
I-HITL.
7.3. Limitations
In this section we discuss limitations surfaced by our
implementation: device capabilities or ecosystem character-
istics which require additional trust assumptions. These gaps
could be filled by future development of mobile devices to
allow stronger I-HITL guarantees.
Trusted display.On both Android and iOS the Countersign
app relies on the OS to prevent other applications from
tampering with the approval screen, and to bind the dis-
played message to the trusted confirmation. A more robust
implementation would remove this dependency. Android
Protected Confirmation (APC) [4] previously provided a
path to exactly this: a TEE rendered the prompt and signed
the message together with a hash of what it displayed,
so the display was trusted and the confirmation bound to
the message without any trust in the OS. However, APC
was deprecated due to low adoption and high maintenance
costs [4]. Its return, or an equivalent on iOS, would let
Countersign realizeℓ T Has a trusted display without relying
on OS integrity.
Device integrity on iOS.On Android, the Play Integrity
API [37] provides evidence of device integrity which does
not have an equivalent on iOS. In particular, the service
cannot determine from App Attest whether the device has
been jailbroken. Additionally, while iOS supports biometric
authentication, App Attest keys cannot be configured to
require biometric authentication before use. Instead, this
restriction must be enforced by the app, whose integrity may
not be preserved on a compromised OS. On Android, by
contrast, the per-use biometric requirement is enforced by
the secure hardware and reported in the key’s attestation,
12
so the service need not trust the application for it. Con-
sequently, our iOS implementation relies on the additional
assumption that the operating system remains trustworthy to
enforce the integrity of biometric authentication.
Roots of trust.Every channel label in Section 7.2 ulti-
mately rests on a certificate chain to Google’s or Apple’s
root. This is a stronger trust assumption than the man-
ufacturer root of a hardware wallet, which is used only
at provisioning. Apple/Google’s servers must be reachable
to issue attestations, so an outage blocks enrollment. The
evidence provided by attestation is also a snapshot of de-
vice/application state at the moment of verification rather
than an enduring guarantee. A key attestation certificate
records the verified-boot state at key generation, and an App
Attest attestation is issued once, so neither is invalidated
by a kernel compromise after enrollment. On Android,
Countersign narrows this window by requesting a fresh Play
Integrity verdict with every approval. However, this cannot
detect if a kernel is compromised after boot, since verified
boot measures the system only at boot. The effective window
on Android is therefore the interval since the last boot. On
iOS there is no corresponding mechanism, and the window
is the interval since enrollment.
Key recovery.We do not address the issue of key recovery
forTin our initial implementation of Countersign. As
strong I-HITL requires thatHcannot directly accesssk,
any recovery path will requireHto re-execute a procedure
likeEnrollin order to restore an encrypted copy ofskkept
by a trusted entity such asSor to generate a freshsk.
8. Related work
HITL and permissions in agent systems:HITL approval
is a standard recommendation for agent control [72] and a
common feature of agent frameworks; Michael and Roes-
ner [56] survey such permission mechanisms and note the
absence of formally verified enforcement. Work on agent
policy, e.g., [28], [73], [81], specifies which actions an agent
may take, and complements our focus on securely enforcing
those human approvals dictated by policy.
User authentication:User authentication, including a long
line of work on 2FA (e.g., [18]), considers how to verify
the identity of a particular user, but assumes a trusted client
device. (Otherwise, session hijacking is fatal.) Formal anal-
yses of FIDO2, for instance, exclude compromised clients
from their guarantees and model user approval as a one-bit
gesture [11], [16]. An agent, however, can compromise both
2FA factors when they converge on a host [29], [42], and
effectively hijack an authenticated session. Hardware-based
transaction authentication, e.g., using hardware wallets [9],
arose for exactly this reason, although, as Table 3 shows,
they are not always sufficient for I-HITL. I-HITL can be
viewed as a generalization of transaction authentication to
agent systems, where transactions can be of many different
types and where adversarial delegation poses a concern.
Agent authorization:A number of works on AI agents
(e.g., [74]) and standards specifications (e.g., MCP [58])
consider ways of using existing authorization infrastructure,
such as OAuth, to authorize agents. These rely on existing
user authentication, and where they call for human approval
of an action, they leave unspecified how that approval
reaches the service. When the human reaches the identity
provider or approval prompt through the agent’s platform,
these designs fall in the plain topology of Theorem 4.
Approved vs. executed actions:Classical work on au-
thorization from untrusted terminals [1], [2], [10] shows
the danger of signed approvals in the face of a deceptive
rendering layer (a problem sometimes calledwhat you see
is what you sign(WYSIWYS)). Weng [83] adapts WYSI-
WYS to agent approvals, but simply assumes a trusted
path. Transaction-authentication devices [82] and trusted-
path systems [89] address the issues in these works by
giving the human a channel that bypasses a potentially
compromised host. Our trusted entityTplays a similar
role, but we characterize exactly which channelsTneeds
and, unlike classical schemes, consideradversarialhuman
delegation. Relatedly, Loopjacking [43] studies the failure
in which an implementation applies a genuine approval of
one operation to a different one. Approval binding rules this
out at the gate, since a proof verifies only for the message
the human approved. We assume the service then executes
that message faithfully [90].
Proof of Personhood (PoP):Many proposed [19] and
deployed systems aim to enable online services to distin-
guish humans and bots. The best known, World, issues
a credential to a human whose uniqueness is established
biometrically or using a national identity document. World’s
human-in-the-loop package [77] gates agent actions on a
World ID proof, but does not realize I-HITL. The biggest
gap is that the human never sees the action on a trusted
display: the action is rendered only by the agent’s platform,
and the World app displays a description written by the
agent itself, which is not bound to the proof. An unreleased
revision of the package replaces World ID proofs with a
standard World ID login, which also never shows the human
the action. More broadly, World does not aim to prevent
delegation: AgentKit [84] links agent wallets to verified
humans. Additionally, because World aims todeduplicate
humans, it requires use of a single global registry, limiting
its scope of application. 2
Other notions of PoP, e.g., [46], give assurance of human
approval only to the user herself, not a relying service.
Secure human–server communication:Building on se-
curity ceremonies [32], [70], Basin et al. [12] character-
ize the topologies permitting authentic or confidential hu-
man–server communication through a compromised plat-
form. We adopt their topology framework and impossibility
argument, but differ in key ways: they fix all non-platform
channels as secure and let the device share keys with the
server a priori, whereas we grade each trusted-entity channel
independently and assume no initial knowledge, making
key establishment (and hence attestation and encumbrance)
2. As of October 2026, users from only 17 countries are eligible for
identity-document-based authentication.
13
central to our characterization (Theorem 7). Our goal also
differs from theirs, in that we bind a human’s approval to
an adversary-chosen message through a publicly verifiable
proof, including (for strong I-HITL) against a human who
attempts to delegate approval to the agent.
9. Conclusion
HITL gates are widely deployed as a critical line of
defense against misbehaving agents, yet most operate in
a topology that does not separate the agent from the re-
sources used to approve its actions. Our experiments with a
mainstream agentic wallet show the practical consequences.
Given access to the inbox that receives a gate’s OTPs and
a task blocked by a spending cap, most frontier models
will autonomously bypass the gate. Some will do so on an
attacker’s instruction and send the attacker money.
We introduced I-HITL to formalize what a secure HITL
gate must guarantee, and strong I-HITL to extend that guar-
antee to humans who attempt to delegate approvals to agents
in violation of oversight policies. In a symbolic model of
channel security we proved that a trusted entity is necessary
for I-HITL (Theorem 4) but not sufficient (Theorem 7):
this entity must attest to the keys it generates and, for
strong I-HITL, encumber them. We classified existing de-
vices against these requirements and presented Countersign,
which realizes strong I-HITL on off-the-shelf Android and
iOS devices.
Takeaways for deployers and platform vendors.The
default topology of many agentic systems, in which the gate
co-resides with the agent, fails in principle (Theorem 4)
and in practice (Section 3). Guidance that restricts agent
capabilities (e.g., denying email access) shifts the burden to
users and is unlikely to hold; we urge service vendors to
adopt the stronger assurances I-HITL makes achievable. We
likewise urge authenticator-app vendors to offer an I-HITL
approval mode, since push authenticators already implement
most of the required mechanisms (Section 7.2). We finally
urge mobile OS vendors to supply two missing primitives: a
TEE-rendered confirmation UI and attestable gating of key
use on biometric authentication.
Takeaway for regulators.Regulations such as the EU AI
Act [33] mandate human oversight without specifying how
to define or evidence it. Strong I-HITL offers a concep-
tual springboard for regulatory frameworks and, in systems
such as Countersign, a mechanism for producing verifiable
evidence of compliance in the form of human approval
(although not evidence of due attention).
Open problems.On the analytic side, a computational
treatment would give finer-grained guarantees than our sym-
bolic one. On the practical side, the key question is how
to extend I-HITL beyond end-user platforms to enterprise
systems and, critically, to the infrastructure on which frontier
models themselves run, where an agent may be positioned
to gate its own oversight (Example 2). By Theorem 4, such
work would begin by establishing a local serviceSisolated
from the agent, e.g., in a hardware enclave, with a trusted
entityTlikewise outside the agent’s reach.

Appendix D.
Disclosure to Circle
We disclosed the self-escalation results of Section 3
to Circle on 22 September 2026. Circle acknowledged the
report, and its agent-wallet documentation now carries the
warning shown in Figure 10 [24]. The notice advises users
never to grant an agent unrestricted access to the email
account to which the OTP is sent, explains that anyone able
to read that inbox can log in to the wallet and change its
spending policies, and recommends that the user enter the
OTP themselves.
We consider this a sensible mitigation, but it places the
burden on the user as it relies on users keeping the inbox
out of the agent’s reach, rather than on the service enforcing
that separation. The policy-update flow itself remains in
the plain topology of Section 5.1, so an agent that reaches
the inbox by any other route (e.g., stored credentials or
an authenticated browser session) can still open the gate.
We therefore recommend that policy updates additionally
be gated by one of the I-HITL constructions of Section 5.

World’s own human-in-the-loop package [77] illustrates
a further gap between the World app/protocol and its in-
tegrations: the published version passes a summary written
by the agent as the action description and does not check
that the returned proof is for the requested action, while
an unreleased revision replaces the proof with a World ID

Push-approval 2FA apprefers to apps such as Duo Mo-
bile, Microsoft Authenticator and Okta Verify. The latter
two gate only sign-ins to their own identity platforms, so
we do not consider them further. Duo’s Auth API lets any
service push a request for an arbitrary action [25], and is
the instantiation we consider as representative. At activation
the app generates a key pair on the phone, typically in
the platform keystore, and sends the public key to Duo.
At approval the service requests an approval for an action
through Duo, the app shows the action on the screen, and the
user approves with a tap or, if the service’s policy requires it,
with a biometric. The app then signs a Duo-chosen identifier
of the request, and Duo reports the outcome to the service
as an allow or deny status. Since the app is closed source,
we cannot see whether what it signs is bound to what it
displays, or whether the signature is gated on the biometric
input. However, we give it the benefit of the doubt and
assume both. Duo also accepts passcodes generated in the
app, SMS passcodes and phone calls as fallbacks [25], which
realize neither form of I-HITL (below), so we consider
the configuration in which the service requires a biometric
push and disables the fallbacks, as it offers the strongest
guarantees.
Under these assumptions, the app display is trusted but
exposed, as for Countersign, and the input is a trusted
confirmation, since the biometric sensor cannot be driven
by software on the platform. The key is encumbered but
unattested: it is generated in the keystore and recovery
is by re-enrollment rather than export, but enrollment is
authorized by an activation code shown on the platform,
so an agent that reads it can enroll a key of its own, as
evidenced by the existence of third-party clients [34]. On
Android, Duo uses Play Integrity device attestations to flag
tampered devices, and recent versions of the app appear to
send a key attestation chain at activation, but Duo documents

the service is left with an allow status rather than a proof it
can present to a third party.
TOTP/HOTP authenticator apprefers to apps such as
Google Authenticator that generate six-digit codes from a
secret shared with the service, derived from the current time
(TOTP [60]) or a counter (HOTP [59]). At enrollment the
service generates the secret seed and makes it available to
the user via a QR code. At approval the user reads the
current code from the app and types it into the platform,
which forwards it to the service. The display is untrusted
and exposed, since the app never shows the message being
approved, only a code that is valid for any message in
its time window, and the phone screen can be read by
accessibility services; the input is untrusted, sinceThas
no input interface in the approval flow at all; and the key
is neither attested nor encumbered, since the secret itself is
provisioned in the QR code shown on the platform, so the
agent can read it at enrollment and generate codes without
the authenticator app. Every label isins, so the app realizes
neither form of I-HITL.
SMS or email OTPis the mechanism used in Example 1
and in many password-reset and account-recovery flows,
where the service generates a code and sends it to the
user’s phone number or inbox, and the user types it into
the platform. The display is untrusted and exposed, since
sender identifiers in SMS and email are easily spoofed, so
the human cannot tell a code from the service from one
the agent injected, and an agent with access to the inbox or
messaging app can read it; the input is untrusted, since the
code is typed into the platform; and there is no key, since
the code is generated by the service and shared over the
channel, so there is nothing to attest or encumber. Every
label isins, so the mechanism realizes neither form of I-
HITL, as Section 3 demonstrates empirically.
Appendix F.
Implementation Details
Our service backend is implemented in Python using
FastAPI with SQLite as a data store. The Android app
creates a signing key in the Keystore, attempting by default
to use StrongBox before falling back to TEE, and enforces
aBIOMETRIC_STRONGrequirement on its use, obtaining
signatures through theBiometricPrompt. The iPhone
app uses theDeviceCheckinterface for App Attest and
theLocalAuthenticationframework to create the
signing key with a biometric requirement. On a user ap-
proval, the signing key signs the exact bytes displayed in the
app as a UTF-8 string. Our code includes instructions for
deploying and testing both apps. Figures 12 and 13 depict
screenshots of the Countersign workflow on Android and
iOS respectively.

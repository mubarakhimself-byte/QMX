Simple Portfolios that Deliver Consistent Decent Returns
Oskar Edén Wallberg1,2 Alexander Karolin1,2
February 5, 2026
Abstract
Portfolio construction has been studied extensively in both academia and industry. Its
applications range from pension funds and hedge funds to private savings. The aca-
demic literature centers on methodology, commonly evaluated in settings that allow
short selling, leverage, and frequent trading. Industry, on the other hand, operates un-
der real constraints, but its data and methods are proprietary and largely unpublished.
Neither is directly available to the private investor. This paper is an empirical study
of established portfolio construction methods in the setting of the private investor. We
consider a small investable universe of liquid US ETFs, where trading is monthly and
carries a cost, and neither shorting nor leverage is permitted. Portfolio construction
is posed as a convex optimization problem, its inputs are estimated from public data,
and every parameter is interpretable. We denote such portfolios simple. We show
that simple portfolios of as few as three ETFs deliver consistent and decent returns,
without tuning to the backtest period. To find them, we have developed a framework,
which we share publicly, able to backtest hundreds of portfolios on a standard laptop
over a twenty year period or longer. When no return forecast is made, portfolios are
constructed from risk alone, using methods such as risk parity and minimum volatil-
ity, built on a statistical factor model fitted with expectation maximization. When a
forecast is made, we combine the Markowitz formulation with momentum signals and
ridge regression. We report what works and what each component adds, weighing its
gain against its complexity and the effort it demands of the investor. The findings
are supported by numerical experiments over the past two decades, and the proposed
portfolios outperform the standard 60/40 allocation.
Keywords:Portfolio construction, exchange-traded funds, convex optimization, factor risk
models, return forecasting, risk-based asset allocation, backtesting, retail investing

1 Introduction
Portfolio construction was first formalized by Harry Markowitz in 1952 [Mar52]. The main
idea behind portfolio construction is to balance risk and reward by diversifying investments.
Markowitz was able to define diversification in mathematical terms, however the concept has
been known for much longer. As far back as the third century, the Talmud advised splitting
wealth three ways, one in land, one in business, and one held in reserve [Mar99]. The core
concepts still apply today, and investing has grown significantly in popularity. Professional
investors such as Warren Buffett and Peter Lynch diversify across tens or hundreds of in-
vestments, while quantitative hedge funds such as Citadel hold positions in thousands of
different financial instruments. For the private investor, tracking such quantities of assets
requires time and effort beyond what is reasonable. In this paper, we present some modern
portfolio construction techniques applied to portfolios with a more manageable number of
assets, making them practical for private investors to replicate and maintain.
1.1 Investing and diversification
A common problem all investors face is how to allocate their capital in a way that balances
risk and return. There are many options to choose from in financial markets, including
securities such as stocks, bonds, and mutual funds. A more recent addition, the exchange-
traded fund (ETF), has become an increasingly popular alternative. An ETF is a fund
traded like a stock, meaning that it can be bought and sold throughout the trading day,
unlike a mutual fund. An ETF holds a basket of underlying assets such as stocks, bonds, or
commodities, and typically tracks an index. The most traded ETF has the ticker (identifier)
SPY [Stab], and tracks the Standard and Poor’s 500 (S&P 500). The S&P 500 is a market
index that tracks the performance of the 500 largest publicly traded companies in the US.
A long-standing fact is that it is difficult to consistently outperform a broad market
index such as the S&P 500, even for professional managers [Mal03, SP 25]. Warren Buffett
famously bet a financial institute $1 million that no funds they selected could outperform the
S&P 500, net of fees, over a 10-year period (2008–2017) [Buf18]. Buffett won. A natural first
investment is therefore to simply hold the S&P 500, e.g., via SPY. Figure 1 shows the growth
of SPY over the past two decades, with an initial investment growing roughly sevenfold.

We refer to the magnitude of these variations as the volatility, or the "riskiness" of investing

pandemic in 2020, and the start of the Russia-Ukraine war in 2022. The largest drawdown
was during the Financial Crisis when SPY fell by55%from its peak, wiping out an estimated
$7trillion of market cap in the S&P 500 [Bru09].
One simple method for mitigating volatility and large drawdowns is to diversify invest-
ments into multiple assets that do not experience losses and gains at the same time. The
most famous and very simple example of diversification is the 60/40 portfolio [DMS24],
which can be replicated with ETFs by combining SPY with AGG into a 60:40 ratio. AGG
is an ETF that tracks an index of US aggregate bonds. Bonds have historically had a low
or slightly negative correlation with equities such as SPY. Figure 3 shows the cumulative

Looking at the figure, SPY ends with the largest total return but is also the most volatile
of the three. AGG, on the other hand, grows much more slowly with an average annual
return of3%, but is much more stable with a volatility of5%. The 60/40 portfolio falls

keep some of SPY’s long-run growth while almost halving the volatility and taking a much
smaller hit during bad years. This is the value of diversification, by combining assets whose
returns are mostly independent you end up with a portfolio that gives you a better balance
between risk and return than either asset on its own. The trade-off between return and risk
is often expressed as a ratio, formally known as the Sharpe ratio [Sha66]. The Sharpe ratio
expresses the efficiency of the portfolio and can be interpreted as return per unit of risk
taken. Between 2005 and 2025 the Sharpe ratios for AGG, SPY, and 60/40 were 0.23, 0.45,
and 0.51, respectively.
1.2 Quantitative trading
So far we have only looked at risk and return with one famous example of a portfolio.
In practice, investing involves many more considerations. Which assets should be held, in
what proportions, and when should they be bought or sold? Most private investors have no
systematic framework to answer these questions, constructing their investment philosophy
instead from experience and word of mouth [MN11, HKS04, KK12]. Decisions made this
way have historically underperformed the market [DAL24, BO00, FL08]. An alternative is to
make decisions systematically. One example of this is quantitative trading, where every rule
is mathematically defined and decisions are computed from historical data. The investor’s
job now reduces to choosing a trading strategy and following it.
To make these decisions systematically, a mathematical framework can be used. One
widely used framework is the Markowitz formulation [Mar52] expressed as a convex problem.
The original Markowitz formulation models the trade-off between return and risk, and adding
cost gives us a complete model for quantitative trading. A simplification of this model can
be expressed as a general convex problem,
maximize Return−Risk−Cost
subject to Trading limits,
where the return is maximized while risk and cost are minimized, subject to limits on the
trades. Each of the three terms can be modeled mathematically. The risk is captured by a
risk model, the return by a forecast, and the cost by a cost model. Forecasted returns are
commonly known as alpha, hence the forecast is described as an alpha model. Combining
the three gives a complete portfolio construction model, as in Figure 4.

where each diagonal entryσ 2i is the variance of assetiand each off-diagonal entryσ ij is

Alpha.The risk model describes how asset returns move together, but says nothing about
where they are headed. That forecast is the job of the alpha model. Within the financial
literature alpha is the expected asset returns over the next period,
αt =E[r t+1].
Within the context of Markowitz, alpha does not have to be the exact future return of the
assets. Rather it can be simplified and estimation errors can be reduced using robustification
techniques. Some simplifications include reducing alpha to a yes or no question, whether the
return of asset A will be positive or negative tomorrow. Another simplification is forecasting
only the relative order of the investable assets. Simple forecasting methods use previous
returns of each asset to model future returns. However, in industry alpha is more diffuse,
and can be interpreted as some information about how the prices of assets will move in the
future. Examples of data that have been used to achieve alpha are satellite imagery, weather
forecasts, or social media sentiment. For the curious reader, [Nar20] gives a more thorough
breakdown, best enjoyed while taking lunch.
Cost model.Trading is not free and each trade incurs two main costs. The first is the
broker fee, or commission, charged to execute the trade. The second is the bid-ask spread.
At any moment an asset has two quoted prices, the bid, the highest price a buyer will pay,
and the ask, the lowest price a seller will accept. The spread is the gap between them, and
it is a cost to both sides. Suppose you want to sell asset A. The ask price is $101 and the
bid price is $99. The effective price sits in the middle at $100, so selling at the $99 bid costs
you $1 against that midpoint, half the $2 spread. Spreads are usually quoted in basis points,
where one hundred basis points equal 1% of the price. A $2 spread on a $100 asset is a
200 basis point spread, and the half-spread paid by each side is 100 basis points. Figure 6
illustrates this with the common example of SPY.
Figure 6:Each side of a trade pays the half-spread against the midpoint.
The figure shows the price of SPY, with the bid and ask quoted on either side as dashed
lines. The true price lies in the middle, and the distance from the midpoint to either quote is
the half-spread, what a buyer or seller actually pays. Cost models can be made considerably
more detailed, accounting for fees, currency conversion, and the price impact of large orders,
but they are often simplified to a fixed percentage charged on the value of every trade.

1.3 Previous work
This paper is an empirical study of portfolio construction applied to a universe of ETFs.
ApplyingsystematicportfoliomethodstoETFsisitselfanactiveareaofresearch, withrecent
work studying multi-asset ETF portfolios built from prediction and optimization [LLD25],
momentum and trend-following across asset classes [Lu+25], and the effect of rebalancing
on ETF-only portfolios [Bán+24]. Our contribution is not a new method but a transparent
application of well-established techniques to a simple, retail-accessible universe, with an
emphasis on what each component contributes. Further work on the topic can be found
in [devanathan:2026], which was developed in parallel within the same group at Stanford.
Portfolioconstruction.TheoriginalMarkowitzformulationhasbeenstudiedthoroughly,
and many variations exist. A few examples include the Black-Litterman model [BL92],
robust mean-variance optimization [GI03], and the modern Markowitz at seventy formu-
lation [Boy+24]. These variations were, among other reasons, developed to address the
estimation sensitivity that return forecasting introduces in the original Markowitz formula-
tion. There are however multiple methods that do not require an estimated future return.
Common methods include minimum variance [CST06], inverse volatility [Lee11], risk par-
ity [Qia05], and maximum diversification [CC08]. These methods instead construct portfolios
based on asset risk.
Risk model.Many portfolio construction methods require a risk model which is a covari-
ance matrix of asset returns estimated from a rolling window of historical data. Estimating
this matrix well is difficult, and a large part of current financial research goes into producing
robust estimates [Tzi+26, Joh25, PHB25, Ced25]. Using a naive sample covariance intro-
duces multiple problems. One of the problems being that when the number of assets is large
relative to the number of observations the estimate becomes unreliable. Another problem is
that using large covariance matrices is computationally inefficient. One way to handle this
problem is to use a factor model. Factor models have a long history. The concept of factor
analysis was first introduced by Spearman in 1904 [Spe04]. It was later adopted in finance
where asset returns are modeled as being driven by a few common factors plus idiosyncratic
noise. While such models date back to the single-factor CAPM [Sha64], the framework was
popularized by Fama and French, who introduced empirically tested models with multiple
factors [FF92, FF93]. Factor models are widely used today in industry [MSC24, Axi24]
and academia [FFL08, FLM13, LW03] and results in interpretable, faster and more reliable
covariance estimate. Most factor models use economically interpretable factors, such as the
market. An alternative is to extract factors statistically from the returns themselves, for
instance through principal component analysis or related latent-factor methods [CK86].
Alpha.The return forecast, or alpha, is a central part of the Markowitz formulation, and
receives a great deal of attention in both academia and industry [KNS20, KPS19, BHT25,
Lóp20]. Return prediction is difficult. The signal-to-noise ratio in financial data is extremely
low, far lower than in most other domains where statistical learning is applied [Lóp18]. It is
easy to overfit to noise rather than signal, and therefore it limits the complexity of the models
that can be used. A common response to the low signal-to-noise ratio is regularization,
6
which constrains the model to reduce overfitting. Examples include ridge regression [HK70],
Laplacian-regularized models [TBB21], and the broader use of shrinkage and sign constraints.
Whetheralphapredictionworksisdebatedinacademia. GoyalandWelch[GW08]foundthat
a long list of popular predictors fail to beat the historical mean out of sample, while Campbell
and Thompson [CT08] showed that the same predictors do beat it once weak restrictions are
imposed. Within industry, alpha is achieved by having an edge against the market. Data and
methods used to produce alpha are highly confidential and employees working with alpha
prediction often have to sign long non-disclosure and non-compete agreements [Zuc19].
Software.Software for solving optimization problems is almost as old as the methods
themselves. The first nontrivial linear program ever solved took nine people around four
months on hand calculators in the 1940s [Bix12], a calculation that solvers now finish in-
stantly.1 Today the field of convex optimization has matured and been adopted in many
software libraries such as CVXPY for Python [DB16], Convex.jl for Julia [Ude+14], and
CVXR for R [FNB20]. These libraries have produced a wave of accessible portfolio opti-
mization tools. A few examples are Portfolio Visualizer [Sil23], PyPortfolioOpt [Mar21],
Riskfolio-Lib [Caj24], and CVXPortfolio [BDB+24]. Their coverage varies widely, from
simple web-based backtesting and visualization to specific methods such as mean-variance
optimization, risk parity [Lóp16], and multi-period construction. Commercial alternatives
also exist and are widely used in industry [Axi24, MSC24, Bla24].
1.4 This paper
This paper studies portfolios that are simple, and yields consistent, and decent returns.
None of the methods we use are new. Techniques are gathered from across the portfolio
construction literature and industry practitioners and we apply them to one universe under
one set of rules, and report what works. The rules are the same for every portfolio. We use
no leverage and no shorting, target an annual volatility of8%, and earn the federal funds rate
on cash. Strategies trade monthly with a5basis points bid-ask spread and are backtested
from 2005-01-01 to 2025-01-01. We do not tune parameters to this period. The results are
presented for research and educational purposes and do not constitute investment advice.
By simple we mean three things. First, the strategies are implementable. They use only
public data and a small set of well-known, liquid US ETFs. Second, the math is explainable.
We use no deep learning and no construction methods that are hard to interpret. We take a
clear set of ETFs, divide them into asset classes, and run well-understood methods on each.
Third, the parameters are interpretable. We use quantities a reader can reason about, such
as a hard target-volatility constraint and turnover limits stated as percentages. If you want
a higher volatility target, you change one number.
By consistent we mean a portfolio should do well across the whole period, not just in
a few good years. We measure this with the Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, and, as a
stricter test, with the lower quantile of the twenty recorded annual Sharpe ratios. By decent
we mean annual returns in the range of 6% to 10%.

We study two families of portfolios. In the first family, we make no return forecast at all.
The allocation methods are fixed weights, inverse volatility, minimum volatility, risk parity,
and maximum diversification, either combined across different asset classes or applied to
just a few select assets. In the second family, each asset produces its own alpha signal. We
combine all of these signals and solve a single Markowitz problem for each trading day. In
the final section we present reasonable portfolios for the different methods we have explored.
We benchmark against the reference60/40portfolio. A backtest over two decades can
look good simply because of how a portfolio handled one or two extreme events, so we also
check how sensitive our proposed portfolios are to the choice of window. Each portfolio is
rerun over two truncated periods, one with the first five years removed, which excludes the
Financial Crisis, and one with the last five removed, which excludes the COVID-19 pandemic
and the start of the Russia-Ukraine war. If a portfolio performs well in all three windows
we can consider it to be less dependent on a specific event.
Software.Along with this paper we release three resources. The first is a web-based
tool that lets readers explore the historical performance of some of the simple portfolios we
construct, with interactive controls for the relevant parameters. The second is our codebase,
made open source so that the strategies described can be replicated, extended, or used as a
starting point for new portfolio designs. Both are available at
https://github.com/AlexEmrik/consistent-decent-returns.
The third is an open source package which lets anyone create a factor risk model through a

What we have found.Simple strategies work surprisingly well, often performing as well
as or better than more complicated approaches. Some quantitative methods for investing
stand out as particularly useful. One example is volatility control, which lets the investor
target a desired level of volatility. Among the simple strategies, fixed weights stand out as
both effective and extremely simple given an educated selection of ETFs. Given that we
are limited to open data, risk forecasting works surprisingly well while return prediction
proves to be futile. However, with simple alpha signals and some robustification, Markowitz
portfolio optimization is still able to deliver consistent decent returns.

Portfolio construction is the problem of deciding how to allocate capital across a set of assets.
This section defines the universe of ETFs we invest in, organized into categories by asset class
and underlying structure. From this universe the simple strategies of §3 later select portfolios
of three to five ETFs, while the Markowitz formulation is given whole asset categories.

Survivorship bias.Our universe was selected from ETFs trading today, and none of the
59 were delisted during the 2005–2025 backtest window; it is therefore survivorship-biased by
construction. Roughly one third of all ETFs ever launched in the US have been liquidated,
with 189 closures in 2024 alone and approximately 1,550 over the preceding decade [Kip25,
Mor25]. However, closures concentrate disproportionately in segments excluded from our
universe. The typical liquidated ETF has fewer than $50M in assets under management,
a sub-five-year track record, and underperformed broad benchmarks by several percentage
pointsinthetrailingyearpriortoliquidation[Mor24]. Leveragedandinverseproducts, which
we exclude entirely, account for a closure rate of 52% versus 31% for regular ETFs [Cha24].
We therefore expect the resulting upward bias on reported Sharpe ratios to be modest,

A bond is a contract between the investor and an issuing institution, typically a government

which holds 20+ year Treasuries, lost close to half of its value as the FFR rose.

3 Portfolio construction
Portfolio construction concerns the problem of deciding how to allocate capital across a set
of assets. This section begins by introducing the notation and terminology for portfolio
weights, cash, returns, and risk, objects fundamental to portfolio construction regardless
of the trading strategy. Our notation is similar to that of [Boy+24]. We then present a
set of common trading strategies in increasing order of complexity: fixed weights, simple
optimization, and our version of the Markowitz formulation.

3.3 Markowitz
The simple strategies of §3.2 all assume no knowledge of future returns. Suppose instead you
have a belief about how your assets will perform, e.g. that your equities will outperform your
fixed income assets, or even an exact figure for each asset produced by a forecast. A natural
extension of a method like minimum volatility is then to not only minimize risk but also max-
imize expected return. This exact trade-off was formalized by Markowitz [Mar52] in 1952.
The work was recognized with the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences [The90],
and still underpins portfolio allocation today, both in academia [KTF14, Kim+21, Boy+24]
and in industry [MSC24, Axi24, Bla24]. At its core the idea is simple: collect the expected
returns in a vectorα, and maximize the return it implies for your portfolio while weighing
that against the portfolio risk. The problem can be expressed in several ways. We present
two standard formulations, followed by the modified version we use throughout this paper.
Formulated with a soft risk constraint.One way to express the trade-off is to sub-
tract the portfolio risk from the expected return in the objective, scaled by a risk-aversion
parameterγ >0,

penalty disappears and the solver puts everything into the single asset with the highest
expected return, and asγgrows the penalty dominates until the portfolio only holds cash.

4 Risk model
Portfolio construction requires an estimate of future risk. While future market conditions
cannot be observed directly, historical returns contain information about the volatility of
individual assets and how they tend to move together, and a risk model uses this infor-
mation to estimate the covariance structure that will prevail over the period for which the
portfolio is constructed. This section explains what a risk model is and how the covariance
is forecast from historical returns. We present two simple estimators, the rolling-window
sample covariance and the exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA), followed by a
more sophisticated approach based on a statistical factor model.
4.1 Risk forecasting
Risk forecasting is concerned with estimating the covariance matrixΣ, which describes both
the variability of individual assets and their co-movement. Following the original formulation
of [Mar52], diversification arises from the covariance structure between assets, making an
accurate estimate ofΣcentral to nearly every portfolio construction method. A useful way
for the reader to think about the covariance matrix is simply as
Σ−encodes future “riskiness” of assets.
The rolling-window and EWMA estimators are procedural approaches to covariance estima-
tion. Both construct a risk forecast by aggregating observed historical returns according to
a predetermined rule, require few assumptions, and are straightforward to implement.
The statistical factor model is particularly useful for large investment universes, where
estimating every pairwise relationship directly becomes increasingly difficult. It introduces
several important mathematical concepts, including statistical factors, regularization, and
expectation maximization. These methods require a somewhat heavier mathematical treat-
ment, and are deferred to Appendix A, where the reader can find details about the deriva-
tions. To keep the main discussion intuitive and readable, the focus here is on the structure
of the factor model, why it is useful, and how its resulting covariance estimate should be
interpreted.

The choice of the lookback windowLreflects a trade-off between estimation accuracy
and responsiveness to changing market conditions. A short lookback adapts quickly to
changes in volatility and correlation, but produces a noisier covariance estimate because it
is based on fewer observations. Conversely, a longer lookback reduces estimation noise by
averaging over more observations, but may incorporate outdated return relationships that
are no longer relevant for forecasting future risk. The faster market conditions change, the
shorterLshould generally be to avoid placing excessive weight on stale data. Consequently,
there is no universally optimal choice ofL, and its value depends on both the forecasting
horizonhand the persistence of the underlying covariance structure. Another limitation of
the rolling-window estimator is that every observation within the lookback window receives
exactly the same weight, so a return observed yesterday influences the estimate just as much
as a return observedLdays ago, until it abruptly leaves the window altogether.
The choice ofLis also constrained by the number of assetsn. WhenL≤n, the
estimated covariance matrix is guaranteed to be rank deficient, implying the existence of
zero-risk portfolios that do not exist in the true market. Even whenLis only slightly larger
thann, the covariance matrix may remain poorly conditioned and numerically unstable , so
in practiceL≫nis generally preferred. For large investment universes, of sayn= 1000
assets, this would require multi-year lookback windows, which both increases computational
cost and incorporates progressively more outdated market information.

The observation weights are parameterized through a half-lifeH, measured in trading

to the exponential decayλτ = 0.5 τ /H, whereτdenotes the age of the observation in days.

influence. Smaller values ofHplace greater emphasis on recent observations, whereas larger
values retain information from a longer history.
Unlike the rolling-window estimator, EWMA never discards observations abruptly. In-
stead, every historical return continues to contribute to the covariance estimate, with its
influence gradually fading as it becomes older. Since the estimator uses the entire available
return history, numerical stability is determined primarily by the total number of available
observations rather than by a fixed lookback window. In practice, provided that sufficiently
many observations are available, typicallyt−1≫n, the covariance estimate remains full
rank while avoiding the abrupt discontinuities introduced by a rolling window. Reported
half-life values range from a few days to several hundred days depending on the intended
application [LS96, PPK04, Bol15]. Another practical advantage of EWMA is that the co-

Thecovarianceestimatorsintroducedaboveestimatehoweverypairofassetsmovestogether.
This works well when the number of assets is small relative to the amount of historical data
available. As the investment universe grows, however, the number of relationships that
must be estimated grows quickly. Fornassets, the covariance matrix containsn(n−1)/2
distinct pairwise covariances. With 1,000 assets, this already amounts to almost half a
million relationships, all estimated from the same finite history of returns. Many of these
estimates will inevitably reflect noise rather than persistent relationships in the market.
23
Factor models address this problem by imposing structure on how assets are allowed to
move together. Rather than estimating every relationship independently, a factor model
assumes that much of the co-movement between assets can be explained by a small number
of common sources of variation, called factors. For example, two equity ETFs may move
together not because their relationship is unique, but because both respond to the same
broad movement in the equity market. If many assets share such common movements, it
can be more reliable to estimate these underlying factors than every pairwise relationship
separately.
There are two common ways to define these factors [Ced25]. Economic factor models
specify them explicitly, using interpretable quantities such as the market, size, and value
factors of Fama and French [FF92, FF93]. Statistical factor models instead infer the common
movements directly from observed asset returns. We use the latter approach, so the factors
need not have an economic interpretation. However, the first factor tends to capture the
market movement. The factor model we use follows the form of [Ced25] and is given by,

5 Alpha model
Markowitz portfolio optimization requires, outside of the risk estimate, also some estimate
of the future attractiveness of investments. An alpha model attempts to summarize the
information contained in historical data into such a forecast. This section explains what an
alpha model is and how a return forecast is constructed from historical prices. We present
two simple signals, momentum and a moving average, followed by a fitted model that uses
ridge regression.
5.1 Return forecasting

Momentum and the moving-average signal are procedural alphas constructed directly from

to. Institutional investors may combine sophisticated statistical models with proprietary
datasets, alternative information, or other signals unavailable to the average market partic-
ipant. Our objective is different. We restrict the forecasting problem to public, historically
observable market data so that the resulting models remain reproducible and accessible. The
regression models introduced later should therefore only be viewed as increasingly flexible
methods for extracting whatever predictive information is present in the available features.

5.2 Simple alphas
There are countless ways to construct an alpha signal from historical market data. Prices
and returns can be aggregated and transformed in many ways, producing a jungle of possible
signals. The purpose of this section is not to exhaustively search for the best-performing
alpha. Instead, we consider two simple and interpretable textbook alphas to investigate
whether useful information can be extracted from recent prices: momentum, which retains
the magnitude of recent returns, and a moving-average signal, which reduces the forecast to
the direction of the recent price trend.

period is carried forward directly as the forecast. An asset that has recently appreciated
receives a positive alpha, while an asset that has declined receives a negative one. The
minimal underlying assumption is that price movements exhibit some persistence, such that
assets performing well relative to their recent history may continue to do so over the near
future. We use one-month momentum, computed from the log return over the precedingL

Low signal-to-noise also changes how model complexity should be viewed. A flexible model
has more freedom to discover relationships in the training data, but when most historical
variation is noise, it also has more freedom to explain patterns that occurred by chance. A
good in-sample fit can therefore be obtained without learning a relationship that persists
out of sample. This is the familiar bias–variance trade-off, but it becomes particularly
important for return forecasting. Reducing model bias by adding flexibility can quickly
increase estimation variance as the model adapts to noise. Model complexity is therefore not
automatically an advantage. The objective is instead to use enough flexibility to capture the
weak systematic component of returns while restricting the model sufficiently that random
historical variation is not mistaken for persistent structure.

6.2 Simple strategies
The simple strategies of §3.2 each need a smaller portfolio to run on. This section examines
the candidate portfolios for each strategy. The strategies are ordered by complexity, and
each addresses a specific shortcoming of the strategy before it. For each strategy we report
what it addresses, our findings on portfolio composition, and which assets are reasonable to
include.
Fixed weights.We begin with equal weights. We backtest every combination of three,
four, and five ETFs, rebalancing monthly, and rank the resulting portfolios twice: first by
SR alone and then by SR followed by LQ. We exclude two groups of assets from the search.
First, the Country and Futures categories, since they are not suitable buy-and-hold assets, as
established in §2.2, and the fixed-weight strategy can do little beyond buying, holding, and
rebalancing. Second, we exclude any ETF that was not listed at the start of the backtest.
An investor in 2005 could not have selected an asset that did not yet exist, and introducing
new ETFs as they became available would change the portfolio composition during the
experiment. These restrictions leave 23 ETFs. Rather than testing every possible allocation
for every combination, we initially restrict the search to equal weights. This separates
two questions that would otherwise be mixed together: which assets should a fixed-weight
portfolio contain, and how should capital be divided among them? Here we focus on the
first question and return to the choice of proportions afterwards. Table 27 in the appendix
reports the top 60 portfolios under both ranking criteria for each portfolio size, while Table 4
presents the best portfolios under each criterion.

33
capture. LQ is based on the lower quartile of annual Sharpe ratios and therefore rewards
portfolios that perform consistently across years, while a single extreme loss has limited
influence on the measure. MDD behaves in the opposite way: it is determined entirely by
the most severe peak-to-trough loss. Since the backtest contains three major market crashes,
the two measures can therefore favor different portfolio compositions. Government bonds
such as IEF and TLT provide protection during severe equity downturns and improve MDD,
whereas replacing some of this protection with additional equity exposure, such as XLV
or XLU, can improve performance across a larger share of individual years and therefore
increase LQ.
Common characteristics.Despite these differences, the strongest portfolios share a clear
structure and generally draw from all three asset classes. Gold appears particularly consis-
tently: GLD, the only Alternatives ETF with sufficient history to enter the search, is present
in every one of the top fifteen portfolios at each portfolio size and in most of the remain-
ing top 60. Fixed-income allocations are dominated by government bonds. Although the
corporate bond ETF LQD occasionally appears, government bond alternatives rank higher
for the same equity-and-gold combinations. The equity allocation, meanwhile, is typically
concentrated in growth-oriented assets, with larger portfolios often adding either a defensive
equity sector or a second government bond position. Adding more ETFs therefore provides
only limited improvement. Moving from three to four or five assets increases the best Sharpe
ratio only slightly, from0.81for three ETFs to0.84for both four and five ETFs, and the
additional holdings are usually variations of exposures already present in the portfolio. The
main result is consequently not a particular winning combination of ETFs, but a recurring
allocation across distinct sources of risk: growth-oriented equities provide return, govern-
ment bonds provide protection during equity downturns, and gold adds a third source of
diversification.

must still decide which assets to hold and how to allocate between them. The next strategy
takes a step toward solving this problem by sizing each position according to its individual
risk.
Inverse volatility.Inverse volatility replaces the static allocation of fixed weights with
an allocation that adapts to changes in risk. Under (3), each asset is weighted inversely to
its estimated volatility, giving larger positions to less volatile assets and smaller positions to
more volatile ones. These weights are recomputed at every trade, allowing the portfolio to
adjust as market conditions change.

simply favors the asset with the lowest individual volatility.
To see whether this pattern holds more generally, we repeat the exhaustive search using
inverse-volatility weights. The top 60 portfolios for each portfolio size are reported in Ta-
ble 29. The same broad portfolio structure found under equal weights remains: the strongest
portfolios combine growth-oriented equities, defensive equities, government bonds, and gold.
However, the best Sharpe ratios increase from0.81,0.84, and0.84under equal weights to
0.84,0.91, and0.90under inverse volatility for portfolios of three, four, and five ETFs,
respectively. TLT also performs better in these portfolios than lower-volatility aggregate
bonds, supporting the earlier result that inverse volatility is most effective when the assets
provide different sources of risk without having vastly different volatility levels. This leads
to the main limitation of inverse volatility: it considers each asset separately. The strategy
knows how volatile an asset is, but not whether it moves together with the other assets
in the portfolio. Adding several similar equity ETFs therefore gives each of them its own
allocation and can unintentionally increase the portfolio’s total equity exposure. Likewise,
including a very low-volatility bond ETF can cause fixed income to dominate the portfolio.
Inverse volatility can determine the size of each position, but it cannot determine which
assets should be held together. The remaining strategies address this limitation by using the
full covariance structure, allowing both individual risk and the relationships between assets
to influence the allocation.

7 Conclusions
This paper studied portfolio construction from the perspective of the private investor. All
methods were evaluated under the same setting: 59 liquid US ETFs, no shorting or leverage,
monthly trading with a 5 basis point spread, an 8% volatility target, and data from 2005–
2025, with no parameters tuned to the evaluation period. Under these conditions, simple
portfolios containing as few as three ETFs achieve consistent and decent returns, and the
portfolios we propose outperform the 60/40 benchmark.
The clearest result concerns asset selection. The strongest simple portfolios combine
growth-oriented equities, government bonds, and gold, while the exact allocation appears
less important than including all three. An equally weighted portfolio of SPY, AGG, and
GLD raises the Sharpe ratio from 0.52 for the 60/40 to 0.68 and reduces maximum drawdown
from 30% to 24%, while requiring only one additional asset.
The simple strategies also perform well relative to more sophisticated alternatives. Fixed
weights is difficult to improve upon for a well-chosen set of ETFs, while volatility control
provides a direct and interpretable way to manage risk. The optimization-based strategies
offer different advantages depending on the assets included. Minimum volatility is most
suitable for complementary assets with comparable risk, while risk parity handles large
differences in volatility more naturally. Maximum diversification is better suited to broader
universes with more varied correlation structures. The strongest portfolio we propose uses
maximum diversification across SPY, TLT, GLD, and IBIT, achieving a Sharpe ratio of 0.90.
The risk and alpha models lead to different conclusions. Risk forecasting proved use-
ful, with the factor model producing the strongest covariance estimates, closely followed
by simpler estimators. Return forecasting was demonstrated within the same simple and
transparent framework, using only publicly available price data rather than proprietary in-
formation. In this setting, ridge regression never outperforms the zero forecast out of sample
and improves only as regularization shrinks its predictions toward zero. This does not rule
out richer return models or data sources, but highlights the difficulty of extracting predic-
tive information from price history alone. The simpler momentum signal, when applied to
a restricted universe, allows the Markowitz portfolio to achieve a Sharpe ratio of 0.78 and
the smallest maximum drawdown among the proposed portfolios, although with substan-
tially higher turnover. Markowitz also reduces the need to select individual ETFs in advance
beyond broad asset categories.
These results should be interpreted with some considerations in mind. All performance
is historical, the ETF universe is survivorship biased, and some use of prior knowledge when
defining the universe is unavoidable. A strategy with perfect knowledge of future returns
and yearly rebalancing achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.57, as shown by the regret bound. The
sensitivity analysis shows that the proposed portfolios do not depend on a single market
crisis, although the relative performance of the 60/40 improves when the Financial Crisis
is excluded. Finally, the framework relies entirely on public data, open-source code, and
interpretable parameters, allowing the results to be reproduced and extended.

Throughout this project several million backtests have been run, most of which solve one
convex problem per trading date. We first present code examples that illustrate how the
backtest and our trading strategies are implemented in Python. We then collect some of the
implementation choices that made this scale possible, which together reduced the running
time of the exhaustive search from days to under an hour.
The backtest loop
A backtest is a loop over trading days, and everything inside the loop should be plain vector
and matrix operations. We load the adjusted closing prices once, convert them to a return
matrixR∈R T×n, andfromthatpointontouchonlyNumPyarrays. Labeleddatastructures
such as pandas dataframes are convenient but slow because of their per-operation overhead
which dominates execution time. Restricting pandas to the boundary of the program and
running the loop on NumPy arrays sped up each computed backtest by roughly 2 times.
Listing 1 shows the loop in full.

Both of the following are standard advice in the convex optimization literature, and both
transfer directly to portfolio problems. First, risk is better expressed through a norm than
through a quadratic form. The constraintwTΣw≤σ 2tgt and the constraint∥LTw∥2 ≤σ tgt,
withLthe Cholesky factor ofΣ, describe the same set, but the norm version hands the solver
a second-order cone directly and avoids squaring the conditioning of the problem. When the

where ˜F=F L f andL f is the Cholesky factor ofΣ f, so the risk constraint involves one

Parallelism
A single backtest is serial, since the holdings on daytdepend on those on dayt−1. The
backtests themselves are independent of each other, so we run them in parallel. We use a
pool of twelve worker processes, one per core. Processes rather than threads, since Python’s
global interpreter lock prevents threads from running Python code simultaneously. The price
data is loaded and sliced once before the pool starts, so each worker only receives the arrays
its own backtest needs.
Going faster
The setup above is fast enough for everything in this paper. If it were not, the same
parametrized problems can be compiled. CVXPYgen [Sch+22] generates custom C code
for a fixed problem structure, removing the remaining Python overhead from each solve, and
slots into the loop with an unchanged interface. We did not need it for the results reported
here, but for a larger universe, more frequent trading, or a wider hyperparameter sweep, it
is the natural next step.

Dependence-Aware Risk Assessment and Optimal Design of
AI-Agent Safeguard Systems

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly rely on safeguards to prevent unautho-
rized actions while preserving legitimate service. However, existing risk assessments often
focus on individual safeguard performance while overlooking dependence among safeguard
decisions. This paper develops a dependence-aware framework for risk assessment and
optimal design of multi-component AI safeguard systems, jointly selecting configurations and
operating policies for sequential layered screening and selective verification. We establish
stochastic comparisons of layered-screening error probabilities and invocation costs under
lower-orthant ordering. For selective verification, we characterize exactly which policies have
error probabilities and review costs that respond monotonically to stronger dependence under
supermodular ordering. For a fixed first stage and reviewer, we show that the optimal routing
map is a two-threshold rule in the first-stage likelihood ratio, and that a reviewer enters an
optimal policy only when its cost is below a quality-adjusted combination of the error losses.
We further develop exact optimization algorithms for selecting safeguard subsets, execution
orders, and verification policies under specified risk and cost requirements. An accompanying
open-source software package, Dependence-Aware Policy Optimization Algorithms (DAPOA),
facilitates the practical implementation of the proposed framework. Numerical experiments
show that ignoring dependence can lead to inaccurate risk assessments and infeasible safe-
guard designs, with independence-based selections violating security requirements in six of
eight scenarios. An empirical study using four LLM safeguards on benchmark-derived agent
actions further demonstrates the practical implications of dependence modeling. Under the
principal operating setting, dependence-aware selection lowers held-out total cost by 39.6%
relative to independence-based selection.
Keywords:AI-agent safeguards; copula; optimal design; stochastic comparisons.

1 Introduction
LLM agents increasingly act through tools that retrieve information, modify records, execute code,
and communicate with external services. An agent can propose an action that is syntactically
valid but unauthorized, such as sending information to an unapproved recipient or changing a
record when the user requested only a read. Such proposals can arise from ordinary model errors
or from indirect prompt injection, in which instructions embedded in external content redirect
the agent’s behavior (Greshake et al., 2023; Debenedetti et al., 2024). Developers therefore deploy
safeguards that screen agent inputs, outputs, and actions. Examples include learned content
classifiers such as Llama Guard (Inan et al., 2023), programmable policy enforcement through
NeMo Guardrails (Rebedea et al., 2023), and the prompt-injection and goal-alignment checks
integrated in LlamaFirewall (Chennabasappa et al., 2025). When such components are combined,
system risk depends on each safeguard’s performance, how their errors overlap, and the rule used
to combine their decisions. A safeguard layer operates on demand and has two consequential
failure modes. Asecurity missallows an unauthorized action, whereas afalse blockstops an
authorized action and interrupts service. Current practice uses several approaches to combining
safeguards. Two approaches that arise in practice are layered screening and cascade verification.
Inlayered screening, several safeguards are applied as successive layers to a proposed action,
and the action proceeds only if every layer allows it. Thus, a blocking decision by any safeguard
can stop the action. LlamaFirewall, for example, halts the agent when either its injection
detector or its goal-alignment check raises a flag (Chennabasappa et al., 2025). Under this rule,
an additional layer cannot increase the miss probability, but it can contribute false blocks of
its own. In LlamaFirewall’s AgentDojo evaluation, task utility was 47.0% with the injection
detector alone, 43.1% with the alignment check alone, and 42.7% with both (Chennabasappa
et al., 2025). Forcascade verification(i.e., flag-to-review), an inexpensive safeguard first screens
the proposed action, and flagged actions are escalated to a more costly reviewer for further
verification. Anthropic’s Constitutional Classifiers++ provides a content-safety example: a
lightweight linear probe screens all exchanges and escalates suspicious ones to a second-stage
classifier, whose final decision combines the probe and classifier scores (Cunningham et al., 2026).
A cascade blocks an action only when the screen refers it and the reviewer confirms it, and it
misses an attack whenever either stage does. Its security-miss probability therefore depends on
the screen’s miss probability and on the reviewer’s miss probability conditional on referral.
Currentpracticeoftenevaluatesandtunessafeguardslargelyinisolation, reportingcomponent
detection and false-positive rates, or system-level performance for a single fixed configuration.
System risk under alternative combination rules is then either not assessed or computed, implicitly,
as if component errors were independent. This assumption is difficult to justify in practice as
safeguards in the same system inspect the same context: an injected instruction that appears in
a retrieved document is presented to every layer that reads it. Many safeguards are themselves
language models, or probes on language-model activations, that share pretraining corpora,
architectures, and often a base model family; some are trained on synthetic data generated from
the same policy or constitution. Attacks also vary in intrinsic difficulty, so an obfuscated or
semantically disguised instruction that defeats one detector tends to defeat others, a phenomenon
2
recently documented for the errors of LLMs (Kim et al., 2025). Finally, architecture itself induces
dependence: in a cascade, the reviewer sees only the actions the screen selects, and its decision
may reuse the screen’s score (Cunningham et al., 2026). Ignoring this dependence biases the
assessment of system risk.
Dependence modeling plays a central role in reliability engineering and operations research.
For example, Wu (2024) models the dependence between the age and usage of items under
two-dimensional warranty with copulas and derives preventive maintenance policies from the
resulting failure process. Rahimi et al. (2026) characterize ordering policies for multi-item
inventory systems with autocorrelated and cross-correlated demands and report substantial cost
reductions relative to policies that treat demands as independent. In portfolio credit derivatives,
the dependence among default times drives pricing, and Lei et al. (2023) estimate copula
sensitivities that quantify how changes in the dependence level affect the output. Kim & Weber
(2022) use copula-based models to assess worst-case average value at risk under tail uncertainty,
with an application to cyber risk. Cyber-risk research has also quantified the losses caused by
security failures: Eling & Wirfs (2019) analyze actual cyber-event costs using operational risk
data, and Dacorogna et al. (2023) develop a method for modeling heavy-tailed cyber losses. For
security planning, Paul & Zhang (2021) develop a two-stage stochastic programming model for
allocating prevention, detection, containment, and intelligence resources, and Li et al. (2026)
represent dependent compromises in multi-tenant cloud systems and connect joint risk assessment
to defensive decisions. Where these studies model dependence, it is among inputs, losses, or
compromised assets. In a safeguard system, the relevant dependence is among the decisions of
components that screen the same action.
Safeguard execution is also related to sequential testing, where costly tests are ordered to
determine a system’s state. Rostami et al. (2019) develop exact methods for testing serial systems
under precedence constraints, and Rostami & Rostami (2026) study exact sequencing in sparse
precedence networks. Agnetis et al. (2022) consider testing and search when limited resources
and deadlines restrict the available decisions. In the safeguard setting studied here, the decisions
include the component subset, execution order, reviewer, and final response to each first-stage
pattern. The objective accounts for invocation costs and the consequences of both security misses
and false blocks, using separate joint decision distributions for unauthorized and authorized
actions. Stochastic comparisons provide a complementary way to assess system designs under
structural assumptions on dependence. Navarro & Fernández-Martínez (2021) use copulas
and distortion functions to compare redundancy mechanisms and determine where redundant
components should be placed. Argyris et al. (2025) derive conditions for multidimensional
stochastic dominance between discrete joint distributions with respect to specified classes of
value functions.
Correlated errors among LLMs have been documented (Kim et al., 2025), and recent work
shows that layered LLM defenses can be defeated jointly by staged attacks (McKenzie et al.,
2026). To our knowledge, however, the joint distribution of safeguard decisions has not been
modeled and used to compare alternative combination policies or to design how safeguards are
selected, ordered, and combined. This gap matters because the effect of the joint distribution on
system risk and cost depends on the policy that combines the decisions. In layered screening, the
3
joint outcomes of the selected safeguards determine system errors, while their execution order
determines how often each safeguard is invoked. In a cascade, the referral rule determines when
the reviewer is used and which actions rely on its decision. These considerations raise two related
questions: (i) how can joint safeguard decisions be modeled and translated into policy-specific
measures of risk and cost; and (ii) how can these measures be used to select safeguards, execution
orders, reviewers, and referral rules under operational constraints? To address these questions,
we introduce two policy classes for the design of AI-agent action safeguards, building on layered
screening and cascade verification used in practice.Sequential layered screeningselects and
orders a subset of safeguards, stopping when any selected safeguard returns BLOCK.Selective
verificationselects first-stage safeguards and a reviewer, then assigns each joint first-stage
decision pattern to automatic allowance, automatic blocking, or review. These classes include
fixed layered configurations and flag-to-review cascades as special cases. We assess and optimize
their risk and operating cost using the class-conditional joint distribution of safeguard decisions.
Our main contributions are as follows.
• Dependence-aware assessment and design formulation.We develop a copula-based framework
for modeling the class-conditional joint distribution of safeguard decisions. For sequential
layered screening and selective verification, we derive system-level miss and false-block
probabilities and establish stochastic comparisons that characterize how dependence among
safeguard decisions affects policy risk and invocation costs. For selective verification, we
prove that a policy’s error probabilities respond monotonically to stronger dependence if
and only if its final-decision rule is supermodular or submodular, and its referrals and review
cost do so if and only if its referral rule is.
• Dependence-Aware Policy Optimization Algorithms (DAPOA).We formulate two design
problems: minimizing expected total cost subject to risk requirements, and minimizing the
security-miss probability subject to a cost constraint. For a fixed first stage and reviewer, we
show that the optimal routing map is a two-threshold rule in the first-stage likelihood ratio.
DAPOA uses subset dynamic programming to select safeguards and their execution order
for sequential layered screening, and binary optimization to select safeguards, reviewers,
referral regions, and final decisions for selective verification. The searches return an optimal
feasible policy within the admitted classes for the supplied class-conditional distributions.
The algorithms are implemented in the open-source (DAPOA)1 package.
• Numerical and empirical evaluation.Through numerical experiments, we identify when
dependence materially changes estimated system risk, policy feasibility, and the optimal
design. Using agent-action safeguard data, we evaluate the fitted joint model and compare
the risk and operating costs of layered, cascade, and selective verification policies. The results
show how joint safeguard outcomes can be translated into safeguard selection, execution order,
and review decisions, and delineate the conditions under which accounting for dependence
improves the risk–cost tradeoff.

3 Dependence Effects and Dependence-Aware Policy Optimiza-
tion
This section has three purposes. First, we establish how dependence changes the risk and
operating cost of fixed safeguard architectures when component-level performance is held
constant (Sections 3.1 and 3.2). For layered screening the direction of the change is always
determined; for selective verification we characterize exactly which policies have a determined
direction. Second, we derive the structure of the optimal routing map for a fixed first stage
and reviewer (Section 3.3): it is a two-threshold rule in the first-stage likelihood ratio, and the
reviewer is used only when its cost is below an explicit function of its conditional error rates
and the error losses. Third, we present the optimization algorithms that constitute DAPOA.
These algorithms incorporate copula-based modeling of dependence among safeguards to select
10
components and operating rules that optimize the specified objectives subject to the stated
constraints.

3.3 Dependence-Aware Policy Optimization Algorithms (DAPOA)
This section develops DAPOA, a unified procedure for optimizing safeguard policies. The
algorithms select safeguards, execution orders, reviewers, and routing decisions as applicable to
each policy class. An objective selectorO∈{J,M} determines which design problem is solved:
O =J gives the minimum-total-cost problem Eq.(2.9), andO =M gives the minimum-miss
problem Eq. (2.10).
DAPOA searches the two policy classes separately and compares their best feasible policies
under the selected objective. For sequential layered screening, it first finds the least-cost execution

verification, it evaluates candidate first-stage sets and reviewers and optimizes the allow, block,
or refer assignment of each first-stage decision pattern. Both searches use the fittedq0 and q1 in
Eq. (2.5) to evaluate system errors and operating cost.

5.1 Dataset and experimental implementation
The studied data are constructed from the retail and airline environments ofτ 2-Bench (Barres
et al., 2026), a benchmark for conversational agents that interact with simulated users and
database-backed tools under domain-specific policies. These environments represent customer-
service operations in which a tool call must satisfy both business rules and the user’s request.
Retail cases are generated from six request templates and sandbox tool responses, while airline
cases cover reservations and bookings, baggage, flight changes, passenger names, cancellations,
and certificates. Each unit contains a trusted interaction context together with one authorized
action and one unauthorized variant. The authorized action is the confirmed sandbox call,
whereas the unauthorized action is obtained by the first applicable perturbation of eligibility,
cabin, scope, target, refund destination, recipient, or reason. Labels follow from this construction
and the trusted authorization specification, independently of safeguard outputs.
25
The dataset comprises 1,262 paired units: 555 retail orders and 707 airline cases, giving
2,524 candidate actions. We divide the data into a calibration set and an evaluation set so that
dependence modeling and safeguard-policy selection are carried out without using the actions on
which the resulting policies are subsequently assessed. The calibration set is used to estimate the
marginal error rates and dependence structure and to select the safeguard configuration, whereas
the evaluation set is used to assess the selected policy. The split is made at the user level and
stratified by source environment; all orders or reservations belonging to the same user, together
with both actions in each pair, remain in the same set. Calibration contains 664 authorized and
664 unauthorized actions from 347 users, and evaluation contains 598 actions of each class from
338 different users; see Table 8. This grouping prevents user-specific information from appearing
in both model development and evaluation.

6 Discussion and conclusions
This paper develops a dependence-aware framework for assessing risk and optimizing the
design of multi-component AI safeguard systems. We consider two practical architectures:
sequential layered screening and selective verification. By modeling the dependence among
safeguard decisions, the proposed framework accounts for common security misses and false
blocks that cannot be adequately characterized by individual safeguard performance alone. We
further develop two exact optimization algorithms for selecting safeguard subsets, execution
orders, reviewers, and verification policies under specified risk and cost requirements. To
facilitate practical implementation, we develop DAPOA, an integrated statistical modeling and
optimization package, which is publicly available.
Ourtheoreticalanalysisdemonstratesthatdependencecansubstantiallyaffectsystemriskand
operating costs. For sequential layered screening, the lower-orthant dependence order provides
stochastic comparisons of security misses, false blocks, and safeguard invocation costs. For
selective verification, a policy’s error probabilities respond monotonically to every supermodular
increase in dependence if and only if its final-decision rule is supermodular or submodular.
Flag-to-review cascades with two or more first-stage safeguards are not among them: their
referral probabilities and operating costs cannot increase under supermodular ordering, but the
direction of their error probabilities is undetermined. For a fixed first stage and reviewer, the
optimal routing map is a two-threshold rule in the first-stage likelihood ratio, and a reviewer is
worth invoking only when its cost is below certain threshold. The numerical experiments further
demonstrate the consequences of ignoring dependence. In six of the eight dependence scenarios,
policies selected under the independence model violate the specified security-miss limit when
evaluated under the dependent distribution. These findings show why joint safeguard decisions
must be considered when assessing risk and selecting a safeguard policy.
The empirical study provides further evidence using actual LLM safeguard decisions. It
evaluates the policy choices made using both fitted class distributions on held-out actions. Under
the principal operating setting, the Gaussian-based selection records zero security misses among
598 unauthorized actions, compared with 14 for independence-based selection, while false blocks
increase from nine to 30 among 598 authorized actions. Operating cost increases from0.3779to
0.7300, and evaluation total cost decreases from1.6237to0 .9809, a reduction of39.6%. Across
the 37 operating settings, dependence modeling changes the selected policy in 22 settings and
yields lower evaluation total cost in 17 settings, higher cost in five, and equal cost in 15. Selective
verification provides additional gains over the optimized baseline in some operating settings.
For example, at(ω,sL,sH) = (0.10, 0.5, 2), flexible routing reduces evaluation total cost from
0.7868to0 .6010, a23.6%decrease. These results demonstrate how dependence information and
routing flexibility affect the security service cost tradeoff.
Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the empirical study evaluates actual LLM
safeguard decisions on benchmark-derived agent actions rather than observations from live
33
deployments. The controlled setting provides well-defined authorization labels and complete
safeguard decisions, allowing alternative policies to be evaluated consistently on identical
actions. Further validation under production operating conditions would help establish the
generalizability of these findings. Second, invocation costs are normalized from observed token
usage under specified pricing assumptions, while the consequences of security misses and false
blocks are represented by assumed loss parameters. Obtaining reliable estimates of these costs
is challenging, as they depend on the operating environment and the potentially substantial
downstream consequences of safeguard errors. Nevertheless, the proposed framework can
directly incorporate deployment-specific cost estimates when such information becomes available.
Third, the current framework assumes that the dependence structure remains fixed during
policy optimization. Extending the methodology to accommodate time-varying dependence and
adaptive attacks would be valuable, particularly when safeguard performance changes with the
operating environment.
Overall, our findings highlight the importance of dependence modeling in the risk assessment
and optimal design of AI safeguard systems. By integrating statistical dependence modeling
with risk-constrained optimization, this work provides a foundation for systematic safeguard
design beyond individual component performance. The operational decisions include which
safeguards to include in a policy, their invocation order, and which first-stage outcomes justify
additional review. Further work can extend this formulation to larger candidate pools, changing
operating mixtures, and adaptive safeguard policies.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increas-
ingly used in dynamic financial trading, yet
real-time trading benchmarks mainly evalu-
ate agents by cumulative returns and risk-
adjusted performance. Such outcome metrics
cannot show whether trades are faithfully re-
ported, supported by available evidence, and
consistent with account states. We introduce
AStockArena, a real-time decision-auditing
benchmark for continuous trading in China’s
A-share market. Under shared market condi-
tions and execution constraints, we evaluate 13
open- and closed-source models over 3,198 se-
quential decisions. AStockArena reconstructs
the actions actually posted to accounts by
aligning reports, trade requests, execution re-
sponses, and account states, and audits them
with eight checks spanning execution trace-
ability (O), evidence sufficiency (RK), and fi-
nancial meta-awareness (B), combining record
verification with consensus expert annotation.
Of 1,070 trading points, 72.99% exhibit at
least one audit gap; model-level rates range
from 52.2% to 91.2%, with evidence gaps
most prevalent. Historical trade-filtering re-
play further shows that portfolio outcomes are
sensitive to these flagged trades. Decision-
process auditing thus offers a verifiable com-
plement to return-based evaluation of financial
agents.
1 Introduction
The reasoning, tool-use, and long-horizon
decision-making capabilities of large language
models (LLMs) are moving autonomous agents
from static tasks into dynamic environments
(Cheng and Chin, 2024; Song et al., 2026). Unlike
one-off question-answering tasks such as FinQA
(Chen et al., 2021, 2022), these environments
require agents to continuously acquire informa-
tion, call tools, and act on evolving outcomes.
Finance is a representative and high-stakes case:

Portfolio returns alone cannot establish
whether the underlying trading decisions are
reliable.An agent may report buying one million
shares while submitting an order for only one hun-

formation. Even under positive returns, such dis-

pervised and reviewed, yet they cannot be inferred
from cumulative return or the Sharpe ratio (Kong

model-level joint gap rates range from 52.2% to

vidual and combined filters replace flagged trades

models, separate O, RK, and B filters yield mean
cumulative-return changes of +3.65, +8.86, and
+6.11 percentage points, respectively. These retro-
spective comparisons complement process audit-
ing with historical portfolio sensitivity analysis.

2 Related Work
Continuous Trading Evaluation.Financial
benchmarks cover numerical reasoning (Chen
et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2025), domain knowl-
edge and analysis (Shah et al., 2022; Xie et al.,
2023, 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a;
Son et al., 2025), and multimodal or multilingual
financial understanding (Bhatia et al., 2024; Peng
et al., 2026; Sugiura et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026).
Trading agents use memory, reflection, and tools
to adapt to market information and past experi-
ence (Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b, 2024;
Ma et al., 2026; Shang et al., 2025). Historical and
real-time trading benchmarks provide evolving ac-
count environments and primarily assess portfo-
lio performance (Li et al., 2025a). Related envi-
ronments address reinforcement learning, order-
level market simulation, and economic competi-
tion (Liu et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025c; Nagy et al.,
2025; Zheng et al., 2026; Weiss et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2026b). We complement these benchmarks
and environments with trade-level audits of execu-
tion, evidence, and account facts.
Trading-Process Verification.Related eval-
uations examine forecasting consistency, finan-
cial safety, and deployment-relevant biases (Yang
et al., 2023; Choi et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025b,
2026a). Financial-agent benchmarks use perturba-
tions and closed-loop audits to assess trading re-
liability, strategy consistency, and alignment be-
tween stated and executed actions (Yan et al.,
2025; Qu and Chen, 2026). General agent evalu-
ations use traces, tool feedback, and environment
states to verify action completion and statement–
outcome consistency (Cao et al., 2026; Ye et al.,
2026). Runtime governance constrains trade au-
thorization and execution gating (Tang et al.,
2026), while provenance mechanisms bind claims,
evidence, and execution records (Hu et al., 2026;
Kim et al., 2026). We extend this line of work by
combining execution verification with separate au-
dits of cited facts, key-fact coverage, and account
judgments.

Rationale and Evidence Verification.
Explanation-faithfulness research examines
whether explanations reflect decision-relevant
factors (Jacovi and Goldberg, 2020; Turpin et al.,
2023); financial argument evaluation examines
evidence–claim support (Sun et al., 2025). Fi-
nancial search and retrieval benchmarks assess
access to supporting information (HU et al.,
2026; Tang and Yang, 2025; Zhou et al., 2026;
Zhao et al., 2025), while financial knowledge
and summarization studies assess factual and
numerical reliability (Shah et al., 2025; Cao et al.,
2024). Symbolic references and executable finan-
cial reasoning enable finer-grained verification
(Hennigen et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2026). We
jointly verify decision-time evidence and account
facts, then use historical trade-filtering replay to
assess the portfolio effects of flagged trades.
3 Method
AStockArena reconstructs executed actions, au-
dits them along three dimensions, and converts
consensus labels into trading-point comparisons
and historical replay (Figure 1).
3.1 Reconstructing Executed Actions
Because reports and orders may differ from
booked outcomes, we establish observed actions
from account changes and execution records. In
each decision window, models retrieve materials,
trade or hold, and submit a structured report. The
information pool is frozen at the window’s start;
cash and positions carry forward.
The system saves reports, requests, execution
responses, fill details, and account snapshots be-
fore and after each window (Table E5). With

Execution traceability (O).The sandbox en-
forces trading rules; O checks the records. Report
checks for a valid structured report; Rpt, Call, and
Resp compare net quantities in report descriptions,
trade requests, and successful execution responses
with net position changes, respectively. Rejected
requests are checked against execution responses.
Evidence sufficiency (RK).R checks for sub-
stantive citation fabrication, factual distortion, or
direct contradiction between cited materials and
the rationale or actual trade direction. A title mis-
match alone is not fabrication; insufficient veri-
fication material yields unable to determine. Di-
rection checks follow the specific claim: favorable
news need not contradict a sale, and profit-taking
or risk-adjustment rationales are checked against
their evidence.
K uses an independently established reference
of key facts. Facts are extracted from archived
contemporaneous materials without model re-
ports, trade directions, or subsequent market and
return data. Favorable, unfavorable, and neutral
facts share an importance threshold; the list is
frozen before checking report coverage (Appen-
dices A.1 and A.5).
K checks omissions or partial treatment of facts
affecting the judgment, such as citing revenue
growth for a purchase while overlooking sub-
stantial losses in the same material. Industry
or macroeconomic sources constitute a gap when
their assessment contradicts the trade and review
finds them insufficient for the stock-specific de-
cision. Coverage concerns treatment, not citation
count: weighing counterevidence, giving an alter-
native interpretation supported by the materials, or
explaining why a fact does not warrant changing
the action counts as addressing it.

to-determine gaps to 1, the evidence gap of trade

Financial meta-awareness (B).D compares
reported positions, costs, profit and loss, and avail-
able funds with ledger records for the same secu-
rity, time, and calculation basis. Reports without
a verifiable account assessment are structurally in-
applicable to D.
Temp complements D by checking transitions
between judgments about the same asset. It pairs
adjacent nonzero net trades in the same stock by
the same model, using the earlier judgment to as-
sess whether new information, market or account
changes, trading constraints, or the prior rationale
explain the later decision. Only coherent labels
pass; partially coherent, incoherent, and unable-
to-determine cases are gaps. The first trade is inap-
plicable, and direction reversals alone are not gaps
(Appendix A.5).
3.3 Forming Consistent Audit Labels
Record comparisons and semantic judgments need
a common labeling basis. O is calculated from
structured logs and account snapshots. Two au-
thors with academic backgrounds in finance in-
dependently annotate R, K, D, and Temp under
shared criteria by examining reports, contempo-
raneous materials, and account records. Two ex-
ternal fintech practitioners with securities research
and financial analysis experience participate in
disagreement review to finalize consensus labels
(Appendix A.1). Table 2 reports pre-consensus
raw-label agreement: 95.77% on average, with
mean Cohen’s𝜅of 0.814. Class imbalance and co-
herent/partially coherent boundary disagreements
affect Temp’s𝜅(Appendix B.5).

An audit gap denotes a confirmed defect or in-
ability to verify an applicable check from archived
records. Both lack a fully verified basis, so the
main audit groups them as gaps while retaining
raw labels; structurally inapplicable checks gener-
ate no gap. This shared definition supplies inputs
to comparison and replay, including confirmed-
error-only replay that retains unable-to-determine
trades (Section 4.4).
3.4 Comparing Audit Gaps and Replaying
Trades
Because checks assess windows, trades, account
claims, or trade pairs, we map labels to a common
comparison unit and to removable actions.
Trading-point comparisons.Main-text com-
parisons use windows containing formally anno-
tated trades, termed annotated trading points. The
gap rate is𝐺 𝑖 =100𝑑 𝑖/𝑛𝑖, where𝑑 𝑖 counts an-
notated trading points with at least one applicable
gap in check𝑖;𝑛 𝑖 is the model’s total annotated
trading points. O, RK, B, and the eight-check joint
audit take constituent-gap unions, counting each
point once with this denominator. Appendix B.1
reports rates over each check’s applicable records.
Action-level replay.Actions retain model, win-
dow, and security identities. Any O gap at a deci-
sion point flags all its net trades. R/K/D gaps flag
their corresponding actions; Temp flags the later
trade in the pair, restricted to formal R/K/D ac-
tions. Calibration actions remain in point-level au-
diting (Appendix C.1). RK, B, and combined fil-
ters take action-level unions of R/K, D/Temp, and
selected dimensions, respectively, counting each
action once.
Flagged actions are replaced with No Trade.
Retained actions keep their original timing, target
quantities, and labels, executing subject to updated
cash and sellable holdings. Results appear in Sec-
tion 4.4.
4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
AStockArena constructs a simulated trading envi-
ronment using 10 STAR Market stocks spanning
different industries to control the volume of infor-
mation within each window and support item-by-
item verification. The evaluation covers 82 trad-
ing days from March 2 to June 30, 2026. The
system generates market snapshots each day at
10:30, 11:30, and 14:00 Beijing time, yielding 246
decision windows. The 13 models run indepen-
dently under the same market conditions, produc-
ing 3,198 decisions.
Each model manages an independent simulated
account with initial capital of CNY 1 million and
no positions. Trading follows cash constraints,
T+1, a short-selling prohibition, and a single-stock
position limit of 50% of total account assets, with
order quantities specified in units of 100 shares.
Trading costs comprise a 0.03% commission on
both purchases and sales (minimum CNY 5 per
trade), plus a 0.05% stamp duty on sales.
The benchmark uses a shared ReAct agent ar-
chitecture, allowing at most 30 tool-interaction
steps per window. All models share prices, techni-
cal indicators, news, and company announcements
available up to the current window, with textual
materials restricted by publication time. The stock
pool, data sources, and model configurations are
detailed in Appendix E.
The simulator uses immediate full execution:
orders that pass the constraint checks are filled at
the current market snapshot price, while all other
orders are rejected. Account states evolve through
simulated trades, with position changes handled
on a fixed share-count basis (Appendix C.1).
The main experiment covers R, K, and D for
1,384 active trades and Temp for 1,293 adjacent
trade pairs.

4.3 Decision Auditing
Tables 3 and 4 report results for the audit di-
mensions and eight checks, respectively, both us-
ing decision points containing formally annotated
trades as the denominator. Of these 1,070 trading
points, 781 have a gap in at least one check, yield-

point results and detailed statistics for individual
trades and adjacent trade pairs appear in Appendix
B.
Most active models’ trading-point joint gap
rates fall between 60% and 90%, with RK exceed-
ing O and B: evidence omissions and citation con-
flicts are common. Trading-point joint rates also
exceed all-point rates; including non-trading win-
dows lowers the overall proportion and obscures
defects concentrated in actual trades.
Complete report retention does not guarantee
accurate trade descriptions. Qwen3 235B retains
valid reports at all trading points, yet descriptions
disagree with account postings at 25.0% of them,
showing why report presence and action consis-
tency require separate checks (Table 4).
R and K flag 714 trading points, with 185 trig-
gering both (25.9%). Most points exhibit either
citation problems or key-fact omissions, indicat-
ing that the two checks cannot substitute for each
other. Pooling all 1,070 annotated trading points,
confirmed RK gaps and undetermined-only cases
account for 63.36% and 3.36%, respectively, giv-
ing a merged gap rate of 66.73% (Appendix B.1).
Account assessments also vary: Claude Haiku
4.5 has a D gap rate of 49.0%, while low rates
should be read alongside account-claim coverage
(Appendix B.1). Temp gaps occur at 8.79% of an-
notated trading points, reflecting explanation con-
tinuity. Formal pairs contain 70 partially coherent
and 27 unable-to-determine cases, with no inco-
herent labels (Appendix B.5).

4.6 Continuous Trading by a New Model
To examine the audit framework’s applicability to
new-generation base models, we evaluate GPT-
6 Sol (with medium reasoning) on the same his-
torical snapshots. The model generates 96 active
trades across 246 decision windows; its audit gap
rates and filtering replay results appear in Table 6.
New models may have encountered public mar-
ket information from the evaluation period, limit-
ing interpretation of historical investment perfor-
mance. Auditing archived decision-time materi-
als, account states, and execution records can still
assess evidence use, account understanding, and
trade execution.
No gaps are identified in GPT-6 Sol’s execu-
tion or account-fact checks, but 41 trades have ev-
idence audit gaps. RK filtering increases CR by
33.43 percentage points and also improves risk-
adjusted returns (Table 6).
5 Discussion and Conclusion
Discussion.The new model has no confirmed exe-
cution or account-fact gaps, yet still omits key ev-
idence, suggesting a need for error-specific feed-
back. Reliable execution therefore needs to be ac-
companied by adequate consideration of decision-
relevant evidence. AStockArena links execution
discrepancies, evidence omissions, and account-
assessment errors to trades and source materials
as a basis for model correction and training. Fu-
ture work can test whether revising rationales in
response to this feedback corrects identified prob-
lems and how it affects subsequent trading deci-
sions.
Conclusion.AStockArena audits continuous fi-
nancial trading through structured-record verifica-
tion and consensus semantic annotation, aligning
reports, execution records, and external evidence
with account changes to verify actions, rationales,
and account assessments. Historical trade-filtering
replay examines the relationship between audit
gaps and portfolio performance. Together, these
analyses provide a verifiable basis for evaluating
and improving subsequent models’ decision pro-
cesses and building more reliable trading agents.
8
Limitations
This study has not integrated audit feedback into
a closed-loop trading system or evaluated subse-
quent model decisions following audit interven-
tions. Due to running and annotation costs, each
model follows a single continuous trajectory, and
variability across repeated runs has not been char-
acterized. The evaluation covers ten STAR Market
stocks over a single period. The stock pool is sized
to control the information load within each deci-
sion window and reduce the risk of dispersed at-
tention when analyzing multiple stocks in parallel.
Execution is immediate at snapshot prices, without
partial fills, queuing, or latency. For models added
later, timestamp constraints on tool inputs cannot
rule out prior exposure to relevant historical infor-
mation during training, which may affect trading
rationales and historical investment performance.

Items with matching results in the two indepen-

Key-fact extraction and coverage assessment
are conducted in two stages. First, annotators use
only the target security, decision time, and external
materials saved at that time to identify important
facts that may affect trading judgments, including
information that changes trading premises, pro-
vides important counterevidence, or constrains de-
cision conditions. Model reports and actual trad-
ing directions are not provided during extraction,
reducing the influence of model decisions on fact
selection. Favorable, unfavorable, and neutral in-
formation is selected under the same criteria, and
duplicate reports are merged into a single fact. Af-
ter the fact list is determined and frozen, model
reports and trading directions are introduced to
check whether these facts have been adequately
addressed. The entire process uses only materi-
als available at decision time, without referring to
subsequent market movements or investment re-
turns.
Coverage assessment does not require reports to
restate facts one by one; we examine whether they
understand and address the implications of those
facts for trading decisions. For example, a re-
port may acknowledge unfavorable evidence and
weigh it, offer an alternative interpretation based
on other materials, or explain why a fact is insuf-
ficient to change the trading judgment. For mate-
rials saved only as titles, assessment is limited to
information explicitly stated in the titles, retaining
uncertainty qualifiers such as ’proposed’ and ’ex-
pected.’
Therefore, K measures whether a decision ade-
quately considers relevant information, rather than
whether the model’s investment view agrees with
the annotators’. Industry and macroeconomic
materials are assessed in relation to the target
stock, report arguments, and actual trading ac-
tions; the same fact may support different trading
choices, provided that the report explains its deci-
sion implications and addresses important coun-
terevidence. Company cash-flow and balance-
sheet information constitutes external evidence,
while cash, holdings, and sellable quantities in the
model’s account are verified through D and exe-
cution records, respectively. For disputed items,
reviewers adjudicate using the original materials
and shared judgment criteria, retaining the corre-
sponding rationales.

C.1 Execution and Valuation
The main-experiment simulator uses current snap-
shot prices. Orders that pass the checks execute
for the full requested quantity; rejected orders do
not change cash or positions. Execution is imme-
diate fill or rejection, without pending orders, par-
tial fills, or cancellation of resting orders. Execu-
tion auditing aggregates the share quantities in fill
records and compares them with position changes
in the same window. Slippage costs are analyzed
separately in C.6.
The simulated ledger updates cash and positions
through fill records and updates sellable quantities
across trading days; dividends and stock distribu-
tions are not entered as separate corporate-action
ledger events.
All main-experiment replays start with CNY 1
million and use shared historical prices. Sales pre-
cede purchases within each window, with same-
direction trades ordered by security. Retained ac-
tions keep their original net quantities as targets.
Purchases are sized to available cash, 100-share
lots, and fees; sales are sized to existing positions
and T+1 sellable quantities. Accounts are continu-
ously marked to market, and positions still open at
the end are valued at historical prices. Each trade
incurs a 0.03% commission with a minimum of
CNY 5, plus a 0.05% stamp duty on sales.
All filters remove trades with failed or unable-
to-determine checks. O removes all net trades at a
decision point when any of its four record checks
has a gap; RK takes the union of R/K removal sets;
B removes 164 D gap actions or 93 Temp gap ac-
tions, with 32 overlapping actions and a union of
225. A Temp gap is assigned to the later trade in
the labeled pair. No-account-assessment and first-
trade cases do not independently trigger removal.
Of the 1,293 Temp pairs, 1,257 correspond to
formal R/K/D actions: 1,164 are coherent, 66 par-
23
tially coherent, and 27 unable to determine. The
latter two categories give 93 removals. Another 36
pairs correspond to calibration actions, including
four partially coherent pairs counted only in point-
level audits. Increases and decreases requireΔCR
above +0.01 or below -0.01 percentage points, re-
spectively; all other changes are classified as un-
changed. Main results cover 12 actively trading
models.

E.4 Material Language and Saved Content
News, company announcements, and annotation
instructions are primarily in Chinese, while model
reports and tool interfaces contain English struc-
tured fields. Annotators view the titles, sum-
maries, or full texts saved in the contemporane-
ous archives; citation checks and key-fact assess-
ments retain the corresponding original text for
reference.
The key-fact material packets used in the inde-
pendent second round of annotation primarily con-
tain news titles and summaries within titles, and
key facts are extracted only from content explicitly
stated in these materials. Materials corresponding
to the current formal trades comprise 872 packets
and 68,741 records. Publication times are no later
than the corresponding decision times, and mate-
rial identifiers cited by key facts can be located in
the corresponding packets.

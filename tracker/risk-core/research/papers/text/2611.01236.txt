Abstract
Robust financial-risk inference requires more than mathematical
correctness. Models must accurately apply conditional rules, main-
tain evolving financial states, and make decisions that respect op-
erational constraints. This work introducesRiskReason, a bench-
mark for verifiable financial-risk reasoning that spans diverse risk-
management mechanisms. The benchmark includes Standard tasks,
which assess individual financial mechanisms, and Long-horizon
tasks, which combine multiple mechanisms through extended de-
pendencies. Each task provides reference outcomes, formal specifi-
cations, reasoning annotations, and dependency representations to
support systematic analysis of model performance. Eleven model
configurations are evaluated using strict single-attempt accuracy,
complemented by structural and qualitative failure analyses. The
strongest configurations achieve 80.0–90.2% overall accuracy, with
several maintaining high performance on composed financial sce-
narios. However, analysis of failures reveals incorrect risk decisions
despite accurate intermediate calculations, inconsistent propaga-
tion of financial states, and remediation actions that violate binding
constraints. In contrast, some strict evaluation failures result from
incomplete reporting rather than incorrect financial conclusions.
These findings indicate that aggregate accuracy alone is insufficient
to characterize financial-risk reasoning reliability and motivate eval-
uations that distinguish numerical correctness, state consistency,
constraint satisfaction, and decision validity.

1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have shown impressive results in
financial question answering, numerical reasoning, and solving
problems within specific domains [ 13, 15, 18]. Still, performing
well on these tasks does not guarantee accurate reasoning when
it comes to financial risk management. For example, a bank with
$50M in eligible liquid assets and $45M in stressed net cash outflows
would calculate a liquidity coverage ratio of 111.11%. If $8M of those
assets become ineligible due to a downgrade rule, the ratio drops
to 93.33%. A model might compute the ratio correctly but misjudge
the situation by not updating which assets are eligible. The core
difficulty is not just in doing the math, but in properly applying
financial rules as conditions change.
Financial risk management is a vital area where such reason-
ing matters greatly. Judging credit exposure, liquidity, collateral,
leverage, and regulatory capital often depends on how financial
relationships, contracts, and regulations interact [3, 8]. A change in
any one amount may impact later obligations, resources, or allowed
actions. As a result, strong reasoning here means more than just
calculating risk measures—it demands keeping track of changing
numbers, conditions, constraints, and decisions throughout the
process.
While current financial benchmarks have improved how we
evaluate financial knowledge, numerical reasoning, and complex
problem-solving —including tasks related to risk and structured
verification [13, 15, 17]—they do not fully capture how different
financial risk mechanisms work together. Specifically, knowing a
model’s accuracy on overall tasks does not reveal if it can mon-
itor changes in financial states, follow conditional rules, respect
constraints, or use its calculations to make the right risk decisions.
To explore these abilities, this article presentsRiskReason—a
benchmark designed for evaluating financial risk reasoning in a
arXiv:2611.01236v1  [q-fin.RM]  31 Oct 2026
verifiable way. RiskReason covers a variety of financial-risk mecha-
nisms with two types of tasks. Standard tasks focus on individual or
basic combinations of mechanisms. In contrast, long-horizon tasks
bring together multiple mechanisms in longer sequences, where
each step’s results affect the following financial states and rules.
Every task comes with a verifiable answer, a formal outline, and or-
ganized reasoning notes that show how each step is related. These
features help assess both model performance and what aspects of
financial problems lead to a model’s success or failure.
This work offers three main contributions: (1) a specialized
benchmark for assessing financial-risk reasoning in both individual
and long-term, combined scenarios; (2) structured task outlines that
clearly represent financial values, conditional rules, system changes,
and their interdependencies; and (3) an evaluation of eleven model
setups, looking at overall results, structural links, and common mis-
takes. The results show that strong model performance on complex
financial scenarios can still coincide with major errors in applying
rules, updating states, or meeting constraints. In addition, incom-
plete or unconventional reporting can lead to evaluation failures,
even when the financial decision itself isn’t wrong. Altogether,
these findings highlight the need for more decision-focused assess-
ments of financial reasoning reliability, rather than just aggregate
accuracy.
2 Related Work
2.1 Financial Reasoning Benchmarks
Financial reasoning benchmarks have progressed from numerical
question answering over financial reports and tables, as exempli-
fied by FinQA and TAT-QA [ 2, 19], toward broader evaluations
of financial question answering, domain knowledge, and complex
problem solving [ 9, 13, 15, 18]. Complementary initiatives such
as PIXIU and Open-FinLLMs have developed finance-specialized
models and evaluation resources covering diverse tasks and data
modalities [7, 16].
These developments have substantially expanded financial LLM
evaluation. However, their primary emphasis is on broad financial
capabilities, numerical reasoning, or knowledge-intensive problem
solving. Although several benchmarks include risk-related tasks,
the consistency of financial-risk calculations, state transitions, con-
ditional constraints, and resulting decisions is not generally their
central organizing principle.
2.2 Financial Risk Reasoning
Financial risk management involves more than isolated calcula-
tions. Risk outcomes can depend on contractual and regulatory
conditions, threshold activation, temporal state changes, and the
propagation of shocks across interconnected market, credit, liq-
uidity, collateral, and capital positions [3, 8]. These characteristics
make the consistency between computed quantities, financial states,
and applicable constraints particularly important.
Research on LLMs in finance has examined stock-return pre-
dictability from news [11], finance-specialized models in portfolio
management [12], and speculative behavior among LLM agents in
financial markets [1]. Ghafouri et al. [5] further investigate LLM
risk preferences, ambiguity attitudes, and decision-making under
uncertainty. More directly related to risk evaluation, FinBen in-
cludes risk-management applications such as credit scoring, fraud
detection, and financial distress [15], while Fazlija et al. [4] inves-
tigate LLM reasoning for assigning regulatory risk weights under
the Basel III standardized approach to credit risk.
These studies establish the relevance of LLMs to financial out-
comes and risk-related applications. RiskReason addresses a com-
plementary question: whether models can consistently apply and
coordinate financial-risk mechanisms to derive verifiable outcomes
and decisions, particularly when intermediate changes alter subse-
quent obligations or constraints.
2.3 Structured, Verifiable, and Long-Horizon
Reasoning
Recent benchmarks have introduced more explicit representations
of financial reasoning. FinMathBench constructs multi-formula
financial problems using hierarchical directed acyclic graphs to
control computational dependencies [ 6]. FinChain employs pa-
rameterized symbolic templates and executable reasoning traces
to evaluate final-answer correctness and intermediate reasoning
consistency [17]. V-FiLLM generates verified financial problems
from executable computation trees, controlling dimensions such
as computation depth, expression breadth, and financial concept
complexity [10]. FinIndices further examines structural and tem-
poral reasoning challenges across long financial statements and
multi-period financial quantities [14].
RiskReason complements these approaches by focusing on de-
pendencies among financial-risk mechanisms rather than computa-
tional composition or document length alone. Its tasks incorporate
changing exposures, conditional rules, temporal transitions, and
binding constraints that can determine subsequent financial states
and admissible actions. Formal specifications and dependency an-
notations characterize these relationships, while the evaluation
examines task-level performance and observable financial errors.
The resulting emphasis is on the consistency of the calculation-to-
decision process in financial risk management.
3 The RiskReason Benchmark
RiskReason evaluates whether large language models can derive
verifiable financial-risk outcomes by correctly applying financial re-
lationships, maintaining consistent financial states, and respecting
applicable rules and constraints. As illustrated in Figure 1, the bench-
mark spans diverse financial domains and reasoning requirements,
extending beyond isolated risk calculations to their interactions
and downstream consequences.
The benchmark comprises two complementary task regimes.
Standard tasks assess individual or moderately combined financial-
risk mechanisms, whereas Long-horizon tasks connect multiple
mechanisms through extended dependencies in which intermediate
outcomes influence subsequent financial states and decisions. Both
regimes draw on the same financial and reasoning dimensions
rather than constituting separate domain-specific evaluations.
Each task includes a verifiable reference answer, a formal finan-
cial specification, reasoning annotations, and a machine-readable
dependency representation. These components support systematic
2
evaluation of task outcomes and analysis of the financial relation-
ships associated with model successes and failures.
3.1 Design Principles
RiskReason follows four design principles.Verifiabilityrequires
outcomes that can be checked against specified financial relation-
ships, assumptions, and constraints. When a task intentionally
provides insufficient information for a unique answer, the expected
outcome identifies the missing information or determinable bounds.
Mechanism orientationemphasizes the application of financial
rules, exposure and balance updates, threshold evaluation, and
feasible financial decisions rather than factual recall.Minimal-
sufficient informationrequires models to identify and apply
the appropriate financial procedures without unnecessary solution
cues. Finally,diagnostic structurerecords task-level financial and
computational dependencies to support analysis beyond aggregate
accuracy.
3.2 Task Construction and Ground Truth
Tasks are manually constructed using synthetic financial quantities,
contracts, cash flows, and market or credit shocks, drawing on
established financial-risk management principles [3, 8]. Rather than
reproducing published exercises, scenarios are designed to combine
financial relationships and constraints in controlled settings. This
reduces direct overlap with existing questions, although exposure
to related financial concepts during model pretraining cannot be
excluded.
Reference answers are derived from the governing financial re-
lationships, conventions, and stated constraints. Depending on the
task, the required outputs may include numerical quantities, clas-
sifications, conditional decisions, multi-period financial states, or
structured combinations of these elements. Intermediate quantities
are documented where they determine subsequent calculations or
decisions. For intentionally underdetermined tasks, the reference
instead specifies the valid range or the information required to
obtain a unique solution.
Task formulations and references underwent financial review
for consistency and sufficiency. Identified reference and scoring
issues are documented separately in the evaluation audit rather
than assumed to be resolved. Where available, original and refined
formulations are retained as task annotations, but are not treated
as separate evaluation regimes.
3.3 Reasoning Taxonomy and Complexity
Each task is annotated by financial domain, subdomain, and rel-
evant reasoning characteristics, as illustrated in Figure 1. These
annotations are not mutually exclusive: a scenario may combine
quantitative calculations, temporal dependencies, conditional rules,
and shock propagation. Additional annotations describe operations,
conditional logic, units, and representations.
Reasoning demands are not determined by numerical workload
or prompt length alone. Tasks may involve similar calculations
but differ in the dependencies among intermediate results, the
activation of conditional rules, or the need to maintain financial
states across successive events. RiskReason therefore combines
authored reasoning annotations with graph-derived measures of
task structure. These measures characterize different aspects of
the problems but are not independently validated difficulty scores.
Their associations with observed model performance are examined
in Section 5.
3.4 Formal Representation and Reasoning DAGs
Each task includes a formal specification identifying its inputs, fi-
nancial relationships, conditions, constraints, and required outputs.
Dependencies among these elements are encoded as a directed
acyclic graph (DAG),

The machine-readable graphs distinguish five vertex types: in-
puts, operations, conditions, states, and outputs. Their edges cap-
ture data dependencies between quantities, control dependencies
associated with conditional rules, temporal dependencies between
successive states, and dependencies connecting intermediate results
to requested outputs. Shared intermediate quantities can support
multiple downstream operations, while state changes are repre-
sented at the relevant stages. Finite repeated calculations may be
summarized through series-level vertices rather than expanded into
individual iterations.
This representation captures the intended logical structure of
a financial task, not a model’s observed or prescribed chain of
thought. It enables the characterization of structural properties
such as longest-path depth, branching, merging, conditional op-
erations, and state transitions. Because these properties depend
partly on the granularity of graph construction, they describe
the represented dependency structure rather than an intrinsic,
representation-independent measure of task difficulty.
The formal specifications and graphs are not provided to evalu-
ated models, which respond to the natural-language tasks. Instead,
they support structural analysis and, where model responses expose
relevant intermediate results, examination of observable departures
from the expected financial relationships. They do not reveal unob-
served model reasoning or automatically establish the cause of a
failed response.
3.5 Long-Horizon Financial Risk Reasoning
RiskReason’s Long-horizon regime extends financial-risk reasoning
through the composition of dependent mechanisms rather than
textual length alone (Figure 1). These tasks combine processes such
as market-value shocks, collateral requirements, funding actions,
cash-flow allocation, and capital constraints, so that the outcome of
one stage changes the financial quantities or applicable conditions
of subsequent stages.
Correct solutions must propagate financial states across suc-
cessive events, apply conditional rules at the appropriate stages,
and evaluate downstream decisions against updated balances and
constraints. For example, a market shock may reduce collateral
value, trigger a margin requirement, create a liquidity shortfall, and
require additional borrowing that subsequently affects leverage
compliance. The financial outcome depends on the consistency

4.2 Answer Extraction and Pass@1 Scoring
Each evaluated model–task pair has one recorded response. We
report Pass@1 as strict single-attempt task accuracy:
Pass@1(𝑚)= 1|I𝑚| ∑︁𝑖∈I𝑚 I 𝑔𝑚,𝑖 =pass ,(2)
whereI𝑚 denotes the set of tasks evaluated for model configura-

Numerical quantities are evaluated using the grader’s tolerance and
precision rules, while categorical decisions, financial states, and
structured outputs are assessed against their corresponding targets.
Recorded outcomes include pass, partial, near, fail_close,
and fail. Only pass contributes to strict accuracy. Missing evalua-
tions are excluded from the denominator rather than counted as
failures. Because each task was attempted once per configuration,
the reported metric describes observed single-attempt performance;
repeated-trial reliability and Pass@3 are not estimated.
Strict accuracy measures completion of the full answer contract,
which is not necessarily equivalent to correctness of the princi-
pal financial decision. A response may reach the appropriate risk
conclusion but fail because of an incorrect secondary quantity, an
omitted field, or a representation mismatch. We therefore retain
available field-level grades and reviewed responses for diagnostic
analysis without modifying the recorded strict-pass scores.
4.3 Structural Evaluation
To investigate how task characteristics relate to model performance,
we join task-level grades with the benchmark’s dependency rep-
resentations. The resulting attributes include longest-path depth,
vertex and output counts, branching and merging structures, con-
ditional operations, and state transitions. These attributes describe
the represented dependencies and are not treated as independent
measures of intrinsic task difficulty.
The structural analysis includes all eleven configurations, com-
prising 2,253 recorded responses across 205 tasks. Of these, 1,923
correspond to the 175 Standard tasks. We examine associations
between structural characteristics and strict task success, including
exploratory binomial logistic regressions with model-configuration
and task-source fixed effects and task-clustered standard errors.
The principal explanatory variables are longest-path depth and the
logarithm of one plus the number of graph output vertices, allow-
ing dependency length to be considered alongside answer-output
complexity.
Because graph construction involves representational choices,
including the summarization of some repeated calculations, struc-
tural measurements depend partly on graph granularity. Moreover,
output count is related to the number of opportunities for failure
under strict all-fields-correct grading. The analyses are therefore
interpreted as associations within RiskReason rather than causal
estimates of the effects of reasoning complexity.
4.4 Qualitative Failure Analysis and
Adjudication
We complement aggregate scores with task-level examination of
available model responses, intermediate calculations, final decisions,
structured outputs, and grading notes. The analysis distinguishes
numerical execution errors, incorrect application of financial rules
or thresholds, inconsistent state transitions, infeasible remediation
decisions, and discrepancies arising from answer formatting or
reference specifications.
The qualitative review focuses on observable differences between
model outputs and the expected financial outcomes. Where inter-
mediate calculations are available, task dependencies help identify
how an incorrect quantity or state affects subsequent decisions.
These observations do not establish a model’s unobserved internal
reasoning process.
Review coverage varies across configurations and grading cat-
egories. Selected partial and closely graded responses received
detailed adjudication, whereas not every unsuccessful response
underwent equivalent review. Complete raw responses were avail-
able for Claude but not for the Gemini analyses. Consequently,
failures of answer extraction cannot always be attributed to specific
financial-reasoning errors, and the selected cases are not used to
estimate the prevalence of failure mechanisms.
We preserve the recorded grades for quantitative comparisons
while distinguishing substantive financial mistakes from report-
ing discrepancies and independently identified reference issues.
Cases requiring additional ground-truth or scoring adjudication
are documented separately in the failure analysis and appendix.

5.2 Long-Horizon Financial Reasoning
Long-horizon tasks assess whether models can maintain consis-
tent financial states, apply conditional rules, and satisfy constraints
across successive decisions. Despite strong performance among
leading configurations, substantial differences emerge across mod-
els (Table 1). Notably, Phi-4 and DeepSeek achieve identical overall
accuracy but differ considerably on Long-horizon tasks, indicating
that aggregate performance can conceal weaknesses in composed
financial reasoning.
Reviewed failures reveal several points at which these dependen-
cies break down (Table 2). Models may correctly compute initial
exposures or identify risk triggers but fail to propagate updated
balances, apply rules at the appropriate stage, or verify that a pro-
posed corrective action satisfies all constraints. Such errors can
invalidate financially consequential decisions even when most in-
termediate calculations are correct. Conversely, some non-passes
arise from reporting discrepancies or incomplete outputs rather
than demonstrably incorrect financial reasoning. These observa-
tions suggest that Long-horizon reliability depends on maintaining
consistency from initial calculations through final decisions, rather
than reasoning length alone.
5.3 Agreement and Task Characteristics
Comparable overall rates can conceal different solved tasks. For
example, Phi-4 and DeepSeek each pass 92 of 205 tasks, but overlap
on only 64 passes, each solving 28 tasks that the other does not.
Likewise, Gemini Flash and Pro pass different subsets despite their
relatively close aggregate scores. On the 203-task common set, 13
tasks receive no exact pass from any configuration, while five are
passed by all eleven; these counts reflect strict answer contracts as
well as problem-solving performance.
Graph-derived measures provide a complementary view of task
characteristics. Pooling the eleven configurations on Standard tasks,
exact-pass rates are 621/957 (64.9%) for tasks with one to three
DAG output nodes and 114/242 (47.1%) for tasks with at least ten.
In an exploratory logistic regression with configuration and task-
source controls and task-clustered standard errors, the number of
output nodes is negatively associated with strict-pass odds (OR
≈ 0.48per doubling of1+output nodes). The adjusted longest-path
measure shows no equally clear association ( 𝑝≈ 0.060). Graph
output counts include unscored nodes, and strict scoring creates
more opportunities for mismatch in tasks requesting more fields;
these results describe associations, not causal effects.

6 Failure Analysis
Aggregate accuracy does not distinguish incorrect financial cal-
culations from failures in rule application, state propagation, or
decision-making. Nor does every non-passing response necessarily
indicate incorrect financial reasoning, as strict scoring also penal-
izes incomplete or noncanonical outputs. We therefore examine re-
viewed model responses according to the financial consequences of
their observable discrepancies. Table 2 summarizes representative
cases, which illustrate distinct failure mechanisms and evaluation
boundaries rather than their prevalence across models.
6.1 Financial Measurement and Decision
Consistency
Correctly computing a risk measure does not guarantee that a
model applies it to the appropriate financial decision. Inprobe-036,
Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview derives the posterior and stressed default
probabilities, loss-given-default rate, and expected credit loss, but
evaluates the monitoring threshold using the posterior rather than
the stressed probability. Similarly, in probe-004, GPT-6 Sol and
Luna calculate declining CET1 capital while accepting a claim of
organic capital strengthening. In both cases, the relevant financial
information is available, yet the resulting assessment is inconsistent
with it.
The converse also occurs. In probe-030, Luna reaches the re-
quired escalation decision despite understating exposure at default
following an incorrect collateral conversion. These cases distin-
guish measurement accuracy from decision accuracy: a correct
risk classification can coexist with an incorrect exposure estimate,
just as an accurate calculation can lead to an incorrect financial
conclusion.
6.2 Temporal Consistency and Feasible
Remediation
Sequential financial scenarios require models to update states and
apply rules at the appropriate stages. In risk-057, reviewed Phi-4
and 7B responses mis-time a covenant-triggered interest-spread
adjustment. In long-012, Phi-4 correctly identifies the initial cash-
flow waterfall and trigger but treats a principal collection as an
increase in senior debt rather than a reduction. This incorrect bal-
ance subsequently affects interest allocation, illustrating how a
state-transition error can propagate through later calculations.

tities, but also on whether the resulting action satisfies binding
constraints.
6.3 Numerical Execution and Branch
Consistency
Some responses identify the appropriate procedure but fail to ex-
ecute all of its components correctly. In risk-018, Gemini 3.8
Flash calculates the return without dividend reinvestment correctly
but produces an incorrect result for the reinvestment scenario. In
risk-116, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview derives the daily price-range
components but substitutes a single-session range for the requested
multi-period average true range. These cases illustrate that success-
ful execution of one calculation does not guarantee consistency
across related computational branches.
Claude provides an additional contrast in risk-119: it ob-
tains the long-only portfolio solution but not the unconstrained
minimum-variance weights. In risk-120, it leaves all 16 requested
VAR information criteria uncomputed. In risk-116, its ATR of
50.2714 matches a simple moving average of true ranges, whereas
the reference is approximately 50.81 under Wilder smoothing; the
task requires clarification of the intended method before assigning
that discrepancy wholly to model error.
Such errors differ from failures in financial interpretation: the
model may select the relevant formula or intermediate quantities
but miscalculate, aggregate, or report a required result. Distinguish-
ing these mechanisms is important because an incorrect numerical
result and an incorrect decision based on an otherwise correct
calculation represent different limitations.
6.4 Answer Fidelity and Reference Adjudication
Strict task accuracy also captures discrepancies that do not neces-
sarily establish incorrect financial reasoning. In long-025, Claude
and both Gemini configurations preserve the principal numerical
borrowing-base, cash, and revolver states in the reviewed outputs,
while some event descriptions are missing or differ from the refer-
ence. Claude’s long-028 response likewise preserves the financial
waterfall and capital effects, with non-passes arising from signs and
percentage representations. Other responses contain alternative
signs, scales, labels, or omitted structured fields. These discrepancies
violate the recorded answer contract but should be distinguished
from incorrect financial decisions. Similarly, an unparsed response
establishes unsuccessful extraction under the evaluation procedure,
not a specific reasoning failure without access to the raw response.
Reference validity introduces a separate consideration. For ex-
ample, risk-005 permits alternative median conventions, while
risk-092 contains conflicting leverage definitions. The reference
estimates for risk-120, a vector-autoregression lag-selection task,
also require independent replication. Although none of the eleven
configurations receives a strict pass on that task, disagreement with
an unresolved reference cannot by itself establish a numerical rea-
soning error. Additional numerical checks have flagged risk-117
and risk-125. Claude’s risk-126 response was truncated at its
16,384-token output limit, so that non-pass is a completion-budget
event rather than a localizable financial calculation error.
Taken together, the reviewed cases distinguish three aspects of
financial-risk reliability: computing the required quantities, main-
taining the financial states and constraints on which those quanti-
ties depend, and deriving valid financial decisions. Errors can arise
at any of these stages, while some strict non-passes reflect report-
ing or reference issues instead. Because review coverage differs
across configurations and grading categories, the selected examples
identify observable failure mechanisms without establishing their
relative frequencies.
7 Discussion
Our findings highlight a distinction between numerical competence
and reliable financial-risk reasoning. Several evaluated configu-
rations achieve high accuracy, including on composed scenarios
involving interacting financial mechanisms. Nevertheless, the re-
viewed failures show that models can compute relevant risk quan-
tities while applying rules to incorrect states, propagating inconsis-
tent balances, or proposing actions that violate binding constraints.

This distinction also has implications for benchmark evaluation.
Strict task accuracy is useful for assessing whether models satisfy
complete answer requirements, but it combines errors with different
financial consequences. A response may preserve the correct risk
assessment while omitting a required field, whereas another may
reproduce nearly every intermediate quantity yet reach an incorrect
compliance decision or permissible action. Evaluating financial-
risk reasoning therefore benefits from distinguishing numerical
accuracy, state consistency, constraint satisfaction, decision validity,
and reporting completeness. These dimensions are complementary
and should not be reduced to an unweighted count of correct fields.
The results do not support a simple relationship between reason-
ing length and observed performance. Several higher-performing
configurations maintain comparable accuracy across Standard and
Long-horizon tasks, while others experience substantial declines.
Similarly, differences in output requirements complicate the in-
terpretation of task-level accuracy under strict scoring. These ob-
servations suggest that financial reasoning difficulty cannot be
adequately characterized by task length or dependency structure
alone. The nature of the financial relationships being coordinated,
the conditions governing state transitions, and the validity of down-
stream decisions are also important considerations. Because the
task regimes are not controlled matched pairs, these comparisons
do not establish the causal effect of reasoning-chain length.
Cross-model comparisons further illustrate the limits of aggre-
gate rankings. Configurations with similar accuracy can succeed on
different tasks, while differences in performance cannot be attrib-
uted solely to model architecture, size, or financial specialization.
Inference conditions and response instructions were not fully stan-
dardized, and the evaluation contains only one recorded attempt
per model–task pair. Controlled comparisons and repeated evalua-
tions would be necessary to separate these factors from underlying
reasoning capabilities.
Taken together, RiskReason supports a more decision-oriented
approach to financial LLM evaluation. Beyond checking final nu-
merical answers, evaluations should examine whether intermediate

8 Limitations
Evaluation conditions and reproducibility.Our findings are
based on one recorded response per model–task pair and there-
fore do not establish repeated-trial reliability. Inference providers,
serving conditions, and response instructions were not fully stan-
dardized, while some configurations were scored with corrected
content-based review. Consequently, observed performance differ-
ences cannot be attributed solely to model architecture, scale, or
financial specialization. Controlled evaluations with matched in-
ference conditions and repeated attempts would provide stronger
evidence about these factors.
Scoring and reference validity.Strict task accuracy requires ev-
ery specified output to satisfy the reference rubric, combining sub-
stantive financial errors with omissions and representation differ-
ences. The qualitative review distinguishes these outcomes where
evidence permits, but coverage varies across models and grading
categories, and complete raw Gemini responses were unavailable.
Selected reference values and financial conventions also require
independent adjudication. Claude’s risk-126 was cut off at its
16,384-token response limit, and its run log requests follow-up even
though the grade summary lists no reruns; this result is retained as
recorded pending an appropriately budgeted rerun. We retain the
recorded grades and document these cases rather than applying
unverified corrections. Additionally, the dependency graphs reflect
authored task structure rather than models’ internal reasoning,
and their structural measurements depend partly on representation
granularity.
Benchmark scope and generalizability.RiskReason uses syn-
thetic, self-contained, text-based financial scenarios. It does not
capture the full complexity of operational risk management, includ-
ing incomplete external data, evolving regulations, institutional
controls, and tool-assisted workflows. The evaluation also does not
include a matched human-expert performance baseline. Although
the reviewed cases identify consequential reasoning failures, their
frequencies cannot be inferred from the nonuniformly reviewed
sample. Accordingly, benchmark performance should not be in-
terpreted as evidence of suitability for autonomous financial-risk
decisions.
9 Conclusion
We introduced RiskReason, a benchmark for evaluating verifiable
financial-risk reasoning through the application of financial rela-
tionships, evolving states, and conditional constraints. Our evalua-
tion of eleven model configurations shows that strong task accu-
racy, including on composed financial scenarios, can coexist with
consequential errors in rule application, state propagation, and
remediation decisions. Conversely, some strict evaluation failures
reflect incomplete or noncanonical reporting rather than incor-
rect financial conclusions. These findings highlight the limitations
of aggregate accuracy for characterizing financial-risk reliability
and motivate evaluations that distinguish numerical correctness,
state consistency, constraint satisfaction, and decision validity. By
combining verifiable financial scenarios with structured task rep-
resentations and qualitative failure analysis, RiskReason provides
a framework for examining where financial reasoning succeeds
and where it becomes unreliable. Future work should strengthen
reference validation, standardize evaluation conditions, and extend
assessment to repeated trials and realistic financial-risk workflows.

A Evaluation Records and Reference Checks
Evaluation record.The evaluation contains 175 Standard and
30 Long-horizon tasks, each scored from one recorded response
per model configuration. All eleven configurations have grades for
the 30 Long-horizon tasks; Astra and Sol each lack one Standard
evaluation (risk-119 and risk-117, respectively). As a result, the
intersection of evaluated task IDs contains 203 tasks. An additional
graph record, risk-006, has no evaluation among the eleven config-
urations and is not included in accuracy calculations. We preserve
the original identifiers for traceability, without treating identifier
prefixes as separate task regimes.
Only the grade pass contributes to exact Pass@1. The other
recorded statuses (partial, near, fail_close, and fail) are non-
passes rather than ordinal measures of financial-error severity. Re-
sponse extraction, numerical tolerances, and field-level complete-
ness affect these outcomes. The selected manual reviews help in-
terpret individual cases but do not establish failure-category preva-
lence. Detailed grade distributions, paired-pass tabulations, and
supplementary cases are retained as machine-readable and source
artifacts accompanying the project, rather than repeated as manu-
script tables.
Reference and scoring checks.The audit distinguishes conven-
tional choices from potentially incorrect numerical references.
Financial conventions require clarification for the median in
risk-005, annualized-return conventions in risk-034, the
moving-average signal in risk-063, leverage definitions in
risk-092, and the simple-versus-Wilder average-true-range
interpretation in risk-116. Numerical checks are still required
for the Pearson correlations in risk-117, vector-autoregression
information criteria in risk-120, and Jarque–Bera statistics in
risk-125. The reported yield matching in risk-082 merits a
tolerance and extraction audit. Claude’s risk-126 run reached
the 16,384-token output budget, which establishes a completion
limitation rather than a specific financial-reasoning failure. These
flags do not automatically vindicate divergent model outputs;
grades are retained as recorded pending verified corrections.
B Illustrative Financial Dependency
The Long-horizon task long-001 illustrates how individually
straightforward calculations can lead to a conflicting pair of
financial constraints. Its machine-readable dependency graph
contains 31 vertices and 35 directed edges. The following summary
traces the key quantities; it is not intended as a full rendering of
the graph.
Market shock and collateral.A bank holds $60M of bonds with
modified duration 5.2 and faces a 120-basis-point increase in yield.
The specified duration approximation gives a $3.744M loss, reducing
collateral value to $56.256M. The $48M secured borrowing requires
125% collateral coverage, creating a $3.744M margin call:
Bond loss=60(5.2)(0.012)=3.744,
Margin call=1.25(48)−56.256=3.744.

the bank’s $4M cash balance to $0.256M. It draws $1.244M on an
emergency credit line to restore the $1.5M cash floor. That financing
decision raises total debt to $80.744M, resulting in

Equity = 80.74420 =4.0372.
The cash-floor condition is satisfied, but the debt-to-equity ratio

C.1.9 Fin-o1-8B.Fin-o1-8B is an 8-billion-parameter research

toward corporate finance reasoning, fiscal statement parsing, and
specialized quantitative arithmetic tasks.
C.1.10 DianJin-R1-7B.DianJin-R1-7B is an open-source, 7-
billion-parameter language model published in early 2025
(https://huggingface.co/DianJin/DianJin-R1-7B). Derived from
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, it was trained via a two-stage alignment
pipeline comprising Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) over reasoning
trajectories followed by Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
reinforcement learning to reinforce structural consistency and
factual precision.
C.1.11 Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct.Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is an instruction-
tuned, 7-billion-parameter foundational model created by Alibaba

7B-Instruct). Pre-trained on an 18-trillion token multilingual corpus
and incorporating architectural mechanisms such as Grouped
Query Attention (GQA) and SwiGLU activations, it delivers com-
petitive general-purpose baseline performance across multilingual
translation, coding, and mathematical reasoning.

A bank has $100M of total assets, $10M of equity, and $3M of
cash. It pledges a $50M bond portfolio against $38M of repo
borrowing. The bonds have modified duration 4.0. Market

After the shock, required collateral market value equals repo
borrowing divided by (1−haircut). Any collateral shortfall is
met with a cash margin payment. The bank must maintain
at least $1M of cash; if the margin payment would push cash
below that level, it borrows unsecured funds just sufficient
to restore the $1M minimum.
The bond loss reduces both assets and equity. New unsecured
borrowing adds cash and liabilities but does not change
equity. The maximum assets/equity leverage ratio is 12×.
